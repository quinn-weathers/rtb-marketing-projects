Top of mind — the week09 · 18

Nothing changed in the law you carry under this week. What changed is how much of the machinery around it became visible. An FBI director confirmed a background-check monitoring programme on the record, a federal rulemaking that touches every NFA applicant moved inside three weeks of closing, and the Supreme Court’s AR-15 case held the top of this board for all five measured readings without a single docket entry.

The week’s honest headline is continuity, not upheaval. The three federal bills this board tracks — the Hearing Protection Act (H.R. 404), the SHORT Act (H.R. 2395) and the Freedom from Unfair Gun Taxes Act (S. 1169) — are all still sitting exactly where they were in early 2025, at “Introduced,” referred and untouched. That is a verified fact this board could not state for the previous twenty-three runs because congress.gov was unreachable; it became reachable this morning. Members asking “did the law change?” can be told plainly: not federally, not this week. The movement is in three other places. First, FBI Director Kash Patel confirmed the existence of NICS Monitoring at a September 15 Senate Judiciary oversight hearing — confirming that a programme exists, and nothing beyond that. Second, an ATF proposed rule on fingerprint and photograph requirements for firearms applications closes for public comment on Monday, October 5 — seventeen days out, 375 comments filed, and the single most concretely actionable date on this board. Third, the Justice Department filed its third suit against a locality over carry-permit costs, this time against San Jose over roughly $1,500 in fees, and a Maryland federal judge granted a synagogue and its rabbi a narrow injunction against a place-of-worship carry ban. Both of those landed this morning and neither has a heat reading yet.

FBI Director Kash Patel confirmed the existence of “NICS Monitoring” in a televised Senate Judiciary oversight hearing

At the Senate Judiciary Committee’s Oversight of the Federal Bureau of Investigation hearing on September 15, 9:00 a.m., Hart Senate Office Building Room 216 — the hearing appears on the committee’s own published calendar — Sen. Ted Cruz (R–TX) asked Director Patel whether a programme known as NICS Monitoring had been used to track firearm purchases by Americans who were legally eligible to buy but suspected of a crime. Patel answered: “Yes.” Read precisely what that establishes and what it does not. It confirms a programme exists. It does not establish how many people were monitored, whether any warrant was required, how many were ever charged, what data was retained, or whether the programme operates today. A separate April 23, 2025 ATF memorandum narrowed the approval process — Special Agent in Charge sign-off, Deputy Assistant Director concurrence, a written factual basis, and 30/60/90/180-day terms renewable with deputy-director approval — but it restricted the programme rather than ending it, and added no warrant requirement. The underlying FOIA records reporting describes a system that alerted investigators when an already-flagged person later attempted a purchase after receiving a NICS proceed.

Washington’s permit-to-purchase confirmed at $126, with certified training required for concealed-pistol-licence renewals as well as new applications

Washington State Patrol confirmed the structure under RCW 9.41.1132: a $126 total — $90 permit plus a $36 fingerprint background check — effective May 1, 2027. Training is not included in the fee and must come from private instructors working under WSP-certified curricula across nine required subjects. The application portal, GetapermitWA.org, launched September 1, 2026. The detail most likely to catch existing licence-holders off guard: from May 1, 2027, certified training is required for renewals of existing Concealed Pistol Licences, not just for new applicants. A source article asserted litigation was “almost certain”; that is a forecast, not a fact, and is excluded here.

On the ballot→ Ballot · T−46 Verified at source 09/18

Two firearm measures are certified for November 3: Massachusetts Question 9 and the Arkansas Right to Keep and Bear Arms Amendment

This board has carried both measures for weeks without ever reaching a primary page. Both were confirmed at source this morning, and Massachusetts now has a ballot number. Massachusetts Question 9 is a veto referendum — a citizen-originated measure on House Bill 4885 (2024). A “yes” vote upholds H.4885, which established an electronic firearm-ownership registration system, prohibited assault-style firearms as defined in the law, required safety certificates for firearm permits, mandated serial numbers, allowed school administrators and licensed healthcare providers to petition for Extreme Risk Protection Orders, and permitted courts to order firearm surrender when issuing Harassment Prevention Orders. A “no” vote repeals it. Note the inversion carefully — on a veto referendum, “yes” keeps the law. Arkansas is a legislatively referred constitutional amendment: a “yes” provides a right to possess and use ammunition, firearm accessories and firearm components “without limitation,” and declares the right to keep and bear arms “a natural, fundamental, and individual right that shall not be infringed.” Oregon IP 28 remains confirmed absent from the certified list.

Sentenced↓ Michigan Wk avg79↓ Peak 87 · hot 4 of 5

Michigan: a lawful defensive shooting and an unlawful carry, in the same act, by the same 19-year-old

In Wayne County, Michigan, Martinez Long, 19, fatally shot Cameron Watkins at Fairlane Town Center in Dearborn on July 3, 2026, after Watkins shot and killed Keonte Seaborn and wounded another person. Prosecutors declined to charge Long over the shooting itself, finding lawful self-defense or defense of others. Long was nonetheless charged with carrying a concealed weapon without a licence, and on September 8 was sentenced to two years’ probation, cognitive behavioural therapy, a court-offered handgun-safety course, and no firearms or ammunition for the term. He had no prior record. The legal pivot is the age gap in Michigan law: MCL 28.425b sets the concealed-pistol-licence minimum age at 21, while MCL 28.422 allows an 18-year-old to acquire a handgun by private transfer. An 18-to-20-year-old Michigan resident can lawfully own a handgun and cannot lawfully carry it concealed. This item carried the highest single reading of any state story in the window and cooled steadily as the news cycle moved on — not because anything was contradicted.

Third suit filed↑↑ DOJ litigation Filed 09/16 · reported 09/18

The Justice Department sued San Jose over carry-permit fees — its third suit against a locality over permitting, with two more threatened

On Wednesday, September 16, the DOJ Civil Rights Division filed United States v. San Jose, challenging carry-permit charges reported at roughly $1,500–$1,600 in total. The complaint argues the fees are so onerous that they infringe residents’ rights: “San Jose is a national outlier when it comes to burdening the exercise of fundamental rights,” and the fees are “abusive and violate the Second Amendment.” This is the Department’s third carry-permitting suit, following actions reaching from Los Angeles to the Virgin Islands, and it has openly signalled further action in Philadelphia and Minnesota. Note the doctrinal care in the filing: DOJ opened by conceding that shall-issue permitting is permissible under Bruen, and argued only that this scheme crosses from “narrow, objective, and definite standards” into an unconstitutional burden. Nothing has been decided; a complaint is an allegation. Earlier DOJ suits against Virginia and California over other gun restrictions are part of the same pattern and are covered from the opposite direction by gun-violence-prevention press.

Final judgment→ D. Del. Wk avg70→ Only item to re-enter the board

Rigby v. Jennings — Delaware’s possession and self-manufacture bans permanently enjoined; the distribution ban and the 3D-print-file ban both survived

Judge Maryellen Noreika (D. Del., appointed by President Trump) entered final judgment on September 11 in a case filed October 27, 2021 by John Rigby, Alan Knight and the Firearms Policy Coalition against Delaware Attorney General Kathy Jennings, challenging H.B. 125 (2021), 11 Del. C. §§ 1459A and 1463. Enjoined facially and as applied: the possession ban and the self-manufacture/assembly ban on untraceable firearms and unserialised unfinished frames and receivers. Upheld: the commercial distribution ban, as a presumptively lawful condition on sale under Heller; and the CAD/3D-print instruction ban against a First Amendment challenge, following the Third Circuit’s Defense Distributed v. AG NJ (2026). The court held frames and receivers are “Arms” and recognised an ancillary right to acquire and repair; Delaware’s historical analogues had already been rejected by the Third Circuit in ANJRPC (2026). The takings claim was not reached, and the opinion contemplates that a serialisation requirement might survive where an outright ban does not. Appeal to the Third Circuit is expected; no timeline. This was the only item in the window to leave the board and come back — 72 on Monday, 65 on Tuesday, absent Wednesday, 74 on Thursday — and the re-entry was caused by a source outage ending, not by anything happening in the case.

YouTube’s Branded Content Policy collides with FTC disclosure duties for firearms creators — and the label applies to the whole video

YouTube’s Branded Content Policy, published in early September 2026, replaces the prior paid-product-placement language. Creators must disclose in Studio when a video was influenced by payment, sponsorship or free product; YouTube then applies a branded-content label and may apply it automatically. Branded content “featuring weapons or ammunition” is prohibited — and the prohibition is applied to the entire video rather than to the sponsored segment. The conflict is with the FTC endorsement guides, which require clear and conspicuous disclosure of a material connection, including a test-and-evaluation firearm provided at no cost. On the face of the policy, reviews of firearms the creator purchased outright remain permitted. No enforcement action against any named channel has been reported; the worst-case interpretations circulating among creators are readings, not outcomes, and are excluded here.

NFA processing has collapsed from 73 days to roughly 18 — while volume nearly tripled

NSSF’s FOIA-derived figures, read at source: the volume-weighted average across the four highest-volume forms fell from 73 days in FY2021 to roughly 18 days; Form 4 fell from about 150 days to about 18; the median individual eForm 4 is six days.
