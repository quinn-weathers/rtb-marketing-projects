Top of mind10 · 23

Bend held the week’s attention, and the NFA question kept moving to the states. Underneath, the courts were busy: the Supreme Court left Maine’s waiting period in place, a coalition sued New Mexico over its own, and by Thursday two courts had called non-firearms “arms.”

What stayed hot. The Bend, Oregon school shooting led all five readings (week average 93, hot 5/5, peak 98), cooling slowly from 98 to 89 as coverage moved from the attack itself to accountability, prevention and recovery. The state NFA “mirror law” question held flat at the top of the hot tier all week (87, 5/5, peak 88) as Mississippi, Florida, Idaho, Montana, Maryland and Alaska each gave a different answer. Waiting periods became a cluster and rose (75, 4/5, peak 80) after the Supreme Court declined Maine’s case on Monday and gun groups sued New Mexico on Tuesday. Massachusetts Question 9 climbed to its high of the cycle (74, 5/5, peak 77), and the state’s top court upheld its under-21 carry-license bar.

What faded. The federal side of the NFA story cooled every day (83 to 73) as the action moved to state capitols. DOJ’s Pennsauken permit-delay probe fell from 74 to 55 with no township response, though DOJ opened two more such investigations in Connecticut on Thursday. The Third Circuit felon-ban ruling slid from 76 to 62 and left the board. Last Friday’s one-day items, from Virginia’s under-21 suit to Denver nonresident carry, did not come back.

Arrived Thursday, not yet ranked. A federal judge struck down New York’s body-armor purchase ban (84), Smith & Wesson bought Savage Arms (80), Michigan prosecutors declined to charge a Dearborn carry-license holder (72), and the Fifth Circuit struck the federal switchblade law (66). Each has one reading; they carry into next week.

Why it matters for RTB. Three member-ready themes this week, none of which touch Bend: what the law says where you live (NFA items and waiting periods both now differ state by state), criminal and civil are separate tracks (Dearborn, Valdosta and Portsmouth all show a prosecutor’s review is its own step), and where the law is still unsettled (body armor is protected in one New York ruling, stayed for now). Bend stays out of all marketing.

DOJ opened two more permit-delay investigations, in Connecticut, and widened its San Jose suit to the city’s gun-owner insurance mandate

The Pennsauken, N.J. investigation announced 14 October cooled from 74 to 55 over the week as no township response surfaced. On Thursday the Civil Rights Division opened a new investigation into the Middletown and Waterbury, Connecticut police departments’ permitting systems, citing Bruen’s warning against “lengthy wait times” and saying applicants may face a potentially years-long appeal process. Separately, TTAG reports DOJ amended its suit against San Jose to add the city’s 2022 gun-owner liability-insurance requirement and $25 annual fee alongside carry-permit costs (the amended complaint itself was not read). Where things stand: these are investigations and allegations, not findings; no municipality’s response has been reported.

Moved ▶ DOJ · under-21 handgun sales Wk avg59↓ Senate letter 10/16 · no court ruling

Twenty senators ask DOJ to explain why it stopped enforcing the federal under-21 handgun-sale ban; the department’s position stands

DOJ’s Office of Legal Counsel concluded in September that the federal ban on licensed dealers selling handguns to 18-to-20-year-olds is unconstitutional, and DOJ said it will not enforce it. A 174-member House letter on 8 October asked DOJ to reverse course; this week 20 senators sent their own letter demanding an explanation, framing the change as raising school-shooting risk. TTAG framed the same letter as an attack on young adults’ rights. Where things stand: no ruling on DOJ’s request to moot Reese v. ATF; no ATF dealer guidance in the Federal Register. State minimum-age laws are unaffected, and Massachusetts’ top court upheld one this week (see 03B).

Moved ▶ States · NFA mirror laws Wk avg87→ FPC letters to 15 AGs · hunting-season questions

State by state, the answer on suppressors and short-barreled guns is still “it depends”

With ATF not enforcing NFA registration, states whose laws tie legality to federal paperwork are answering differently. AmmoLand’s 10/20 round-up: Idaho’s attorney general recognizes the change; Mississippi’s AG opinion treats the federal form as voluntary; a Florida state attorney’s memo covers six counties; Montana officials are drafting legislation to legalize the items; Maryland State Police say nothing changes. Alaska’s acting attorney general asked the next Legislature to fix the state’s own suppressor and short-barreled-rifle felony law. The Reload singles out Virginia’s short-barreled-shotgun law as the clearest remaining source of uncertainty. Hunting season brought local explainers: Missouri shops report same-day suppressor sales on a standard Form 4473, and Pennsylvania outlets walked readers through what changes there. ATF’s director, in an American Rifleman interview, said state laws “must be understood” before traveling with a suppressor.

New ▲ Massachusetts · Commonwealth v. Thomson Wk avg71↓ SJC 5–2 · decided 10/20

Massachusetts’ highest court, 5–2, upholds the bar on licenses to carry handguns and large-capacity firearms for people under 21

In the case of a 20-year-old convicted of carrying a loaded handgun, the Supreme Judicial Court held that restricting possession by people “deemed too young to handle them safely outside of supervised settings” fits the nation’s regulatory tradition (Justice Wolohojian for the majority, per CommonWealth Beacon). The court looked to how the state’s early militia regulated youths (MassLive). AmmoLand frames the decision as deepening a split with federal circuits that have struck down under-21 limits. The same age rule is part of the law on the ballot as Question 9. The opinion itself was not read.

NFA guidance without a rule, waiting periods, and the felon-ban docket
Watch ● ATF · NFA non-enforcement Wk avg78↓ Day 14 · 0 Federal Register documents · Brown conference held 10/22

Two weeks into NFA non-enforcement there is still no rule, and the practical questions moved to the gun counter

ATF’s 9 October guidance (no federal enforcement of registration, prior approval or tax-stamp requirements for suppressors, short-barreled rifles and shotguns, and “any other weapons”) remains guidance: the Federal Register shows no ATF documents since 1 October. In an American Rifleman interview, ATF’s director addressed traveling across state lines with a suppressor. Retail moved fast: GrabAGun issued a statement, and Missouri shops are completing same-day suppressor sales on a standard Form 4473 (OzarksFirst). TTAG fielded the solvent-trap question. The legal gap remains: in September the Third Circuit (U.S. v. Berger) held silencer registration does not “infringe” (Duke analysis). The Brown v. ATF status conference on how far relief reaches was set for 10/22 in the Eastern District of Missouri; we found no report of its outcome in the sources we could reach. Background checks, state laws and some travel rules still apply.

The Supreme Court left Maine’s 72-hour wait in place; a day later, a nine-plaintiff coalition sued over New Mexico’s seven-day wait

The 19 October order list denied review in Beckwith v. Frey (26-258), leaving in place the First Circuit’s April ruling that Maine’s law is likely constitutional; there was no written dissent. Maine enacted the wait after the 2023 Lewiston shooting (Press Herald). On 20 October NSSF, the Second Amendment Foundation, the NRA, New Mexico groups, two retailers and a private citizen filed NSSF v. Torrez, arguing the state keeps enforcing its wait against everyone except the plaintiffs who won Ortega v. Grisham in the Tenth Circuit; Courthouse News describes the state as waiving the wait only for “a select duo.” Court and case number were not found. AmmoLand notes the circuit split stays unresolved. Brand note: one plaintiff organization is named “Right to Bear Arms”; it is not affiliated with RTB.

Moved ▶ Bend, Oregon · aftermath Wk avg93↓ Day 8 · schools reopened 10/21 · no charging decision

Bend, week one: the focus moved from what happened to accountability, prevention and recovery

On 15 October a 16-year-old killed three children and a teacher at Silver Rail Elementary School before an armed parent stopped him. Across the week: police finished evidence collection and confirmed four staff and five children were in the classroom (KTVZ, 10/19); one victim’s family retained three law firms to examine school protocols and how the attacker obtained the gun (AP via U.S. News, 10/19); police confirmed the shotgun was bought legally, and experts told OPB that Oregon law may make it hard to hold anyone else criminally responsible; the FBI is examining the attacker’s online radicalization (OregonLive, 10/21); classes resumed 10/21 and the injured teacher spoke publicly. How each side framed it: Brady called for secure storage; gun-rights writers, including Reason’s Volokh Conspiracy, pointed to the armed parent and the district’s staff-carry ban. No charging decision has been reported. As in the dailies, we do not name the victims, the attacker, the parent or the attacker’s family.
