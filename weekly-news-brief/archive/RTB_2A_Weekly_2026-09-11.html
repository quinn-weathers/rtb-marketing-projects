Congress came back this week and did nothing measurable — and federal firearm law still moved four times in a single Thursday. Every one of those four moves came from a court, the Solicitor General, a state attorney general, or ATF itself. For members the practical frame is unchanged and is the whole RTB position: what the law says today, what is pending, and what is actually on a calendar.

One story held the top of this board on every day it ran. The consolidated AR-15 cases — Viramontes v. Cook County (No. 25-238) and Grant v. Higgins (No. 25-566) — averaged 92 across three observed days with a 94 peak, the highest weekly average this board has recorded. The reason is a verification upgrade rather than a new fact: for the first time, supremecourt.gov was reachable and the Court’s own December argument calendar was read at source. The consolidation, the December 2 date and the one-hour allotment are now Court-issued facts, not reported ones. Against that hour, Solicitor General D. John Sauer has moved for ten minutes of divided argument — a motion that is pending, not granted. A decision on the merits is not expected before roughly June 2027, and that sentence belongs in the first paragraph of anything RTB publishes on it.

The week’s real structural development is that the whole December case has narrowed to four words. There are now four incompatible readings of “dangerous and unusual” on file. DOJ measures “common use” by how heavily an arm has historically been regulated. The Cuccinelli–Mayfield militia-clause brief says a government may not ban an arm into unusualness and then cite the rarity it created. Attorney Alan Beck, writing for the Hawaii Rifle Association, argues the phrase never described a weapon at all — it described conduct. And Missouri turns it into arithmetic: 6.65 million registered suppressors cannot be unusual. That last one arrived as a lawsuit. On Thursday, Missouri Attorney General Catherine Hanaway sued ATF and DOJ in the Eastern District of Missouri — Marshak v. ATF — opening a third circuit on the theory that once § 70436 of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act set the NFA transfer tax to $0, the registry lost its taxing-power footing under Sonzinsky (1937).

And the single most useful item of the week was the quietest one. An ATF open letter dated August 28 and posted September 8 reverses part of the agency’s 2008 position: a suppressor already registered in the NFRTR may be repaired, or have its caliber changed, without a new making, marking or registration. It is guidance, not a rule, it carries no stated effective date, and it does not reach devices transferred under the injunctions without registration. But it lands on a cohort that barely existed a year ago — August NFA-related checks ran roughly three times year-ago levels while processing times fell — and it answers the first question those owners will actually hit. That pairing is the strongest content asset RTB has available this month.

Four words are now carrying the whole December case — and there are four incompatible readings of them on file

Fresh entrant, and the most intellectually consequential development of the week. Alan Beck — a San Diego civil-rights attorney who filed for the Hawaii Rifle Association on August 31 — writes in The Reload that “every appellate court to rule on the definition of the term dangerous and unusual weapon has gotten its history wrong.” His argument is that the phrase Heller borrowed was conjunctive and behavioural: all weapons were “dangerous,” and “unusual” described a manner of carrying arms apt to terrify — so a weapon becomes dangerous-and-unusual only through use, and no arm or class of arms can be dangerous and unusual as such. He traces it through Blackstone and the 1328 Statute of Northampton, Hawkins (1716), Wilson (1804), Dunlap (1815) and Russell (1826), plus O’Neill (Ala. 1849), Langford (N.C. 1824), English (Tex. 1871), Simpson (Tenn. 1833) and State v. Lanier (N.C. 1874), where a man riding through a courthouse unarmed could commit affray. Set that against the Ninth Circuit’s reasoning in United States v. Henry (2012), which upheld machine-gun restrictions precisely by calling them dangerous and unusual. It is now one of four readings before the Court: DOJ’s historical-regulation test; the Cuccinelli–Mayfield militia-clause brief, which says a government may not “ban an arm into unusualness and then cite the unusualness it created”; Beck’s conduct reading; and Missouri’s arithmetic. One outlet — but of a particular kind: the filing attorney writing under his own byline about his own brief, with the brief PDF hosted at supremecourt.gov. The brief itself was not read end to end.

RTB angleThis is the week’s publishable item, and the piece is not about any one brief — it is about the fact that four words are carrying the whole case. RTB can write the explainer nobody in the peer set has written: the December argument will turn on what “dangerous and unusual” means, and there are at least four serious answers on the table. That single act of restraint is the entire brand position: a member should leave RTB’s page understanding that the law here is genuinely unsettled — which is exactly why counsel matters — and should leave without being sold a prediction. Three hard limits. A brief is a litigant’s argument, not a holding: “argues,” “asks the Court,” every time. Beck is counsel in the case, so his analysis is advocacy by a party’s lawyer; attribute it that way, which is more interesting rather than less. And publish nothing suggesting machine-gun, suppressor or NFA rules have changed or are about to — forum readers have already run this reasoning to § 922(o) and the Hughes Amendment, and repeating that leap would be the most expensive sentence RTB could print this quarter.

Twenty-seven states told the Court a constitutional right cannot depend on which state you are standing in — and the brief is the week’s richest quarry of attributable facts

Filed September 4 in support of the petitioners and read at source. Tennessee and Kansas lead a coalition of Alabama, Alaska, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, New Hampshire, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, South Dakota, Texas, Utah, West Virginia and Wyoming. The argument is common-use plus federalism: the states accept broad authority over policy inside their borders but deny that federalism lets a state erase an enumerated right. Their framing sentence — a fundamental right should not be “subject to legislative caprice and judicial speculation about what the right-holder really ‘needs’” — is the most quotable line on this board. The evidentiary spine: more than 32 million modern sporting rifles manufactured in or imported since 1990; cited research placing 24–28 million AR-15s and comparable rifles in circulation; a 2022 Washington Post-Ipsos survey in which 91% of responding AR-15 owners named protection as a reason; and a Justice Department report that 94.5% of local police departments authorise semi-automatic rifles for on-duty officers. On crime data the brief cites FBI figures showing rifles of every action type identified in 378 homicides over the one-year period cited, against 3,817 for handguns, 1,229 for knives and 506 for hands, fists or feet — and it expressly acknowledges that the FBI separately reports cases where firearm type is unidentified and does not claim rifles are never misused. The item cooled three points across the week as it was absorbed into the broader “dangerous and unusual” argument. One sourcing wrinkle: AmmoLand renders the Grant docket number as 25-556 in one piece and 25-566 elsewhere; this board uses 25-566.

Missouri sued ATF to end NFA registration for the four categories Congress just untaxed — a third circuit, and the first state attorney general to bring the theory

The week’s fastest-moving structural story, and it arrived on Thursday. Missouri Attorney General Catherine Hanaway sued ATF and the Justice Department in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Missouri, joined by co-plaintiff Dave Marshak of Festus. Marshak v. ATF asks the court to end NFA registration for suppressors, short-barreled rifles, short-barreled shotguns and “any other weapons.” The lead argument is structural rather than constitutional: Sonzinsky v. United States (1937) upheld the National Firearms Act as a revenue measure — registration, fingerprints, photographs and ATF approval existed to collect a tax — and § 70436 of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act set that tax to $0 for those four categories effective January 1, 2026 (machine guns and destructive devices remain at $200). Missouri’s claim is that once the revenue vanished, so did the enumerated power supporting a national database backed by biometric packets and up to ten years and $250,000 in criminal exposure. That tracks Judge James Wesley Hendrix (appointed by Donald Trump) and his August 5 reasoning in Silencer Shop Foundation v. ATF in the Northern District of Texas. A second, Second Amendment count turns ATF’s own registration figures against it: roughly 6.65 million registered suppressors and 1.23 million registered SBRs, which the state says makes “dangerous and unusual” unavailable as a characterisation — the fourth reading of that phrase now in play. Relief sought is declaratory judgment plus a permanent injunction. The suit was filed, not decided; the Texas injunction remains limited in scope and ATF has still issued no nationwide implementation guidance.

RTB angleThe genuinely useful thing here is the map, not the merits — and RTB is well placed to draw it because nobody else has. There are now challenges to the post-tax NFA registry in at least three federal districts across three circuits, brought by industry groups, gun-rights organisations, more than a dozen states, and now a state attorney general with a private co-plaintiff. A member who owns a suppressor has no idea whether any of that applies to them, and the honest answer — almost certainly not yet, and here is how you would know — is exactly the clarity a legal-protection brand should be famous for. Cite § 70436 and the $0-versus-$200 split plainly; it is the cleanest explanation of why suppressors moved and machine guns did not, and it answers a question members are actually asking. Do not characterise the Attorney General or quote the political framing in her statement — the legal theory is the story and it survives on its own.

Two men are out of federal prison in NFA cases — and the precedent that put one of them there is still on the books

The week’s largest social and forum story, and its steepest fall: 84 on Monday, 72 on Tuesday, off the leaderboard by Thursday with nothing new attached and nothing contradicted. President Trump issued full and unconditional pardons to Patrick “Tate” Adamiak and George Peterson. Adamiak: a former Navy petty officer first class whose Virginia Beach home was raided in April 2022 after a paid confidential informant facing his own charges told ATF he had a grenade launcher. Agents found demilled parts kits, inert and DEWAT launchers, a non-firing replica and unfinished sheet-metal “flats.” A jury convicted on five counts; consecutive stacking produced 20 years. The Fourth Circuit found double jeopardy on two counts charging the same item under both 26 U.S.C. § 5861(d) and 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), vacated one and remanded; the Supreme Court denied certiorari in May 2026; the government asked the district court to reimpose 20 years. Current ATF Director Robert Cekada has publicly called that sentence excessive, saying comparable non-violent NFA possession cases usually land in the three-to-five-year range. Peterson: a Louisiana FFL sentenced in January 2024 to 24 months for a homemade, unserialised suppressor found in a bedroom closet safe and not entered in the registry — the national test of whether the NFA’s suppressor scheme survives Bruen. The Fifth Circuit held suppressors are firearm accessories, not “Arms,” so the registration and tax regime does not implicate the Second Amendment; en banc rehearing was denied in December 2025 and certiorari denied April 20, 2026. The pardons do not disturb either appellate opinion. A Gun Owners of America post the same day named two further recipients, Jeremy Kettler and Shane Cox — treated here as reported, not independently verified.

ATF quietly reversed part of its 2008 suppressor-repair position — the single most immediately useful thing that happened to members this week

Fresh entrant, and the highest search channel on the board at 84. In an open letter to all FFLs dated August 28 and posted September 8, ATF states that a complete suppressor already registered in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record may be repaired, or have its caliber changed, without a new NFA making, marking or registration — provided the work does not destroy the serialized frame or receiver, alter required markings, or otherwise create a new firearm. Under the superseded 2008 guidance, replacement of damaged internals was generally one-for-one and drilling baffles or an end cap for a larger caliber was treated as making new silencer parts, each separately regulated. ATF’s own worked example now goes the other way: enlarging the baffle and end-cap openings of a registered .22 suppressor to take a 9mm projectile “will not result in making new parts.” Qualified manufacturers may also source unregistered, unmarked components from other qualified manufacturers and install them as replacements. Four boundaries are explicit. The serialized component still cannot be replaced, destroyed or re-marked. “Minimal” tube shortening from rethreading is acknowledged but undefined. Owners still cannot buy loose baffles or spare internals — the flexibility runs to qualified manufacturers holding the parts, not to consumers. And the letter expressly does not reach suppressors transferred under the Silencer Shop or Jensen injunctions without NFA registration, because its own condition is a device already in the NFRTR. ATF recommends, but does not require, notifying the agency so the registry reflects a caliber change. One outlet plus the letter PDF; this is agency guidance, not a statute, regulation or ruling, and it carries no stated effective date.

The 2026 National Firearms Survey figures are now doing legal work — and that is a higher bar than being interesting

One of only two items on the board every observed day, decaying gently (78, 74, 72) as nothing new attached — but where the numbers are being used changed, which raises the accuracy stakes. Petitioners cite the survey in their merits brief, and AmmoLand’s 09/09 argument deploys the 40 million and 26.4 million figures directly against the “unusual” prong — the same prong three amicus briefs and Missouri’s complaint are now fighting over. The figures: 40 million AR-15-type rifles; 60 million including other similarly styled semi-automatic rifles; 26.4 million adults owning at least one of the two categories; 461 million firearms overall (roughly 208 million handguns, 154 million rifles, 99 million shotguns); 33.6 million permitted public carriers; 50.5 percent of owners with magazines over ten rounds, 88 percent of those citing defence; roughly 2.2 million defensive uses a year. The week’s validation attempt is the interesting part: Dean Weingarten compared the survey’s handgun / rifle / shotgun proportions against cumulative ATF manufacturing, import and export records stretching back more than 125 years — a dataset published online by The Trace — implying roughly 513 million firearms produced-plus-imported-minus-exported through end-2024. The proportions agree to within about 0.1 percentage point; the totals differ by roughly 50 million, attributed to attrition. Method disclosed plainly: 51,398 adults screened, 18,206 potential owners, 16,688 validated owners, 98.2 percent passing an embedded attention check, and high-income households under-represented at 9 percent of respondents against an expected 23 percent. The comparison chart was produced with AI assistance.

Platform signals (Google Trends, X, Reddit) were unreachable in every run, so the distinct-outlet pickup proxy stood in for the News channel throughout — one outlet baseline, two to three elevated, four or more high salience — with one refinement introduced on 09/10: a document issued by the deciding institution itself is credited above any number of outlets.
