Top of mind10 · 02

A week spent waiting on the Supreme Court ended with a district court moving first: Connecticut’s “convertible pistol” law is on hold. The Hawaii permit ruling, the under-21 opinion and the AR-15 cases held the board all five days — and none of them changed the law a member lives under this week.

What stayed hot. The full Ninth Circuit’s Yukutake ruling led every weekday (week average 75, hot 5/5, peak 79), followed by the Justice Department’s under-21 handgun opinion (73, 5/5) and the AR-15 cases (72, 5/5, flat). All three held on commentary, term-opening coverage and member questions rather than new documents: no rehearing petition, no ATF guidance, no docket entry. Nothing reached volcanic for a fifteenth straight run.

What moved late. On Wednesday 30 September, US District Judge Kari A. Dooley preliminarily enjoined Connecticut’s Public Act 26-41 hours before its 1 October start date — the first court win against the new “Glock ban” laws. Connecticut’s attorney general says the state will keep defending the law; the NSSF, a plaintiff, welcomed the order. It entered Friday’s board at 81, so it is carried into next week rather than scored for staying power. Also Friday: the Justice Department formally told Congress it will not appeal the NFA suppressor/short-barrel ruling, and nine plaintiff states asked the court to say how far it reaches.

Two corrections, stated plainly. Johnson v. Jacobson (26-255) is a carry-licence reciprocity case, not a Minnesota under-21 case. And the Justice Department did sue Minnesota on 24 September. The daily editions of 28 September–1 October mislabelled Johnson; last week’s weekly and the dailies through 1 October said nothing had been filed against Minnesota. Both errors came from gaps in our sources and are corrected throughout this edition.

Why it matters for RTB. This week’s member-facing facts are status facts, which is exactly where calm, accurate content wins: Connecticut’s pistol law is not being enforced while the injunction stands; Minnesota’s permit law is in force while DOJ’s suit proceeds; the reciprocity petition is still pending. Draft now, in this order: a Connecticut status explainer, a one-page Supreme Court term primer timed for Monday’s order list, and a reciprocity explainer — “your permit at home is not a permit everywhere.”

03BState legislation & ballot measures — sustained heat

Statehouses, initiatives, certified questions — and federal suits against state laws
Moved ▶ Minnesota · permit-to-purchase · DOJ suit Wk avg63→ CORRECTION · complaint filed 09/24

Minnesota held the board all week — and carried the week’s biggest correction: the Justice Department had already sued on 24 September

The Justice Department’s own release, read by Friday’s daily, says DOJ sued the State of Minnesota, Minneapolis and St. Paul on 24 September, claiming the state’s permit-to-purchase and waiting-period scheme violates the Second Amendment for people buying a firearm for self-defense at home. Per DOJ, Minnesotans who do not intend to carry face a 30-day wait, and the two cities’ waiting periods run close to 60 days. This board’s editions from 25 September to 1 October, including last week’s weekly, said nothing had been filed. That was wrong; the filing was missed by our feeds, and it is corrected here as a correction, not a new event. The Reload argues permit-to-purchase is the next big litigation front and separately questions whether DOJ’s statutory basis (34 U.S.C. § 12601) supports these suits — analysis, not events. Counter-framing: The Trace’s lead analysis this week examines a year of DOJ suits against states and cities over their gun laws. Minnesota’s permit law is in force; nothing is enjoined. Minnesota sits in the Eighth Circuit, so the Ninth Circuit’s Hawaii ruling does not govern it.

03CATF, courts & regulatory — sustained heat

Agency action, dockets, rulings
Watch ○ 9th Cir. en banc · Yukutake v. Lopez · 21-16756 Wk avg75↓ day 8 · no rehearing or cert filing reported

The Hawaii permit ruling led the board every weekday — on commentary alone. No rehearing petition or cert filing yet

The full Ninth Circuit, 6–5, upheld Hawaii’s 30-day purchase-permit window and in-person inspection, treating objective, non-abusive shall-issue rules as presumptively valid unless put to “abusive ends” — the court’s reading of Bruen’s footnote nine. It binds the nine western states of the Ninth Circuit and changes no law elsewhere. Everything new this week was opinion: The Reload’s 27 September newsletter and podcast worked through the dissents, a Kauai editorial supported Hawaii’s rules and a National Review op-ed criticised the ruling (headlines, via the daily). Both readings, in their own terms: supporters see a workable test for administrative licensing rules; critics see a carve-out that lets permit regimes skip Bruen’s history test. The ordinary cert deadline is about 21 December and is routinely extended.

The docket, read again by this run, shows no entry after a 22 September amicus brief; the case was distributed for the 28 September conference and was not among Thursday’s three grants.
