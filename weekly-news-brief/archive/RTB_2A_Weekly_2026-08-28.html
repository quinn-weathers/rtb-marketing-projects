Top of mind08 · 28

Nothing moved in Congress this week — and the ground under federal firearm law still shifted three times. Every durable story of the week came from a court or an agency: a struck-down ATF kit rule, a finalised DOJ rights-restoration process, and a live NFA injunction that has now survived contact with ATF inspectors. For members, the practical question is not “what is coming” — it is what the law says today, and what is scheduled next.

Three items stayed hot on every day this board ran. Defense Distributed v. Blanche led the week at a 91 peak and an 87 average after a federal judge held ATF’s frame-and-receiver rule unconstitutional — and the most important thing the marketing team can say about it is the limit: the relief is as-applied to Defense Distributed, the Second Amendment Foundation and covered members, not nationwide. DOJ’s final firearm-rights restoration rule held an 85 average across three days on four named structural features, with the rule text itself still unread from primary source. And the Silencer Shop v. ATF NFA injunction stayed live all week at a 70 average — quieter in the numbers, more consequential in practice, because four FFLs in four regions cleared ATF compliance inspections after Form 4473-only suppressor transfers. That is the first operational evidence the injunction works in the field rather than only on paper.

Only two things rose across the week, and both are counter-narrative data, not litigation. CDC restored its defensive-gun-use estimates — a range of roughly 60,000 to 2.5 million a year, traceable to a 2013 Institute of Medicine report and removed from the CDC site in 2022 — and the item climbed 73 → 86 as it went from one outlet to three. FBI 2025 crime data rose 59 → 67 on the same mechanism. Handle both carefully: the width of a range spanning a factor of forty is the finding, and the honest framing is that CDC posts the estimate rather than having produced it.

What this week is really telling the marketing team is about tempo. Four of the five most consequential items are waiting on a single procedural event apiece — a notice of appeal, a Federal Register publication, an agency guidance document, a hearing date. None of them is a surprise. All of them are calendarable. The strongest content RTB can ship into next week is not reactive; it is the Renna v. Bonta multi-state explainer, written now and published the afternoon of September 4, when the first courtroom test of the “convertible pistol” theory happens anywhere in the country.

02State legislation & ballot measures — sustained heat

Statehouses, AG actions, ballot questions · two items cleared the gate
Moved → CA · NY · MD · multi-state hot 3 of 3 days run · first hearing T-7

The “convertible pistol” laws reach their first courtroom in seven days — Renna v. Bonta, September 4, before Judge Dana Sabraw (appointed by George W. Bush)

This was the steadiest item on the board — 68, 69, 68 across the three days the daily ran, never spiking and never falling off. The theory at issue is that a handgun is regulable because a trigger bar or similar internal part could be replaced to make it fire automatically, which reaches ordinary Glock and Glock-pattern pistols that no owner has modified. California’s AB 1127 has been in force since July 1; New York’s equivalent is under separate federal Second Amendment challenge; Maryland has enacted a version; and a fourth state is reported but not confirmed from statute by this board. Renna v. Bonta is a preliminary-injunction motion against California Penal Code § 27595(a), and the September 4 hearing is the first time any court anywhere examines the theory on the merits. Everything in this card is carried from trade press; legiscan.com was unreachable every day this week, so no statute text has been read directly.

03ATF, courts & regulatory — sustained heat

Rulemaking, dockets, enforcement · five items cleared the gate
Moved → N.D. Tex. · frame-and-receiver rule week leader · peak 91 · as-applied

The story of the week: ATF’s frame-and-receiver rule held unconstitutional in Defense Distributed v. Blanche — and the relief is as-applied, not nationwide

Opened Monday at 91, the only volcanic reading of the week, and cooled to 83 by Thursday on tempo rather than on any retraction. The holdings as reported: vagueness under the Fifth Amendment, and failure of the government’s colonial-era analogues under Bruen. Two corrections this board issued during the week are the substance of the story. First, the relief is expressly “as applied to Defense Distributed, the Second Amendment Foundation, and covered members” — trade coverage put it plainly that the ruling “is significant, but it is not nationwide.” Second, the separate Ninth Circuit item that circulated alongside it was a standing holding, not a merits ruling — the court rejected an attempt by California and Giffords to expand the ATF rule, which is a procedural outcome rather than an endorsement of anything. The remaining open facts are real: the ruling date is still unresolved across three accounts (some say Monday 08/17, the SAF-hosted opinion PDF is filenamed 8.19.26), the opinion PDF could not be retrieved from this board, and no notice of appeal is visible. Note the irony worth one sentence and no more: this is the same judge, in the same case, that the Supreme Court reversed fifteen months ago in Bondi v. VanDerStok.

Hot 3/5 days Peak 91 Avg 87 Week ▼

RTB angleAudit every ghost-gun draft written before 08/26 and fix the scope sentence before anything ships. The publishable line is: “a federal court held the rule unconstitutional as applied to the parties in that case — it did not repeal the rule for everyone.” Telling members otherwise would expose them. Do not print a ruling date until one is confirmed from the docket. The genuinely useful member piece here is not the ruling at all — it is “what party-scoped relief means and why it is not the same as a law changing,” which explains this case, the Silencer Shop injunction, and the next three rulings after them. Evergreen, on-brand, and nobody in the coverage-peer set has written it.

Moved → ATF · field compliance · suppressors hot 3 of 3 days run · peak 74

Four FFLs in four regions have cleared ATF compliance inspections after Form 4473-only suppressor transfers — the first operational proof the injunction survives contact with the agency

Ran 74, 70, 67 — the quietest important story of the week. Under the Silencer Shop injunction, dealers in four separate ATF regions have transferred suppressors on a Form 4473 alone, with no Form 4 and no tax stamp, and then passed ATF compliance inspections afterwards. That is the difference between a court order that exists and a court order that works. The first no-Form-4 transfers were reported out of Texas. The countervailing fact is the state layer: a dealer or buyer in a state like Ohio can be compliant federally under the injunction and still be exposed under state law, which is precisely the trap SB 214 leaves open. ATF has published no guidance — day fifteen and counting — and everything here is carried from a single trade outlet at headline-and-dek level.

Watch → N.D. Tex. · NFA injunction · day 15 hot 3 of 3 days run · appeal window open

Silencer Shop v. ATF — roughly 34 days of appeal window left, and the one-dollar-tax problem is still the most important sentence in the case

The flattest line on the board: 72, 69, 70. That flatness is the point — the injunction has been live and unchallenged all week. The mechanism is the quotable part, and it is arithmetic rather than a docket entry: the making and transfer taxes on silencers, SBRs, SBSs and AOWs fell from $200 to $0 on January 1, 2026 under the reconciliation package enacted July 4, 2025, the registration requirement survived the cut, and Judge Hendrix found that mismatch fatal — a registration scheme justified as a tax measure, with the tax gone. The fragility is equally real and should be said out loud: this is party-scoped preliminary relief that an appellate court could undo. No notice of appeal has been filed, and the outside edge of DOJ’s window sits near October 1. Forty-seven Republican lawmakers have publicly pressed DOJ to stop defending the scheme and to apply the ruling nationwide; DOJ has said nothing publicly in response.

Hot 3/5 days Peak 72 Avg 70 Week ▼

RTB angleThe evergreen asset the whole week points to is “the four mechanisms”: statute, litigation, rulemaking, and agency discretion — four routes changing firearm law right now with four completely different reversibilities. This case is the litigation column and the Hearing Protection Act is the statute column, and the contrast teaches a member how to read the next two years without any candidate framing at all. Ship it as a member-email lead with a permanent blog home. Separately: if the appeal window lapses on October 1 with no filing, the injunction’s practical durability changes materially, and that is a same-day publish trigger.

Trade, competitors, public-opinion data · the week’s only two risers
Moved → CDC · defensive gun use · data biggest riser · 73 → 86 · three outlets

CDC has restored its defensive-gun-use estimates — and the procedural record behind the restoration is more useful to RTB than the statistic ever was

The largest single move of the week: 73 on Wednesday to 86 on Thursday, driven entirely by outlet pickup rather than by any new fact. The restored language is reported to be identical to the text removed in 2022, and it cites a 2013 Institute of Medicine report, Priorities for Research to Reduce the Threat of Firearm-Related Violence, for a range of roughly 60,000 to 2.5 million defensive gun uses a year. The Trace is credited with reporting the restoration first, more than a month ago. CDC made the change with no public statement and declined to explain it when asked. It is also being reported as one piece of a broader administration rollback, which means the honest telling includes that context.

Hot 2/5 days Peak 86 Avg 80 Week ▲

RTB angleDo not lead with 2.5 million. A range spanning a factor of forty is a statement about how hard the thing is to measure, and the width is the finding — leading with the top of the range is exactly the kind of number-grabbing RTB’s brand is supposed to be the alternative to. Two more precision points: the range traces to an Institute of Medicine report, not to a CDC study, so say CDC posts it; and credit The Trace as first to report, including the rollback context, because omitting it would be selective and a reader will find it. The piece worth writing is the process story — “how a number gets removed from a federal website and how it gets put back” — which is genuinely interesting, defensible from any direction, and not something any coverage peer has attempted.

FBI 2025 crime data reaches two-outlet standing — reported as “the largest one-year rate decline since national estimates began”

Rose 59 to 67 across the two days it appeared, on pickup rather than on new data. The reported finding is that the 2025 murder rate ties or sets a historic low, described as the largest one-year rate decline since national estimates began, and that it occurred after a period of record firearm sales. The analyst who forecast the decline says on the record that it has not stopped and that its breadth defeats the simple partisan explanations offered by both sides — it is happening in cities and states with very different policy regimes. That last point is the whole value of the datum and the reason it should not be used as a partisan chip. fbi.gov was unreachable from this board all week, so the figures are carried from trade press without primary verification.

Do not run “more guns, less crime.” Run the analyst’s actual point: the decline is broad enough that no single policy story explains it, which is a reason for members to be calm and a reason for everyone to be humble.
