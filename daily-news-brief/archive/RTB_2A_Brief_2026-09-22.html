  1. 01

    DOJ’s threat to sue Minnesota over permit-to-purchase — first place on the calendar, not on the news

    Up one to the top slot, and the honest reason is that tomorrow is the letter’s stated deadline, not that anything was reported. On Thursday 17 September the Assistant Attorney General for Civil Rights sent a pre-suit letter to Minnesota’s governor and to the mayors of Minneapolis and St. Paul, asserting that “Minnesota’s licensing scheme imposes unreasonable barriers to residents’ lawful exercise of their right to acquire and bear arms.” The attack is on delay rather than on the permit requirement: DOJ concedes Bruen upheld shall-issue permitting and rests on Bruen’s caution against regimes where “lengthy wait times in processing license applications… deny ordinary citizens their right to public carry,” citing approval waits that “commonly exceed the statutory limit of 30 days,” some “nearly 60,” applied even after a passed NICS check. The stated deadline is Wednesday 23 September — T−1. The single most valuable thing this board can say about that date is that it is not an event. A pre-suit deadline is a date in a letter between a federal agency and a state; its passing produces no docket entry, no ruling and frequently no announcement. What to watch for is a complaint appearing on a federal docket in the days afterwards, or continued silence — and either could take a week or more to become visible. Nothing has been filed, nothing is enjoined, and Minnesota’s Report of Transfer and Transferee Permit requirements are fully in force for every applicant today. The structural weakness is unchanged and belongs in any treatment: the theory rests on the same § 12601 pattern-or-practice authority a California federal judge called “not obviously sound” in July. No second outlet has picked the letter up in two days; the agency-artefact credit taken on 09/21 was one-time and is not renewed. News falls slightly on that absence while search and forums firm on deadline anticipation — which is exactly the shape of a story that is being waited on rather than reported.

    Down three, and the first item in several runs to fall out of the t-hot band and into t-warm. The posture is unchanged: a federal judge in the District of Maryland granted a preliminary injunction barring Montgomery County from enforcing its firearm restrictions against the Silver Spring Jewish Center and its rabbi, and expressly declined to block enforcement against anyone else. The two challenged provisions bar licensed owners from possessing a firearm within 100 yards of a place of worship, and within 100 yards of places of public assembly including schools, parks and libraries. The court found a likelihood of success on the as-applied Second Amendment claim and on the free-exercise claim, citing a Ninth Circuit observation that no colony, state or territory banned firearms at places of worship until after the Fourteenth Amendment, and finding the County’s historical analogues neither close, nor early, nor well-accepted. Two constitutional claims are unresolved, nothing was held unconstitutional, and the judge flagged that further Supreme Court and circuit guidance may change the result. A separate DOJ suit against the same county’s wider gun-free-zone mandates is still pending. A falling score is not a signal that the copy discipline can relax — the 100-yard provisions remain enforceable against everyone except the two named parties.
