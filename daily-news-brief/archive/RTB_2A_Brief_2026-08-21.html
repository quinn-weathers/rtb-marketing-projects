The whole NFA win rests on a $0 tax — and the analysis now states plainly that a one-dollar tax would likely undo it

Congress remains in recess with no chamber activity of any kind until September, so nothing legislative can move for another fortnight. What sharpened this week is the reversal mechanism, and it is now expressible in a single sentence. On January 1, 2026, the transfer and making taxes on silencers, short-barrel rifles, short-barrel shotguns and “any other weapon” items fell from $200 to $0 under the reconciliation package enacted on July 4, 2025. Registration survived the cut, which is what produced the constitutional argument Judge Hendrix accepted. The Reload’s 08/16 analysis explains the return path: gun-control legislators noticed what happened, Senator Chris Murphy and others have called for raising the tax to as much as $5,000, “but even if they only succeed in raising it to $1, that would seem to undo Judge Hendrix’s order because the tax would once more achieve the goal of raising at least some revenue.” The same analysis makes the counterfactual argument explicitly: outright repeal of those NFA sections would have produced “a much more stable legal situation that’s more difficult to reverse,” but repeal needed 60 Senate votes that did not exist in 2025, which is why reconciliation plus litigation was the chosen route — “a weaker outcome, but it still actually produced one.”

Cody Wilson said he believes the concept would work and that Defense Distributed has “built a suite of tools at DEFCAD to defeat these efforts,” some live this month. New ▲▲▲ DOJ · Final rule · 18 U.S.C. § 925(c) Heat92▲▲▲#1 · new entry · top of the board

DOJ has finalised the federal firearm-rights restoration process — 330,000 applications expected in year one, effective within the month, and it does not touch state law

The largest federal firearms development of the year, and the first item in fourteen days to displace the NFA injunction at the top of this board. DOJ released its final rule publicly on Monday. Applicants must submit records, fingerprints, three character references and other information, after which DOJ determines whether they pose a danger to others and whether restoring their firearm rights would be in the public interest. Attorney General Todd Blanche: “The Second Amendment is not a second-class right, and the federal government should not permanently deprive Americans of a constitutional right without regard to whether they pose a danger to public safety… This rule establishes a rigorous, commonsense process that protects the public while giving deserving Americans a real path to restoration.” Scale. DOJ expects upwards of 330,000 applications within the first year. The rule itself states “at least 20 million adults” could be covered; US Pardon Attorney Edward Martin has estimated “30 million Americans” could be eligible. DOJ has pointedly not estimated how many will actually qualify once its restrictions are applied. Who can apply. Anyone federally prohibited — the category includes felons, fugitives, unlawful drug users, those adjudicated mentally ill, people in the country illegally, those dishonourably discharged, and those subject to domestic-violence restraining orders or convicted of domestic-violence misdemeanours. Nobody is restored automatically, and certain categories, especially violent records, are presumptively ineligible temporarily or permanently unless the applicant shows the “extraordinary circumstances of their case” overcome the presumption. There are five tiers of presumptive disqualifier: current status, permanent, ten years, five years, and reapplication. Two changes from the 2025 proposal. First, applicants whose unlawful drug use or addiction “consists only of the use of marijuana will no longer face a presumption against restoration” on that basis. Second, several offences moved from permanent presumptive disqualification to a ten-year presumption — animal abuse, threats of violence, certain explosives violations, some firearm or ammunition violations, and carrying a weapon on school grounds — running from conviction or completion of the relevant part of the sentence. Drug trafficking and misdemeanour domestic violence were proposed as ten-year presumptions and stayed there. The fee rose from the proposed $20 to $30. And the limit, in DOJ’s own words: the rule “does not restore the right to possess or otherwise handle a firearm under state law if the applicant is independently subject to any such state-law prohibition.” The process takes effect within the next month.
