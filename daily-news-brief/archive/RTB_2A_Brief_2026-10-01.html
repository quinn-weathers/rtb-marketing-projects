Top of mind10 · 01

The Supreme Court’s first orders from its long conference added three cases to the docket — none involving firearms. The Minnesota and Maryland carry petitions are still pending, and the rest of the conference’s orders are due Monday.

What happened. The Court’s Thursday order list granted review in a religious-zoning dispute (Missionaries of St. John v. Frederic), a military-divorce benefits case (Marschner) and an immigration-detention case (Rhoney v. Barbosa da Cunha), and granted the Solicitor General divided argument in Beaird v. United States. SCOTUSblog reports more orders from the 28 September conference on Monday 5 October at 9:30 a.m., just before the term’s first arguments. Johnson v. Jacobson (Minnesota’s carry age) was not among the grants and remains pending; Novotny v. Moore (Maryland’s sensitive places) was rescheduled on 24 September and was not before the Court this week.

Two things to keep straight. Beaird originally asked whether the federal felon-in-possession ban is constitutional, but the Court limited its grant to a sentencing-guidelines question; it is not a Second Amendment case, and should not be described as one. And a correction: Wednesday’s edition said no orders had been posted for the term. That was the wrong index page; orders through 1 October sit under term year 2025, and the 29 September lists concerned execution stays and an immigration application, none firearm-related.

Why it matters for RTB. Nothing changed for a member today. The useful move is timing, not prediction: Monday’s list is the next point at which the Minnesota under-21 carry question could move, and the term’s first week is when members ask “what is the Court deciding this year?” A plain “the Supreme Court term, in one page” piece — the AR-15 cases, what is pending, what a grant or denial would and would not mean — is ready to write now and safe to publish without forecasting.

02State legislation & ballot measures

Statehouses, initiatives, certified questions
New ▲ California · Colorado · firearm excise taxes analysis · one outlet · no new lawsuit reported

A Second Amendment Foundation lawyer argues history offers no tradition of special taxes on common guns — the legal theory to watch against California’s 11% and Colorado’s 6.5% levies

In a 30 September analysis for The Reload, Konstadinos Moros, SAF’s director of legal research, summarises a working paper arguing that, from the colonial period to 1900, special weapons taxes targeted Bowie knives and pocket pistols rather than common firearms, so a modern excise tax on common guns and ammunition would struggle under Bruen’s history test. The taxes he discusses are already law: California’s 11% retail excise on firearms, precursor parts and ammunition, on top of sales tax, and Colorado’s 6.5%, both alongside the federal Pittman-Robertson manufacturer’s tax. Supporters of the state taxes have tied the revenue to school safety and violence-prevention programs. This is advocacy analysis, not a ruling or a filed case; no new lawsuit was reported.

RTB angleEvergreen explainer, not a position: “what taxes and fees you actually pay when you buy a gun in California or Colorado” — the rates, what they apply to, and where the money goes. Name the legal question exists; take no side on it.

Delaware’s under-21 limits remain in force; no appeal or new filing reported

As verified Wednesday from CoastTV: the Delaware Supreme Court’s 4–1 ruling of 8 September restored House Bill 451, which generally bars 18–20-year-olds from buying or possessing most firearms and ammunition, with exceptions for shotguns and shotgun ammunition, muzzle-loading rifles, active military, qualified law enforcement and concealed-carry licence holders, and supervised use with someone 21 or older. The plaintiffs said they were evaluating options; nothing further has been reported.
