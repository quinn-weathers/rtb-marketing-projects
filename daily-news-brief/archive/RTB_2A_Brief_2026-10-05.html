Top of mind10 · 05

The Supreme Court declined this morning to hear the carry-licence reciprocity case, Johnson v. Jacobson, and the under-21 carry case, Salas-Martinez v. Texas. The term’s gun docket is still the AR-15 cases, argued 2 December.

What happened. The Court’s first order list of the term (read at source, 5 October) puts both petitions in the certiorari-denied list. There is no noted dissent and no written statement. Johnson asked whether Americans must hold a licence from each state to carry across the country, and whether reciprocity laws that don’t recognise every state’s licence survive Bruen. Salas-Martinez challenged Texas’s carry ban for 18-to-20-year-olds. Of the 57 gun-related petitions the Duke tracker listed for the 28 September conference, 48 were denied and none granted. Nine were not acted on, including Novotny (25-1324) and Calce v. City of New York (26-46), where the city’s response isn’t due until 23 November. Most of the denied petitions were federal felon-in-possession appeals.

What a denial means. A denial sets no precedent and says nothing about the merits. It does mean the Eighth Circuit’s decision upholding Minnesota’s reciprocity rules stands, and that nothing about carrying across state lines changes today. Each state still decides whose permits it honours.

Also surfaced: a ruling this brief missed. On 18 September the Fifth Circuit held, 2–1, that the federal ban on buying a handgun directly from an out-of-state dealer violates the Second Amendment (Elite Precision Customs v. ATF; AmmoLand, read). Our feeds were dark that week. It is not a new event, and it is labelled as a late find throughout.

Why it matters for RTB. Members who travel ask about reciprocity more than almost anything else, and today gives a clean, factual answer: the rules are what they were on Friday. A short “your permit at home is not a permit everywhere” explainer can ship today without any forecast in it.

New ▲ 5th Cir. · Elite Precision Customs v. ATF Heat69▲▲ decided 9/18 · LATE FIND · 8 outlets

Late find: the Fifth Circuit held the federal ban on direct interstate handgun purchases unconstitutional, 2–1

On 18 September a Fifth Circuit panel reversed a dismissal and rendered judgment for the Firearms Policy Coalition, a Texas dealer and two buyers from Arizona and New Mexico (AmmoLand, read). The court held that 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(a)(3), (a)(5) and (b)(3) together, which stop a dealer from handing a handgun directly to an out-of-state buyer, violate the Second Amendment. Today a buyer must have the handgun shipped to a dealer in their home state, who charges a transfer fee. The majority relied on the circuit’s earlier Reese decision that the right to keep arms includes the right to buy them; one judge dissented. AmmoLand notes the opinion gives dealers no operating instructions and that the government may seek rehearing or Supreme Court review. Bloomberg Law lists it in its September circuit-split review. Mother Jones (10/2) headlines a “new Trump rule” that “could transform gun sales”; the page wouldn’t load, and we have not linked it to this case. This brief missed the ruling for two weeks, while the recall layer was dark. It is a correction of omission, not a new event.

RTB angleDo not tell members they can buy handguns across state lines. The ruling binds only the Fifth Circuit (Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi), the judgment covers these plaintiffs, and ATF has issued no guidance. A short “what the Fifth Circuit decided, and what hasn’t changed” note is accurate and useful.
