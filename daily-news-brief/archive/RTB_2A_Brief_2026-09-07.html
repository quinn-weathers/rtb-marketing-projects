Top of mind09 · 07

The AR-15 case now has a date, and the United States has taken a side. The Supreme Court will hear the consolidated “assault weapon” ban cases on Wednesday, December 2, and on Friday the Justice Department filed a brief telling the justices those bans are unconstitutional — using a test that would protect the rifle and leave the National Firearms Act largely where it sits.

Three things landed over the long weekend and they belong in one paragraph, because they are the same story. First, the Court published its December argument calendar: Viramontes v. Cook County (No. 25-238) and Grant v. Higgins (No. 25-566), consolidated, one hour, 10 a.m. on December 2. Second, the Justice Department filed a 40-page amicus brief supporting the gun owners, signed by Solicitor General John D. Sauer. Third, 27 state attorneys general filed on the same side, led by Tennessee and Kansas. None of this is a ruling — a decision is not expected before roughly June 2027 — and the most important sentence in the government’s brief is the one nobody is quoting: DOJ asks the Court to measure “common use” by how heavily an arm has historically been regulated, expressly to avoid “complex empirical inquiries.” That test protects the AR-15 and, read plainly, cuts against the suppressor and machine-gun cases on this same board. Separately, and on the human side of the ledger: the President issued full and unconditional pardons in two of the most-watched NFA prosecutions of the decade.

Moved → California · enacted · verified upheld 08/27 · verified 09/03Heat 71

California’s 24/7 gun-store audio-and-video mandate survives — and the case was never about the Second Amendment

Promoted off the unverified list with the panel opinion read at source. A Ninth Circuit panel upheld California Penal Code § 26806 on Thursday, August 27, in Richards v. Newsom, 2–1. The statute, in force since January 1, 2024, requires licensed dealers to record video of every entry and exit, of areas where firearms are displayed, and of “all points of sale, sufficient to identify the parties involved in the transaction” — and requires audio from interior cameras. The plaintiffs did not bring a Second Amendment claim. They argued the First, Fourth and Fifth Amendments, and the panel held they failed to state a claim on each. Judge Lucy Koh, joined by Judge Ana de Alba, wrote that the plaintiffs “failed to allege any physical intrusion or access of recordings by the state” and had alleged “only a subjective fear that the state might misuse recordings in some unspecified way.” Recordings must be kept a year and cannot be accessed without a court order. Judge Kenneth K. Lee dissented: video would pass muster, he wrote, but “there is no rational reason to audiotape every single conversation between a firearms dealer and a potential customer,” and gun stores are “a hub of political activism.” He noted Illinois, New York and Washington require video only, as does California’s own cannabis-surveillance rule. The majority answered that the dissent rested on “theories not advanced by Plaintiffs.”

RTB angleThis is a retailer and privacy story, not a rights-restriction story, and mislabelling it would be the easy error. The publishable frame is narrow and accurate: a court found the plaintiffs had not pleaded their claims, not that the surveillance is a good idea. The dissent is the quotable half and it is safe to quote because it is a judge’s published reasoning. Note explicitly that no Second Amendment claim was raised — a reader will assume one was. And note the forward risk the ruling itself names: other states may copy it.

03ATF, courts & regulatory

New ★ SCOTUS · argument date set · US files calendar 09/04 · brief 09/04Heat 92

The AR-15 ban cases will be argued December 2 — and the Justice Department has filed for the gun owners with a test worth reading twice

Two separate developments on Friday, both verified against the Court’s own server. First, the December 2026 argument calendar sets Viramontes v. Cook County (No. 25-238) and Grant v. Higgins (No. 25-566), consolidated, for Wednesday, December 2 at 10 a.m., one hour. Second, the Justice Department filed a 40-page amicus brief supporting the petitioners, signed by Solicitor General John D. Sauer with Assistant Attorneys General Harmeet Dhillon and Brett A. Schumate. Its conclusion is unambiguous: “The AR-15 rifle is unquestionably in common use among law-abiding citizens for lawful purposes… A ban on possessing such rifles thus violates the Second Amendment.” The method is where it gets interesting. Rather than counting rifles or owners, DOJ asks the Court to read “common use” off the regulatory record: “If Congress and a significant majority of the States have long banned or severely restricted a class of arms, that practice is strong evidence that the arms are not in common use… That approach avoids the types of complex empirical inquiries that this Court’s Second Amendment cases generally eschew.” Applied to the AR-15, DOJ says the 1994 federal statute “did not limit the possession or transfer of existing AR-15s, was not widely replicated at the state level, and expired in 2004,” and that the rifles are lawful federally and in 40 states. Applied to arms with a long restrictive tradition, the same test points the other way — DOJ’s own examples of arms outside protection are Bowie knives, sword canes and brass knuckles, and it notes that “the government has argued that our regulatory tradition permits heightened regulation of certain classes of arms that are especially susceptible to criminal misuse.” It also concedes courts “may need to use ‘a more nuanced approach’” for newly introduced arms. DOJ’s fallback: “to the extent statistics are relevant, they confirm that AR-15s are in common use.” A decision is not expected before roughly June 2027.

RTB angleThis is the content anchor for the next twelve weeks and the December 2 date belongs on the calendar today. Discipline, in order: (1) it is an argument date, not a ruling, and the ruling is most likely eight or nine months out — say both in the first two sentences; (2) DOJ filed an amicus brief, it is not a party, and a government brief is advocacy, not a holding; (3) do not predict the outcome, do not repeat the “6–3” forecast circulating in the space, and do not tell a member in a ban state that anything has changed for them — nothing has; (4) the honest and genuinely useful thing RTB can publish that almost nobody else will is the tension inside the government’s own test: a standard that reads common use off the regulatory record protects the most popular rifle in the country and simultaneously supplies an argument against the suppressor and machine-gun cases sitting one section below this one. Present that as a live question, quote both sides of it, and endorse neither. Members who own suppressors deserve to know the government’s brief is not simply good news for them.

New ★ Clemency · NFA prosecutions signed 09/04 · effective nowHeat 84

Two men are out of federal prison in NFA cases — and the precedent that put one of them there is still on the books

President Trump issued full and unconditional pardons to Patrick “Tate” Adamiak and George Peterson. AmmoLand’s account is long, named and carries direct statements from both men; a Gun Owners of America post the same day named two further recipients, Jeremy Kettler and Shane Cox, which this board treats as reported-not-independently-verified. Adamiak: a former Navy petty officer first class whose Virginia Beach home was raided in April 2022 after a paid confidential informant facing his own charges told ATF he had a grenade launcher. Agents found demilled parts kits, inert and DEWAT launchers, a non-firing replica and unfinished sheet-metal “flats.” A jury convicted on five counts; consecutive stacking produced 20 years. The Fourth Circuit found double jeopardy on two counts charging the same item under both 26 U.S.C. § 5861(d) and 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), vacated one and remanded; the Supreme Court denied certiorari in May 2026; the government asked the district court to reimpose 20 years. Current ATF Director Robert Cekada has publicly called that sentence excessive, saying comparable non-violent NFA possession cases usually land in the three-to-five-year range. Adamiak: “After four years on the inside, I’ve been free for less than an hour, and I’m still in shock.” Peterson: a Louisiana FFL sentenced in January 2024 to 24 months for a homemade, unserialised suppressor found in a bedroom closet safe and not entered in the registry — a case that became the national test of whether the NFA’s suppressor scheme survives Bruen. The Fifth Circuit held suppressors are firearm accessories, not “Arms,” so the registration and tax regime does not implicate the Second Amendment; en banc rehearing was denied in December 2025 and certiorari was denied April 20, 2026. The pardons do not disturb either appellate opinion. The Fifth Circuit’s “accessories are not Arms” holding remains binding law in that circuit this morning.

RTB angleThe most empowerment-led item RTB has had in weeks and the one with the highest chance of being reported sloppily. Lead with the people: two men convicted in possession cases involving no violence, no trafficking ring and no completed commercial sale are home. Then do the thing almost no one else will bother to do — explain that a pardon frees a person and changes no law. That is a sixth entry for the court-win-can-fail explainer, arriving from the opposite direction: relief for the individual, precedent untouched. Practical consequences a member will actually ask about: a pardon does not automatically restore military service or rank, does not by itself return seized property, and does not make the underlying conduct lawful for anyone else. Answer those three questions and skip the rest. Attribute the Kettler and Cox names to Gun Owners of America and label them reported. Quote Director Cekada’s sentencing comment because it is on the record. Do not characterise any agent, prosecutor or judge’s motives — the comment threads are doing plenty of that and RTB should read differently.

New ★ SCOTUS · 27-state amicus filed 09/04Heat 72

Twenty-seven states tell the Court that a constitutional right cannot depend on which state you are standing in

Filed September 4 in support of the petitioners and read at source. Tennessee and Kansas lead; the coalition is Alabama, Alaska, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, New Hampshire, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, South Dakota, Texas, Utah, West Virginia and Wyoming. The argument is common-use plus federalism: the states accept broad authority over policy inside their borders but deny that federalism lets a state erase an enumerated right. Their framing sentence — “a fundamental right that is subject to legislative caprice and judicial speculation about what the right-holder really ‘needs’” — is the most quotable line of the weekend. The evidentiary spine: more than 32 million modern sporting rifles manufactured in or imported since 1990; other cited research placing 24–28 million AR-15s and comparable rifles in circulation and 16–24.6 million Americans owning or having owned one; a 2022 Washington Post-Ipsos survey in which 91% of responding AR-15 owners named protection as a reason and 65% as a major reason; English’s earlier survey finding 61.9% citing home defence; and a Justice Department report that 94.5% of local police departments authorise semi-automatic rifles for on-duty officers. On crime data the brief cites FBI figures showing rifles of every action type identified in 378 homicides over the one-year period cited, against 3,817 for handguns, 1,229 for knives or cutting instruments and 506 for “personal weapons” — hands, fists, feet or teeth. Across the ten-year figures the gap widens: 3,809 rifle homicides against 43,342 handgun, 10,787 knife and 5,439 personal-weapon. Within the two defending jurisdictions over that decade the brief records 4,440 Illinois handgun murders against 130 involving rifles, and 178 Connecticut handgun murders against six — and it expressly acknowledges that the FBI separately reports cases where firearm type is unidentified and does not claim rifles are never misused. Closing analogy: the bans “are the equivalent of banning the musket in 1789.” A footnote notes “AR” refers to the original ArmaLite brand and does not mean “assault rifle.” Note one sourcing wrinkle: AmmoLand renders the Grant docket number as 25-556 in this piece and 25-566 elsewhere; the board uses 25-566.

RTB angleThe single richest quarry of publishable, attributable facts on the board. Two rules. First, every figure carries its source and its year — the police-authorisation figure and the FBI comparisons are the two most likely to be lifted without attribution and the two most likely to be challenged. Second, the FBI comparison is a relative-frequency point and must be written as one, including the brief’s own caveat about unidentified firearm types; presenting it as proof that rifles are not used in crime would be inaccurate and would hand a critic an easy win. The “what the right-holder really needs” line and the ArmaLite footnote are both safe, useful and evergreen. Do not adopt the brief’s advocacy voice as RTB’s; report what 27 states argued.

Watch ○ 3d Cir. · published opinion 09/02 · still single-outletHeat 74

Berger did not spread, but Friday gave it a second life

Five days on, the Third Circuit’s published opinion has still picked up no second outlet this board can reach, so the News channel stays near the single-outlet baseline. The holding is unchanged and still the most consequential sentence published in the space this month: a two-judge majority wrote that “minimal, objective buying-and-selling regulations are not infringements” and therefore sit outside the Second Amendment entirely — a route past Bruen’s history-and-tradition burden requiring no historical analogue. Judge Chung concurred in the judgment but expressly declined to join that reasoning, noting tension with the Supreme Court’s rejection of interest balancing. What changed is the company it keeps. DOJ’s Friday brief asks the Court to treat long, widespread regulation as evidence that an arm is not in common use; Berger asks courts to treat “minimal” regulation as not an infringement at all. Different doors, same room, and NFA-registered items are inside it. Comment threads continue to assert a conflict with the Fifth Circuit on machine guns and to cite Caetano common-use figures. This board has not verified a circuit split and no court has declared one.

RTB angleQuote the majority’s sentence and quote Judge Chung’s refusal to join it, and endorse neither. Do not write “circuit split.” Tell no member that machine-gun or suppressor law changed for them, because it did not. The genuinely new and publishable observation is the alignment with the DOJ brief — write it as a question about where registration sits after this term, not as a prediction, and pair it with the Peterson pardon so a reader can see the whole shape: the person went free, the doctrine did not move.

Moved → Research · primary paper located published 08/29 · reconciled 09/07Heat 78

The 2026 National Firearms Survey, reconciled — and the four numbers that are not interchangeable

The second wave of Georgetown professor William English’s National Firearms Survey is now available as an SSRN paper with the full data deposited on Harvard’s Dataverse, and three outlets have reported it. Method: fielded July and August 2026; 51,398 American adults responded; 16,688 self-identified gun owners were validated by being asked how many of each specific category of firearm they own. English made six methodological changes from the 2021 wave in response to an academic critique, including splitting AR-15s out from other rifles and adding an ownership-verification step. Funding came from the Wealth of Nations Institute, described by The Reload as a small libertarian-leaning non-profit. The four figures, which mean four different things: approximately 40 million AR-15-type rifles; approximately 60 million when the roughly 20 million other similarly styled semi-automatic rifles are included; approximately 26.4 million adults (30.0% of gun owners) who own at least one firearm in either category; and approximately 461 million firearms in total, comprising roughly 208 million handguns, 154 million rifles and 99 million shotguns. Further findings: about 33% of U.S. adults, roughly 88 million people, own a firearm; the average owner owns 5.2 firearms and 91.6% of owners own a handgun; approximately 33.6 million owners (39.3% of those aged 21 and over) are permitted carriers who carry at least sometimes; 50.5% of owners, about 44.5 million adults, own magazines holding more than ten rounds, roughly 539 million such magazines, and 88.0% of those owners cite a defensive purpose; 37.6% of gun owners report having used a firearm defensively; and the annual defensive-use estimate rose from 1.6 million in the 2021 wave to 2.2 million. Demographically, 45.7% of validated owners are women, up from 42.2%; Black ownership rose from 25.4% to 33% against 34.6% for White Americans; Hispanic ownership rose to 31.3% and Asian American to 23%. English: “firearms ownership has continued to grow, and firearms owners are more diverse than ever.” The petitioners’ Supreme Court brief already cites this wave; the 27-state brief relies on the 2021 wave.

RTB angleFigure discipline is the entire job here. The 40, 60 and 26.4 million numbers are not alternatives to each other and mixing them is the single most likely factual error RTB could make this month: 40 million is AR-15-type rifles, 60 million adds similarly styled semi-autos, 26.4 million is people. Lock a house sentence for each and reuse it verbatim. Every figure is an estimate from a survey and must be called one; attribute to English, Georgetown, 2026 wave, and name the funder if the figure is doing persuasive work. Do not lead with the 2.2 million defensive-use estimate — the DGU literature spans a range so wide that the spread is the most honest finding, and this survey is one estimate inside it, now revised upward, which is itself worth a sentence about measurement rather than a headline. The strongest and most defensible items for a membership audience are the ones about ordinary people: 45.7% of owners are women, minority ownership up across every group measured, 88.0% citing defence, and 33.6 million permitted carriers. Those need no adjectives.
