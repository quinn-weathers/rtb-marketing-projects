The one-week stay on the NFA ruling runs out today — and as of this morning there is no confirmation that DOJ has appealed.

Sourcing status — partial-access run, and this edition covers six days. There were no August 7, 10, or 11 editions, so everything below spans Aug 6–12. The Reload was reachable and has published five new pieces in the window. All four competitor sites were reachable and checked directly. The Google News RSS recall layer returned empty on all six queries for the ninth consecutive run — the multi-outlet pickup proxy is unavailable, and local, regional, and defensive-use stories are under-sampled by construction. WebSearch was unavailable. AmmoLand, NSSF, SCOTUSblog, congress.gov, regulations.gov, atf.gov, justice.gov, the Federal Register, CourtListener, and Ballotpedia were not reachable; anything sourced to them is carried forward and flagged.

Today is the calendar item this board has been pointing at for a week. On August 5, US District Judge James Wesley Hendrix (N.D. Tex.) permanently enjoined the National Firearms Act's regulatory provisions for the items Congress zeroed out — silencers, short-barrel rifles, short-barrel shotguns, and “any other weapons” — then stayed his own ruling for one week to give the Justice Department time to appeal. That week lands on or about today, Wednesday, August 12. We could not confirm this morning whether DOJ has filed a notice of appeal or sought a stay pending appeal. justice.gov, CourtListener, and the Federal Register were all unreachable, and The Reload has published nothing on the docket since its August 9 podcast. Treat the appeal status as unresolved, not as “no appeal.” This is the single most important thing for the marketing team to verify directly before anything publishes today.

What the ruling still does and does not do. Nothing about the scope has changed since last week and it is worth restating, because the category is about to produce a great deal of loose content. The injunction runs to the plaintiffs and, where applicable, “their agencies, political subdivisions, members, and customers—both current and future” — that is Gun Owners of America, the Firearms Policy Coalition Action Foundation, the Citizens Committee for the Right to Keep and Bear Arms, and the Firearms Regulatory Accountability Coalition. It is not a nationwide holding. The plaintiffs abandoned their Second Amendment claims, so the court never reached them; the holding is an enumerated-powers one, which is exactly the kind an appellate court can narrow without saying anything about the Second Amendment. On the August 9 podcast, FRAC president Travis White — a plaintiff — laid out the theory himself: the NFA is at heart an exercise of the taxing power, and a $0 tax cannot justify registration and fingerprinting to prove a tax has been paid. Judge Hendrix agreed, and rejected the Necessary-and-Proper and Commerce Clause fallbacks.

The second story is a deadline almost nobody is covering. Per The Reload's August 9 analysis, the public comment period on ATF's “New Era of Reform” rulemaking package closed this week. Two of those rules go directly to something every RTB member does: traveling with a firearm. Clarifying Interstate Transportation of Firearms Under the Gun Control Act would treat overnight lodging, fuel and food stops, vehicle maintenance, emergencies, medical treatment, and transfers between modes of transportation as protected “transport” under FOPA — the gap that has produced arrests at New York airports for years. Interstate Transport and Temporary Export of NFA Firearms would eliminate the Form 20 notice requirement for trips under a year; as of August 3, ATF listed Form 20 wait times at four days for eForms and seven for paper, which makes spontaneous travel with an NFA item effectively impossible today. SAF's Kostas Moros argues the historical record supports both: traveler's exceptions to carry restrictions appear from 1686 East Jersey through 1813 Kentucky, 1820 Indiana, Tennessee, Arkansas, Alabama, 1864 California, 1867 Nevada, 1871 Texas, and the Arizona and New Mexico Territories.

Third: two courts split on foster parents and gun storage in the same week. On Monday, August 3, US District Judge Sue Myerscough upheld Illinois' storage rules for foster homes and licensed in-home day cares in Miller v. Mueller, treating them as “sensitive places analogous to schools.” On July 31, a Wisconsin appeals court unanimously went the other way in Lafferty v. Pertl, holding that foster parents “did not waive their Second Amendment constitutional right by volunteering to serve the community,” and striking the unloaded-storage requirement. Both applied Bruen. This is a small-audience story with unusually high member relevance.

And the numbers came in. NSSF's adjusted July NICS figures: 1,061,883 sales-related checks nationwide, up 8.5% year over year. Colorado recorded 82,103 against 22,507 a year earlier — a 264% jump ahead of its permit-to-purchase law taking effect August 1. NFA checks nationwide more than doubled year over year to 130,013. Virginia's surge has faded as its ban was enjoined and AR-15s returned to shelves.

Moved ▲▲ Federal · Senate · NFA Congress in recess · congress.gov unreachable

If the appeal narrows the ruling, S. 218 is the only path that produces a durable answer — and Congress is on recess until September

The court result and the legislative result look the same from a distance and are completely different in kind. Judge Hendrix's injunction reaches the plaintiffs and their members and customers, rests on an enumerated-powers theory the plaintiffs did not have to win on the Second Amendment, and can be narrowed, stayed, or reversed by the Fifth Circuit. The Hearing Protection Act (S. 218) would do what a court cannot: remove suppressors from the NFA by statute, for everyone, permanently, with no scope question and no appeal. Nothing has moved on it — the Senate is in August recess and returns in September — but the ruling changed the political framing. A bill that was a symbolic gesture in May is now the clean, non-litigated version of an outcome one federal judge has already reached. (Bill number, status and cosponsor counts are carried forward from earlier reporting; congress.gov, senate.gov and docs.house.gov were not reachable this run and none of it was re-verified.)

RTB angle: This is the honest counterweight to the “the NFA is dead” content the category is about to produce. The explainer writes itself and it flatters nobody's outrage: a court ruling is contingent, party-scoped and appealable; a statute is none of those things. Publish it as civics rather than advocacy, and it doubles as the reason members should keep paying attention in September rather than assuming the fight ended in Texas on August 5.

Watch → Federal · OBBBA · Tax Legislative root of the ruling

The $0 rate is still the operative fact — a provision Congress could not repeal was undone by a rate change Congress did pass

Worth keeping on the board because it is the part of the story most likely to be dropped from other people's coverage. On January 1, 2026, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act's firearms provisions took effect, cutting the NFA transfer and making taxes on silencers, short-barrel rifles, short-barrel shotguns, and “any other weapons” from $200 to $0 — while leaving registration in place, because a registry repeal could not survive reconciliation. That surviving registration requirement is precisely what fell. FRAC filed suit the same day the taxes hit zero; on the August 9 podcast its president said plainly that the tax cut is what made the statute vulnerable. Judge Hendrix found the NFA was enacted exclusively under the taxing power, that the essential feature of a tax is that it produce at least some revenue, and that the separate Special Occupation Tax on manufacturers and dealers cannot carry the user-side rules. (OBBBA effective date and rate figures per The Reload's reporting and the opinion; congress.gov not reachable.)

RTB angle: Still the best pure-civics explainer available to RTB, and it needs no adversarial framing at all: a tax rate, a reconciliation rule, and an enumerated-powers doctrine combined to produce an outcome nobody voted for directly. Lead with the mechanism, close with the stay and the appeal. It also quietly makes the case for reading past headlines, which is a habit RTB benefits from members having.

New ▲▲▲ ATF · Rulemaking · Interstate transport Heat78▲▲▲#2 · comment period closed this week

ATF has proposed to fix the two things that get lawful gun owners arrested while traveling — and the comment window just shut

Per The Reload's August 9 analysis, the public comment period on ATF's rulemaking package — which the agency branded a “New Era of Reform” — closed this week. Two of the proposed rules deal with transporting firearms and are, by the analysis's own assessment, “among the most impactful in ATF's package.” The first, Clarifying Interstate Transportation of Firearms Under the Gun Control Act, would provide that activities “reasonably necessary to transportation such as staying overnight in temporary lodging, stopping for food, fuel, vehicle maintenance, an emergency, or medical treatment, or transiting between modes of transportation” count as protected transport under FOPA — the gap that has produced prosecutions for people passing through jurisdictions like New York City and its airports. The second, Interstate Transport and Temporary Export of National Firearms Act Firearms, would eliminate the NFA travel-notice requirement for trips shorter than a year; as of August 3, 2026, ATF listed Form 20 processing at four days for eForms and seven for paper, which makes any spontaneous trip with an NFA item impossible in practice. SAF's Kostas Moros argues the historical record strongly supports both under Bruen: traveler's exceptions to carry restrictions run from the 1686 Province of East Jersey through 1813 Kentucky, 1820 Indiana, Tennessee, Arkansas, Alabama, 1864 California, 1867 Nevada, 1871 Texas, and the Arizona and New Mexico Territories — a recent Hawaii filing compiled more than 80 examples. Both rules are, as he notes, among the most likely in the package to be challenged in court. (Comment-period close, rule citations and Form 20 wait times are all from The Reload's Aug 9 analysis; regulations.gov, atf.gov and federalregister.gov were unreachable and none of it was independently verified. The exact closing date was not stated.)

New ▲▲ Courts · Storage · Ill./Wis. Heat69▲▲#6 · two rulings, one week, opposite results

On Monday, August 3, US District Judge Sue Myerscough upheld Illinois' firearms storage restrictions for foster homes and licensed in-home day cares in Miller v. New ▲▲ Culture · History · Travel Evergreen · strongest content asset on the board

The traveler's exception is one of the oldest continuous ideas in American gun law — and almost nobody in the category knows it exists

SAF's Kostas Moros assembled the record in The Reload on August 9, and it is remarkable how consistent it is. In 1686 the Province of East Jersey banned “privately” wearing weapons but exempted “all strangers, travelling upon their lawful occasions thro' this Province, behaving themselves peaceably.” An 1813 Kentucky law banned concealed carry “unless when travelling on a journey.” An 1820 Indiana statute said its ban “shall not be so construed as to affect travellers.” Tennessee, Arkansas and Alabama followed before the Civil War; California in 1864 and Nevada in 1867 after it. Texas' sweeping 1871 carry ban exempted “persons traveling.” The Arizona Territory did the same in the 1880s; the New Mexico Territory in 1887 allowed travelers to “carry arms for their own protection while actually prosecuting their journey and may pass through settlements on their road without disarming.” A recent Hawaii filing compiled more than 80 such laws. Even Oliver Wendell Holmes, Jr. noted that whatever one thought of concealed-carry bans generally, carry by travelers was a recognized right. The through-line is a principle rather than a rule: visitors from elsewhere got more leeway than residents, because a person on a journey cannot comply with fifty different local regimes at once. That is the same problem FOPA was written to solve and the same one ATF's proposed transport rules address.

RTB angle: This is the best piece of pure content on today's board and it has nothing to do with a news cycle. It is history, it is verifiably sourced to primary statutes through the Duke firearms-law archive, and it makes an empowerment argument without an antagonist: the idea that a traveler keeps the means of self-defense is older than the Republic. Pair it with the practical interstate-travel guide as a two-part set — the heritage piece earns the attention, the practical piece earns the membership. Be precise that these were exceptions to carry restrictions, not transport rules, and that the analogy is the argument rather than a settled holding.
