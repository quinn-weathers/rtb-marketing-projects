Two courthouse doors opened on the same question in one afternoon: whether a permit — or a right — survives the drive across a state line. 03ATF, courts & regulatory

Litigation, dockets, rulemaking, enforcement
New ● SCOTUS · No. 26-255 · petition Heat82▲ filed 08/24 · reported 09/03

A trucker’s petition puts carry reciprocity in front of the Supreme Court for the first time since Wolford

Jeffrey M. Johnson Sr., a Georgia resident who drives commercially nationwide, holds valid Georgia and Florida carry licences. Minnesota recognises neither. Crossing the state line, he must unload and case the handgun; crossing back, he may carry again. His petition for certiorari — filed August 24, docketed as No. 26-255 — challenges an Eighth Circuit ruling that Minnesota’s reciprocity statute, considered separately from its permit requirement, does not regulate conduct protected by the Second Amendment at all; recognising another state’s permit is, in the panel’s words, a “convenience” grounded in interstate comity. The panel compared carry permits to professional licences. The petition leans hard on Wolford v. Lopez’s holding that the Second Amendment has the “same meaning in all parts” of the country, and argues Minnesota’s surety-law analogue fails because surety laws required an accusation, a hearing, and an individualised finding. Johnson conceded below that Minnesota’s shall-issue system itself is constitutional — so this is not a challenge to permitting, only to non-recognition. The Court has not granted review; the justices must first decide whether to take it.

RTB angleThis is the best membership-education hook on the board and it does not require a single prediction to be useful. The evergreen piece is “your permit is a state document, and here is what that means when you drive” — reciprocity maps change, they change without notice, and the legal consequence of getting it wrong falls on the traveller. Anchor it to the case as context, then keep the piece useful after the docket resolves either way. Say clearly and early that the Court has not agreed to hear it. Do not tell any member their permit is or is not honoured in a given state — point them to the issuing and destination authorities. Do not imply a ruling is coming this term.

New ● D. Mass. · Lawson v. Campbell Heat76▲ MTD order 08/31 · reported 09/03

A federal judge refuses to let Massachusetts moot a carry-delay case by issuing the permits late

Chief U.S. District Judge Denise Casper denied dismissal of the core claims brought by the Second Amendment Foundation, Gun Owners’ Action League, and three nonresident licence holders. The alleged facts are administrative, not dramatic: Massachusetts issues nonresidents one-year licences where residents get six, charges the same $100 either way, requires an in-person appearance in Chelsea for new applicants, and gives residents — but not nonresidents — a renewal grace period. Statute allows 40 days to approve or deny; the state’s own site reportedly warns of up to 90; plaintiffs allege far longer, including one renewal issued nearly six months after expiry. The court held that a sufficiently long delay can itself be a constitutional injury, that the pattern is “capable of repetition yet evading review,” and that Massachusetts identified no historical tradition supporting it. The equal-protection claim survived on the grace-period disparity. The facial Second Amendment challenge and the Article IV Privileges and Immunities claim were dismissed. This is a ruling on a motion, not on the merits; the case now moves toward discovery.

RTB angleThe most under-served story in the category and the one closest to what members actually experience. The frame is “delay is not neutral” — the days you cannot legally carry are days you cannot legally carry, whatever the eventual paperwork says. That is empowerment content, not grievance content, if it ends somewhere useful: keep your own dated record of application and renewal submissions, know your state’s statutory clock, and know what a grace period is and whether yours has one. Be explicit that this is a procedural ruling and that two claims were dismissed — overstating it would be the easiest error available today.

Novotny v. Moore is distributed for the long conference — four outcomes are live and none is predictable

Counsel for Maryland permit holders, SAF, FPC, Maryland Shall Issue and the Maryland State Rifle & Pistol Association filed their reply brief on September 2; the Court simultaneously distributed the petition for its September 28 conference, the first after the summer recess. The question is whether Maryland can achieve through a stack of location-specific bans — mass transit, many healthcare facilities, state parks and forests, amusement venues, and anywhere licensed to serve alcohol on site — what Hawaii could not achieve with one private-property default rule struck down in Wolford. Challengers call it “death by a thousand cuts”; Maryland calls its approach targeted and asks for more percolation in the circuits. Current enforcement posture is mixed: the Fourth Circuit stayed its mandate, so the district court’s injunctions against the demonstration and alcohol-service bans appear to remain in effect, while most other restrictions remain enforceable.

Moved → Circuits · under-21 · conflict Heat72▲ 3 rulings · 3 directions

The under-21 question is now three courts pointing three ways — and the newest opinion runs on Founding-era contract law

Judge Jill Otake’s 43-page order in Roache v. Lopez (09/01) granted Hawaii summary judgment against SAF and two dealers, upholding a ban that stops 18-to-20-year-olds from acquiring essentially any firearm or ammunition. The court conceded that 18-to-20-year-olds are part of “the people,” conceded that acquisition is covered by the plain text, and conceded the Founding-era record on parental gifts was ambiguous — then upheld the ban anyway, treating the common-law “infancy doctrine” governing minors’ contracts as a historical firearm regulation and leaning on later nineteenth-century pistol statutes. It expressly found the Fifth Circuit’s contrary reasoning in Reese v. ATF less persuasive than Fourth and Eleventh Circuit decisions. Four days earlier a federal judge in Massachusetts upheld that state’s under-21 semi-auto possession ban. Same constitutional question, three different answers depending on which map you are standing on.

RTB angleA clean, non-partisan explainer sits here: “why the same right can mean different things in different circuits, and what that means practically for an 18-to-20-year-old.” Quote the concessions — a court admitting young adults are “the people” and that acquisition is protected is the most quotable material in the opinion — and quote the disagreement, without calling any judge an activist. This is also the sharpest forum thread on the board (33 comments), which means the audience is present and the tone discipline matters more, not less. No prediction on appeal.

Three federal and academic datasets now point the same direction, and none of them needs an adjective to be persuasive

CDC has restored defensive-gun-use estimates to its published material, reported by The Reload on 08/28; the same edition carried crime analyst Jeff Asher explaining a record-low national murder rate; and the 2026 English National Firearms Survey estimates roughly 60 million AR-style rifles in American hands, plus an estimate of roughly 180,000 defensive gun uses against animals annually. These are survey estimates and agency ranges, not counts, and the DGU literature spans a range so wide — a factor of forty between the low and high credible estimates — that the spread is itself the most honest finding.

RTB angleThe most durable content RTB can publish this month, and the discipline is entirely in the numbers. Attribute every figure by name and year, call estimates estimates, and do not lead with 2.5 million. A range spanning a factor of forty is a statement about how hard this is to measure, and saying that plainly builds more credibility than any single number would. The audience-facing version is “here is what we actually know, here is what nobody knows, here is why the numbers disagree” — which is exactly the tone that differentiates RTB from fear-first marketing.
