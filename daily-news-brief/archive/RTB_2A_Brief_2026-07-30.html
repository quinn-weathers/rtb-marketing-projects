The Freedom From Unfair Gun Taxes Act moves from background to foreground as state excise-tax bills multiply

S. 1169 (Sen. Jim Risch, R-Idaho) and H.R. 2442 (Reps. Darrell Issa, R-Calif., and Richard Hudson, R-N.C.) would bar states and their political subdivisions from imposing excise taxes on the sale of firearms, ammunition, or components by manufacturers, producers, or importers. The bills are drafted to leave the federal Pittman-Robertson framework untouched — an important detail, because conservation funding is the usual first objection. The reason this is climbing: California already levies an additional 11 percent state excise tax, Colorado imposes 6.5 percent on firearms, precursor parts, and ammunition, and NSSF counts introduced bills in five more states — Maryland, Massachusetts, New York, Virginia, and Washington. Virginia’s HB 919 would impose 11 percent on gross receipts from retail firearm and ammunition sales. Sen. John Cornyn (R-Texas) has signed on as a cosponsor, framing it as protecting the $0 federal suppressor stamp enacted in the Working Families Tax Cuts Act from being offset by state-level surcharges. (Bill numbers and cosponsor counts are from NSSF’s summary; congress.gov was not reachable this run — verify status before publishing.)

RTB angle: This is the cleanest “cost of exercising a right” story available, and it lands squarely on the members who feel it most: first-time buyers and working families. The strongest asset is a simple state-by-state table of what a box of ammunition and a $600 handgun actually cost after each state’s stack of taxes and fees. Use the poll-tax analogy carefully and attribute it — it is an argument advocates make, not a holding. Note honestly that opponents frame these taxes as offsetting public-safety costs, and that the federal preemption question here is genuinely contested.

New ▲▲ State · Virginia · Litigation AmmoLand opinion column · motion text not independently retrieved

Virginia’s Attorney General defends the state ban by arguing Virginia’s own arms provision is a collective, militia-tethered right

In a motion to dismiss and demurrer filed July 15 in Crump v. Katz — one of five challenges to Virginia’s SB 749 firearm and magazine ban — Attorney General Jay Jones argues first that no plaintiff has standing, and then that “federal Second Amendment doctrine does not control the meaning of § 13” because “marked textual differences” between Article I § 13 of the Virginia Constitution and the Second Amendment mean federal interpretations “inform, but do not necessarily govern” the state provision. The motion then reads § 13’s text and ratification history as establishing a collective guarantee tied to militia service rather than a freestanding individual right. Two things are worth knowing before repeating any characterization of this: the operative clause of § 13 reads “the right of the people to keep and bear arms shall not be infringed,” and the Supreme Court in Wolford v. Lopez rejected Hawaii’s parallel attempt to read local tradition into the scope of the right, writing that the Second Amendment “has the same meaning in all parts of the United States.” Because the Second Amendment binds Virginia through the Fourteenth under McDonald, a narrower state-constitutional reading does not lower the federal floor. (Sourced to an AmmoLand opinion column that quotes the filing; the motion itself was not independently retrieved. Note also that the column’s lead plaintiff is that outlet’s own contributor — read it as advocacy and verify quotations against the docket.)

RTB angle: Explain, do not dunk. The useful member takeaway is structural and reassuring: state constitutions can add protection but cannot subtract it, because incorporation sets a floor. That is a genuinely empowering fact and most people do not know it. A short “how state and federal gun-rights protections stack” explainer would serve members in every state, not just Virginia. Pull the actual motion before quoting it, and keep the tone measured — the argument is unusual, and letting readers see it plainly is more persuasive than mocking it.

Oregon’s anti-hunting ballot initiative fails to qualify — and the reason it mattered was the funding, not just the hunting

Initiative Petition 28, styled the “People for the Elimination of Animal Cruelty Exemptions (PEACE) Act,” was disqualified by the Oregon Secretary of State’s Elections Division from the Nov 3, 2026 ballot after certifying 104,262 valid signatures against a 117,173 requirement. The measure would have removed the legal exemptions that make regulated hunting, trapping, fishing, and livestock production lawful — effectively criminalizing them — while Oregon’s existing animal-cruelty statutes already stand. The structural stake, and the reason NSSF opposed it in the industry’s own interest, is the conservation-funding chain: roughly 85 percent of Oregon’s wildlife money comes from the combination of federal excise taxes paid by firearm and ammunition manufacturers and hunting license, permit, and stamp fees. Of the nearly $1.3 billion the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service apportioned to state wildlife agencies last year, more than $804 million came from the Pittman-Robertson excise tax; Oregon received over $9.7 million.

RTB angle: This is the empowerment lane at its best and the frame RTB should own: gun owners and hunters are the people who actually pay for American conservation, and the number is public and verifiable. A short explainer — “every box of ammunition you buy funds wildlife restoration, here is exactly how” — reaches an audience well outside the usual 2A conversation and requires no adversary. Report the outcome as a signature-threshold failure, which is what it was; do not overstate it as a popular vote against the measure.

Two new stays freeze Virginia’s AR-15 fight in place: the ban is unenforceable until the Supreme Court rules

On Wednesday, July 29, Judge Jeffrey L. Campbell of Virginia’s 28th Judicial Circuit ordered in Santolla v. Katz that his preliminary injunction “shall remain in full effect” and that the firearms and magazine bans “are not enforceable, until further Order of this Court or no later [than] 60 days after the U.S. Supreme Court issues a decision in Viramontes and Grant, whichever occurs.” On Friday, July 24, US District Judge Leonie M. Brinkema entered a parallel order in McDonald v. Katz, the Second Amendment Foundation’s federal challenge, staying all deadlines and proceedings pending the Supreme Court’s mandates in the consolidated cases. Together the orders convert a volatile injunction posture into a stable one: sales that resumed statewide on July 21 continue until SCOTUS decides the AR-15 ban question — almost certainly by the end of next June. Attorney General Jay Jones, who had himself urged courts to stay the pending suits to “prevent conflicting outcomes,” maintains the state will “continue to vigorously defend” the ban; his office did not respond to a request for comment on the new stays. The caveats are real: other challenges remain pending (including DOJ’s), a higher court could undo Judge Campbell’s injunction, and The Reload’s earlier analysis of residual legal liability for buyers and sellers under an enjoined-but-not-repealed statute still applies. NSSF’s Mark Oliva says manufacturers are shipping; VCDL’s Philip Van Cleave notes most — not all — dealers have resumed sales.

RTB angle: The member-facing story is stability with an asterisk, and precision is the product here. What changed: enforcement is off until 60 days after the Supreme Court rules. What did not change: the statute is still on the books, an appellate court could disturb the injunction, and an injunction is not a repeal. A dated Virginia update — “what the new stays mean if you bought, sold, or are considering buying” — with those three caveats stated plainly is exactly the calm, legally literate voice RTB owns. Tie the timeline to Viramontes so members understand why June 2027 is the date that matters.

New ▲▲▲ Courts · D. Conn. Heat70▲▲▲The Reload · opinion linked via CourtListener; CT AG statement linked

Succow v. Blanche: a bench-trial ruling upholds the federal and Connecticut under-21 handgun restrictions — and deepens a 3–3 circuit split

US District Judge Sarala Nagala upheld both the federal ban on FFL handgun sales to adults under 21 and Connecticut’s broader restrictions barring 18-to-20-year-olds from possessing or carrying handguns, in a 76-page ruling issued after a two-day bench trial with expert historical testimony (reported July 28). The reasoning is the part worth understanding, because the two holdings rest on different history. For the federal sales ban, Nagala found the Founding-era “infancy doctrine” — under which those under 21 were minors who “lack[ed] appropriate judgment and discretion” to contract for non-essential goods — a sufficient analogue, expressly invoking the no-“historical twin” latitude. For Connecticut’s possession-and-carry restrictions, she conceded there were “no laws or restrictions that demonstrate a general societal restriction” on minors possessing arms at the Founding, and reached instead for Reconstruction-era state laws as the analogues — the exact evidentiary move (later history doing the work Founding history cannot) that under-21 plaintiffs nationwide contest. The plaintiffs — two 19-year-olds with the Connecticut Citizens Defense League and Second Amendment Foundation — called that the ruling’s flaw and say they are “evaluating all options… including at the US Supreme Court if necessary.” AG William Tong called it “a major victory for commonsense gun safety.” The circuit courts remain split 3–3 on under-21 restrictions, and SCOTUS has so far declined to resolve it.

RTB angle: The under-21 question is one of the most practically confusing areas in firearms law and a real member concern — households with young adults live the patchwork daily. The durable piece is a neutral explainer: what federal law restricts for 18-to-20-year-olds, what varies by state, and why courts disagree (Founding-era versus Reconstruction-era history). Report both holdings and both sides’ statements; the Founding-vs-later-history methodology fight is the same one running through Viramontes, which makes this a useful on-ramp to the fall’s main event. No outcome predictions on appeal.

New ▲▲ Courts · E.D. Mo. · NFA Heat64▲▲AmmoLand summary of Mark Smith commentary · brief not independently retrieved

DOJ tells a district court Wolford “does not meaningfully affect” the NFA challenge — and calls the registry a “shall-issue licensing scheme”

In a July 27 supplemental brief in Brown v. ATF (E.D. Mo.), the challenge to NFA registration of short-barreled rifles and suppressors, the Justice Department answered the court’s post-Wolford briefing order by arguing the decision “does not meaningfully affect how this Court should resolve plaintiffs’ Second Amendment challenge” — reading Wolford as a case about where firearms may be carried rather than what arms may be regulated. The brief reportedly extends Miller’s short-barreled-shotgun language to short-barreled rifles, keeps the “common use” inquiry at Bruen’s plain-text step (placing the burden on gun owners rather than the government), and describes the NFA as “a presumptively constitutional shall-issue licensing scheme” even while conceding it “requires… that the firearm be registered to whomever will possess it.” Critics, led by constitutional attorney Mark Smith, argue each move contradicts Wolford’s sequencing — Justice Barrett’s concurrence warned against “smuggl[ing] additional limits” into the plain-text stage — and note the friction with the administration’s stated Second Amendment posture, including its own support for the challengers in Wolford itself. U.S. v. Squire, Day 10: still no argument date on the Commerce Clause challenge to the felon-in-possession ban

Nothing new reached this run. The posture is unchanged: the Fifth Circuit voted July 20 to rehear United States v. Squire en banc on a pure Commerce Clause theory — that a firearm possessed in a Louisiana home, federal only because it once crossed a state line, exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers to reach. A win on that ground could invalidate § 922(g)(1) on its face, which is why Judge Higginson’s lone recorded dissent warned the grant could “imperil much of the United States Code.” The court took up an invitation Justice Thomas issued in his solo Hemani concurrence (“the Court, and lower courts, should revisit the constitutionality of § 922(g)”). Ten days on: no argument date, no vote breakdown published. Every appellate court to date has upheld § 922 against Commerce Clause challenges.

RTB angle: Explainer only, and precision matters more here than anywhere else on the board. The correct framing remains that a federal appellate court agreed to reconsider whether Congress had constitutional authority to enact a specific gun law — not that anything changes for anyone today. Quote Higginson’s dissent to show the stakes honestly. No predictions, no timeline claims.

New ▲▲ Industry · Banking NSSF (Keane) · company-supplied accounts, one side reported

De-banking flares again — and this time a governor, an attorney general, a state statute, and a federal rule are all already in place

NSSF reports that financial services provider Ramp told executives at Kent Cartridge Company, a West Virginia ammunition manufacturer, that it could not do business with them because of the partners supporting its back-end cart and payment operations. West Virginia Gov. Patrick Morrisey posted publicly that he is looking into it, invoking the state’s Firearm Industry Nondiscrimination (FIND) Act that he signed; NSSF says Attorney General JB McCuskey is reviewing the matter as well. The federal backdrop has also shifted since the last round of de-banking fights: a 2025 executive order on fair banking, and an OCC/FDIC final rule eliminating “reputational risk” from supervisory programs, which bars the agencies from requiring, instructing, or encouraging institutions to close accounts based on lawful but politically disfavored business activity. The inconsistency angle is what gives the story legs: NSSF reports that eight days after it publicized the Kent Cartridge denial, Ramp was still emailing executives at Davidson’s Inc., a Prescott, Arizona firearm distributor, seeking their business — by Davidson’s count roughly the thirty-ninth such email in a couple of years. (Both accounts come from the companies themselves via NSSF, with permission; Ramp’s response is not reported and no regulatory finding exists. Treat as one side of a disputed matter.)

RTB angle: The empowerment version of this story is not outrage — it is recourse. Members who own firearm-adjacent businesses, and there are many, mostly do not know that roughly two dozen states now have FIND-style statutes, that the OCC/FDIC rule exists, or that state attorneys general have opened inquiries on facts like these. A practical explainer — “if a processor drops you, here is who to contact and what to document” — would be genuinely useful and almost nobody in this space has written it. Name Ramp only in the context of what NSSF reported, note that Ramp has not publicly responded, and avoid asserting a legal violation that no regulator has found.

New ▲▲ Industry · Manufacturing AmmoLand · company announcement, figures unaudited

B&T USA says its Utah plant can now build 7,000 suppressors a month — the supply side of the suppressor boom gets a number

B&T USA says its expanded 88,000-square-foot facility in Herriman, Utah now has capacity for as many as 7,000 suppressors per month, or roughly 84,000 a year, with product development, machining, additive manufacturing, finishing, assembly, quality control, customer service, and shipping consolidated under one roof. The growth figures are the story: from three employees in late 2025 to more than 55 in under a year, and from two industrial 3-D printers and one CNC machine to nine printers and seven CNC machines, with more than 20 machinists hired to run the in-house shop. The company says it will keep hiring and adding equipment through 2026. Context worth carrying: the announcement was issued under the B&T USA name with Ridley Key as CEO, and follows a comprehensive settlement in which Switzerland-based B&T AG resolved trademark litigation and, per its own statement, regained control of the U.S. trademarks while establishing a transition framework and its own American operation. The release does not explain how the Herriman facility fits into that framework. (Capacity and headcount figures are company-supplied and unverified; the corporate-structure question is genuinely unresolved.)

RTB angle: This is the counter-narrative asset the industry rarely gets to use, and it lands the same week as the export-jurisdiction change: a firearms-adjacent company creating skilled American manufacturing jobs — machinists, engineers, quality-control specialists — in a specific town with specific numbers. It pairs naturally with a plain, non-defensive explanation of what a suppressor actually does: reduces muzzle blast and sound pressure, protects hearing, reduces disturbance to neighbors. Not a silencer. Keep the corporate-dispute caveat in any coverage; the ownership picture is not settled.

New ▲ Industry · Conservation NSSF / USFWS apportionment data

Nearly $1.3 billion to state wildlife agencies — more than $804 million of it paid by firearm and ammunition makers

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s latest apportionment sends nearly $1.3 billion to state conservation and wildlife-access programs, derived from Pittman-Robertson excise taxes paid by firearm and ammunition manufacturers and Dingell-Johnson excise taxes paid by the fishing-tackle and boating industries. NSSF puts the firearm and ammunition share at more than $804 million. The Oregon ballot fight in Section 02 shows why the number is more than a talking point: roughly 85 percent of that state’s wildlife funding comes from the combination of these excise taxes and hunting license and stamp fees, and Oregon alone received over $9.7 million. It is also the reason the federal excise-tax preemption bills in Section 01 are drafted to explicitly leave Pittman-Robertson untouched — the distinction those bills draw is between a user-pays conservation model and state surcharges routed to gun-control programs.

RTB angle: The single most underused fact in this entire space, and it belongs in RTB’s evergreen library rather than a news post: gun owners are the largest private funders of American wildlife conservation, by statute, and the dollar figures are published on a federal dashboard anyone can check. It reframes the audience for people who have never thought about it, requires no opponent, and is fully verifiable. Link the USFWS dashboard directly so readers can look up their own state’s number — that self-verification is what makes the piece credible.

Range USA puts all 50 locations into the Gun Storage Check Week retailer pilot

NSSF announced that all 50 Range USA locations are participating in the Gun Storage Check Week retailer pilot program — a voluntary, industry-run effort in which retailers prompt customers to check their storage practices and make secure-storage options visible at point of sale. RTB angle: Secure storage is one of the few subjects where the empowerment frame and the safety frame are the same frame: storage that is fast to access and secure against everyone else is what a responsibly armed household actually needs, and it is a skills-and-equipment conversation rather than a political one. Watch → National · Defensive use Local reporting via AmmoLand · three separate incidents

Three defensive-use cases now resolved or unresolved in different ways — and the differences are the lesson

Three cases carried across recent runs have reached different postures, which is exactly why they are useful together rather than separately. In Springfield, Massachusetts, a legally armed woman fired a single shot after an armed man allegedly pursued her husband at their home; prosecutors ruled the shooting lawful, with video, witness testimony, 911 recordings, and physical evidence supporting her account. In Gastonia, North Carolina, a 74-year-old homeowner fired once at an alleged repeat burglar forcing a window; police say he will not face charges. In Phoenix, Arizona, a resident reportedly intervened during an ongoing knife attack in a neighboring apartment, shooting a man police say was stabbing two women — and that legal outcome remains unresolved. The pattern is not that armed defense works; it is that the evidentiary record and the relationship between the defender and the threat drive what happens afterward. Massachusetts had corroboration from four independent sources. The Phoenix case involves intervention on behalf of third parties in another dwelling, which is the hardest category in almost every state’s law.

RTB angle: This is the core of what RTB actually does, and the honest version is more persuasive than a highlight reel: lawful self-defense is frequently followed by a legal process, and how that process goes depends heavily on evidence and on the specific legal posture of the encounter. Third-party defense is genuinely more legally complex than defending yourself or your household, and members deserve to know that before they need to know it. Factual sequence only, no celebration, no speculation about the Phoenix outcome, and no naming of private individuals beyond what local reporting has published.

The Court’s summer docket is busy in the background — and the emergency-application pattern is the thing to watch

SCOTUSblog’s calendar puts the next summer order lists on Aug 17 and Sep 4, the long conference on Sep 28, and the resumption of arguments on Oct 5. October Term 2025 closed with 74 cases granted, 58 arguments held, 67 decisions issued, and no pending decisions; the interim docket recorded 50 decided matters. The recess is not quiet: the administration asked the Court on July 27 to clear the way for new mail-in voting restrictions, appears not to have sought rehearing in the birthright citizenship case, and on July 29 formally asked the Court to hear the appeal of the $83.3 million defamation verdict — with the separate $5 million petition possibly considered in August. None of this is a firearms matter. It is relevant because the emergency and interim docket — how often applications are granted, how they are explained, and how much reasoning accompanies them — is the same procedural machinery that will handle any request for emergency relief in a state gun-law case, and Viramontes/Grant will be argued into whatever institutional posture the Court is in by then.

RTB angle: This is analyst context for the marketing team rather than member-facing content, and it should stay that way. The one durable member-facing piece here is a short, neutral explainer on how the Supreme Court’s year actually works — cert petitions, the long conference, argument sittings, opinion season, emergency applications — so that when a 2A ruling comes members understand what they are reading and what it does and does not change. Publish nothing partisan on the non-firearms cases; the goal is procedural literacy.
