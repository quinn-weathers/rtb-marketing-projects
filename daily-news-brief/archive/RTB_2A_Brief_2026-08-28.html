The Friday ballot sweep reached Ballotpedia on its eleventh attempt — and found a firearm ballot measure this board has never carried. In the same run, ATF confirmed it was breached by a ransomware crew, and the Supreme Court’s AR-15 case picked up two named amicus efforts built on a clause the Court has never squarely construed.

The ballot file first, because eleven runs of failure ended today and the result changes what the marketing team can publish. ballotpedia.org/2026_ballot_measures was reachable and read. Two firearm-specific statewide measures are certified. Massachusetts carries a Firearm Regulations Referendum, described by Ballotpedia as a veto referendum to “Uphold the legislation, An Act Modernizing Firearm Laws (H. 4885), passed in 2024.” The mechanics matter more than the label and are the single most correctable error in circulation: this is a veto referendum, so a vote to keep the 2024 law is a vote in favour, and the repeal side is the “no” side. Note also that Ballotpedia does not use the “Question 9” label this board has carried for eleven runs — the question number is assigned by the Secretary of the Commonwealth and should be confirmed there before it appears in copy. The second measure is new to this board entirely: Arkansas has a legislatively referred Right to Keep and Bear Arms Amendment that would “create a right to keep and bear arms without limitation on the possession and use of ammunition, firearm accessories, or firearm components.” That is an affirmative state-constitutional expansion aimed squarely at accessory and component restrictions, and it has been sitting certified while this board reported the ballot file as unverifiable.

Two cautions on that, stated plainly. The Ballotpedia overview line reads “As of September 24, 2025, 56 statewide ballot measures were certified for the ballot in 28 states” — an eleven-month-old snapshot against a 2010–2024 even-year average of 161. Treat today’s roster as a floor, not a ceiling: measures certified since that date may not appear. And Oregon IP 28 does not appear anywhere — Oregon has no 2026 entry on the page at all, which is consistent with the disqualification this board has carried but is an absence of evidence rather than a confirmation. Both firearm measures need a Secretary of State confirmation before they carry a question number in published copy.

Second: ATF confirmed a cyber intrusion, and the discipline here is to report exactly what was confirmed and nothing beyond it. AmmoLand flagged a claim by the Russian-speaking ransomware syndicate Qilin; the Department of Justice then confirmed the attack. @ATFHQ posted on August 26: “ATF is responding to a cybersecurity incident affecting a standalone system not connected to the ATF enterprise network, ATF eForms system, or any other ATF system,” adding the system “was quickly shut down when the breach was discovered” and that this “is an ongoing investigation.” Sources inside ATF told AmmoLand the intruders obtained investigative tools and operational files and that gun-owner information was not compromised. So: an agency admission of a breach on a segmented system, an agency denial that eForms was touched, an anonymous internal assurance about gun-owner records, and a criminal crew’s boast on the other side. Qilin is a ransomware-as-a-service platform that started in 2022 as Agenda, runs a double-extortion playbook, pays affiliates 80–85 percent of ransoms, and has previously claimed the U.S. Marshals Service, FEMA, DHS and the FBI. The honest headline is “ATF confirms breach of a standalone system; says eForms untouched” — not a registry-exposure story, which no one has substantiated.

Third: the AR-15 case now has a doctrinal theory attached to it, and the movement is visibly not unified on it. Attorney G. Antaeus B. Edelsohn filed a 48-page amicus brief on August 27 in the consolidated cases Viramontes v. Cook County (No. 25-238, 7th Cir.) and Grant v. Higgins (No. 25-566, 2d Cir.), arguing that because the prefatory clause names a well-regulated militia as necessary to a free state, an arm’s militia usefulness is “an affirmative reason for protecting it” rather than a reason to ban it. Separately, former Virginia Attorney General Ken Cuccinelli is drafting an amicus on the same theory for a coalition, expected to be filed by September 4; his framing is that the Court “has never squarely explained how the Second Amendment’s Militia Clause informs” the operative right, while United States v. Miller, 307 U.S. 174 (1939), “plainly connected the Amendment to the militia.” Here is the part a marketing team should not miss: AmmoLand’s own editor criticised the Edelsohn brief in print for conceding the “general consensus” that automatic weapons are unprotected, arguing the text contains “no cyclic-rate test.” A public disagreement inside the movement about how far the theory should reach is a real feature of this record, and coverage that presents a single unified 2A position on Viramontes would be inaccurate.

Fourth: the New Jersey decision is now on this board with its actual numbers, and they are the most useful figures produced all week. AmmoLand reports the en banc Third Circuit held 10–5 on July 17, in Cheeseman v. New Jersey, that New Jersey’s bans on commonly owned semiautomatic rifles and standard-capacity magazines violate the Second Amendment — majority opinion by Judge Arianna Freeman, a Biden appointee, applying Bruen, and citing roughly 24 million AR-15-type rifles and more than 100 million 30-round magazines. On August 4 the court stayed its own mandate while New Jersey prepares a Supreme Court petition, so the bans remain enforceable today. That last sentence is the one members will get wrong: a win on the merits that is stayed changes nothing about what is legal in New Jersey this morning.

Congress did produce one thing during recess, and it is now attributable: a House committee chairman asked NIST whether microstamping works, and NIST answered in writing

The microstamping item moves from “single-outlet, unread” to attributable and quotable. The review was produced at the request of House Science, Space, and Technology Committee Chairman Brian Babin (R-Texas), and the National Institute of Standards and Technology — an agency of the Department of Commerce — responded in writing: “There is significant disagreement regarding the viability of this technology.” The response was reported by the National Shooting Sports Foundation and AmmoLand links the letter as a PDF. Substance: NIST flagged firearm design, ammunition type, primer coatings, residue, firing-pin movement, wear, repeated firing and maintenance as factors affecting mark transfer, said the available studies are too limited to show viability across firearms and ammunition in actual use, and noted there have been no adequate large-scale independent blind studies. Two candid admissions worth quoting carefully: NIST did not conduct its own laboratory testing and maintains no microstamping research program; and it acknowledged that firing pins can be replaced, engraving damaged, and parts swapped, with no realistic mechanical means of preventing those modifications. nist.gov remains unreachable directly, but the finding is now sourced to a named requester and a quoted sentence rather than to a headline.

RTB angleThis is now publishable, and it was not yesterday. The precise formulation is: a federal standards agency told a House committee chairman that the research literature does not establish microstamping reliability, and said it has not tested the technology itself. That second half is essential — leaving it out would overstate the finding, and including it is what makes the piece credible. The durable frame is “what a mandate would actually require, and what the standards agency says we know”, pinned to California’s conditional January 1, 2028 date. Still do not write “NIST found microstamping does not work.”
