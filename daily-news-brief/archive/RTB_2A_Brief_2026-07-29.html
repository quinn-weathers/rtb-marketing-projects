The lead. Montgomery County, Maryland — the state’s most populous jurisdiction — lost in the Maryland Supreme Court in April, when Engage Armament LLC v. Montgomery County held the County exceeded its home-rule authority by applying a 100-yard carry prohibition to state permit holders on public highways. Rather than narrow the scheme, the County Council passed Emergency Bill 23-26E on July 21; County Executive Marc Elrich signed it July 27 and it took effect immediately. Maryland Shall Issue and five permit holders filed Barreto v. Montgomery County in federal district court the same day. The complaint’s central number is the one that will travel: using county land-use data, plaintiffs identify at least 10,000 parcels covering more than 7,900 acres — over 26 percent of the County — that may qualify as “places of public assembly,” before the 100-yard buffers around each are added. The revised ordinance lets a permit holder carry in a vehicle but not step out of it, which the suit calls a right to remain armed inside a moving automobile. Three counts: two under Maryland law (Article XI-A local-law limits and state preemption) and one under the Second Amendment, citing Bruen and Wolford v. Lopez.

The clock that matters this week. New Jersey is at T-2. The Third Circuit’s en banc decision striking the state’s semiautomatic rifle and standard-capacity magazine bans (ANJRPC v. Platkin) takes effect Friday, July 31, and the only guidance dealers hold is still a State Police email. The promised Attorney General compliance guidance has not posted. Colorado’s permit-to-purchase regime begins Saturday, Aug 1 with the Department of Revenue’s governing firearms list still unpublished, and the ATF “engaged in the business” comment window closes Aug 4 — the same day NSSF’s Import/Export Conference opens in Washington.

Two clean wins worth banking. Oregon’s Initiative Petition 28, the “PEACE Act,” was disqualified from the Nov 3 ballot after certifying 104,262 valid signatures against a 117,173 threshold — it would have criminalized regulated hunting and trapping and, with them, the excise-tax and license revenue that supplies roughly 85 percent of Oregon’s wildlife funding. And B&T USA says its expanded Herriman, Utah plant can now build up to 7,000 suppressors a month, having gone from three employees in late 2025 to more than 55 today. Both are empowerment-lane material: participation and manufacturing, not fear.

The Freedom From Unfair Gun Taxes Act moves from background to foreground as state excise-tax bills multiply

S. 1169 (Sen. Jim Risch, R-Idaho) and H.R. 2442 (Reps. Darrell Issa, R-Calif., and Richard Hudson, R-N.C.) would bar states and their political subdivisions from imposing excise taxes on the sale of firearms, ammunition, or components by manufacturers, producers, or importers. The bills are drafted to leave the federal Pittman-Robertson framework untouched — an important detail, because conservation funding is the usual first objection. The reason this is climbing: California already levies an additional 11 percent state excise tax, Colorado imposes 6.5 percent on firearms, precursor parts, and ammunition, and NSSF counts introduced bills in five more states — Maryland, Massachusetts, New York, Virginia, and Washington. Virginia’s HB 919 would impose 11 percent on gross receipts from retail firearm and ammunition sales. Sen. John Cornyn (R-Texas) has signed on as a cosponsor, framing it as protecting the $0 federal suppressor stamp enacted in the Working Families Tax Cuts Act from being offset by state-level surcharges. (Bill numbers and cosponsor counts are from NSSF’s summary; congress.gov was not reachable this run — verify status before publishing.)

RTB angle: This is the cleanest “cost of exercising a right” story available, and it lands squarely on the members who feel it most: first-time buyers and working families. The strongest asset is a simple state-by-state table of what a box of ammunition and a $600 handgun actually cost after each state’s stack of taxes and fees. Use the poll-tax analogy carefully and attribute it — it is an argument advocates make, not a holding. Note honestly that opponents frame these taxes as offsetting public-safety costs, and that the federal preemption question here is genuinely contested.

New ▲▲ State · Virginia · Litigation Heat68▲▲AmmoLand opinion column · motion text not independently retrieved

Virginia’s Attorney General defends the state ban by arguing Virginia’s own arms provision is a collective, militia-tethered right

In a motion to dismiss and demurrer filed July 15 in Crump v. Katz — one of five challenges to Virginia’s SB 749 firearm and magazine ban — Attorney General Jay Jones argues first that no plaintiff has standing, and then that “federal Second Amendment doctrine does not control the meaning of § 13” because “marked textual differences” between Article I § 13 of the Virginia Constitution and the Second Amendment mean federal interpretations “inform, but do not necessarily govern” the state provision. The motion then reads § 13’s text and ratification history as establishing a collective guarantee tied to militia service rather than a freestanding individual right. Two things are worth knowing before repeating any characterization of this: the operative clause of § 13 reads “the right of the people to keep and bear arms shall not be infringed,” and the Supreme Court in Wolford v. Lopez rejected Hawaii’s parallel attempt to read local tradition into the scope of the right, writing that the Second Amendment “has the same meaning in all parts of the United States.” Because the Second Amendment binds Virginia through the Fourteenth under McDonald, a narrower state-constitutional reading does not lower the federal floor. (Sourced to an AmmoLand opinion column that quotes the filing; the motion itself was not independently retrieved. Note also that the column’s lead plaintiff is that outlet’s own contributor — read it as advocacy and verify quotations against the docket.)

RTB angle: Explain, do not dunk. The useful member takeaway is structural and reassuring: state constitutions can add protection but cannot subtract it, because incorporation sets a floor. That is a genuinely empowering fact and most people do not know it. A short “how state and federal gun-rights protections stack” explainer would serve members in every state, not just Virginia. Pull the actual motion before quoting it, and keep the tone measured — the argument is unusual, and letting readers see it plainly is more persuasive than mocking it.

Oregon’s anti-hunting ballot initiative fails to qualify — and the reason it mattered was the funding, not just the hunting

Initiative Petition 28, styled the “People for the Elimination of Animal Cruelty Exemptions (PEACE) Act,” was disqualified by the Oregon Secretary of State’s Elections Division from the Nov 3, 2026 ballot after certifying 104,262 valid signatures against a 117,173 requirement. The measure would have removed the legal exemptions that make regulated hunting, trapping, fishing, and livestock production lawful — effectively criminalizing them — while Oregon’s existing animal-cruelty statutes already stand. The structural stake, and the reason NSSF opposed it in the industry’s own interest, is the conservation-funding chain: roughly 85 percent of Oregon’s wildlife money comes from the combination of federal excise taxes paid by firearm and ammunition manufacturers and hunting license, permit, and stamp fees. Of the nearly $1.3 billion the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service apportioned to state wildlife agencies last year, more than $804 million came from the Pittman-Robertson excise tax; Oregon received over $9.7 million.

RTB angle: This is the empowerment lane at its best and the frame RTB should own: gun owners and hunters are the people who actually pay for American conservation, and the number is public and verifiable. A short explainer — “every box of ammunition you buy funds wildlife restoration, here is exactly how” — reaches an audience well outside the usual 2A conversation and requires no adversary. Report the outcome as a signature-threshold failure, which is what it was; do not overstate it as a popular vote against the measure.

New ▲▲▲ Courts · D. Md. Heat78▲▲▲AmmoLand · MSI confirmed filing on X 07/27

Barreto v. Montgomery County: a 100-yard carry ban covering more than a quarter of the county, challenged the day it took effect

Maryland Shall Issue and five Maryland carry-permit holders filed in federal district court on July 27, hours after County Executive Marc Elrich signed Emergency Bill 23-26E, which the County Council passed July 21 and which took effect immediately. The County’s stated purpose was compliance with the Maryland Supreme Court’s April decision in Engage Armament LLC v. Montgomery County, which held the County exceeded its home-rule authority by applying its 100-yard prohibition to state permit holders traveling on public highways. The rewrite adds a motor-vehicle exception but, plaintiffs argue, preserves substantially the same burden. Code § 57-11 bars possessing or transporting firearms, ammunition, or major components within 100 yards of a “place of public assembly,” and Bill 23-26 defines that to include publicly or privately owned parks, places of worship, schools, libraries, recreational facilities, fairgrounds, conference centers, certain government buildings, polling places, courthouses, and legislative assemblies — plus associated grounds and parking lots. Using county land-use data, plaintiffs identify at least 10,000 parcels covering more than 7,900 acres, over 26 percent of the County, before buffers. The complaint alleges no meaningful mens rea requirement, making violations strict liability, with up to six months in jail and a $1,000 fine, and each continuing day treated as a separate offense. Three counts: not a valid “local law” under Article XI-A of the Maryland Constitution; preempted by state law; and unconstitutional under Bruen and Wolford v. Lopez. Individual plaintiffs include a man whose home sits within 100 yards of small public parcels that may qualify as parks — the ordinance’s exception covers the “home” rather than the property, so he argues he cannot lawfully walk armed to his own vehicle — and a congregant who carries at his church with leadership’s permission. MSI says its members include synagogue security volunteers carrying at rabbis’ specific request. Relief sought: declaratory and injunctive, plus nominal and compensatory damages and fees.

RTB angle: This is the strongest teaching case of the week for what RTB actually sells: legal exposure that a careful, law-abiding person cannot avoid through care alone. There are no painted lines marking a 100-yard buffer, and the complaint alleges strict liability — meaning good faith is not a defense. That is precisely the scenario members buy coverage for, and it can be said plainly without fear-mongering: here is the ordinance, here is the map, here is what happens if you get it wrong. Pair it with a practical “how to research carry restrictions where you live” guide so the piece leaves readers more capable, not just more worried. Maryland members should be told the ordinance is in effect now and that litigation does not suspend it.

Moved ▼ Courts · 5th Cir. en banc Heat84▼The Reload · petition and dissent quoted, CourtListener-linked

U.S. v. Squire, Day 9: still no argument date on the Commerce Clause challenge to the felon-in-possession ban

The Fifth Circuit voted on Monday, July 20 to rehear United States v. Squire en banc, after a three-judge panel rejected the challenge on June 2. What distinguishes it is the theory: Squire’s lawyers set the Second Amendment aside entirely and attack § 922(g)(1) under the Commerce Clause, arguing that the only federal hook was that a firearm possessed in a Louisiana home “happened to have been manufactured outside the state” and had at some unknown earlier point traveled in interstate commerce. A Second Amendment win frees one defendant; a Commerce Clause win could invalidate the felon-in-possession ban on its face. Every appellate court to date has upheld § 922 against Commerce Clause challenges. The court is taking up an invitation Justice Thomas issued in his solo concurrence in United States v. Hemani, the unanimous June 2026 decision by Justice Gorsuch, where Thomas wrote that the prohibited-persons categories “appear[] to exceed Congress’s enumerated power to regulate interstate commerce” and that “the Court, and lower courts, should revisit the constitutionality of § 922(g).” Judges Ho, Smith, Engelhardt, Willett, and Duncan have all previously written in this vein in non-precedential opinions. Judge Higginson, the lone recorded dissenter from the en banc grant, warned it would “imperil much of the United States Code,” asking what becomes of federal statutes criminalizing possession of child pornography, explosive materials, or biological weapons. Nine days on: no argument date, no vote breakdown published.

RTB angle: Explainer only, and precision matters more here than anywhere else on this board. The correct framing is that a federal appellate court agreed to reconsider whether Congress had the constitutional authority to enact a specific gun law — not that felons are about to be able to buy guns, and not that anything has changed for anyone today. Higginson’s dissent is the honest way to show the stakes to a general audience, and quoting it demonstrates the evenhandedness that distinguishes RTB from advocacy shops. No predictions. No timeline claims.

Suppressor export jurisdiction moves off the Munitions List — effective Nov 20, with the first industry working session Aug 4

Two coordinated interim final rules move export jurisdiction over suppressors for non-automatic and semiautomatic rifles, pistols, and shotguns from the State Department’s U.S. Munitions List to the Commerce Control List, ending their treatment as ITAR defense articles. Most provisions take effect November 20, 2026. The American Suppressor Association, which pushed the change for over a decade, says it opens established foreign commercial markets to U.S. manufacturers; the rules also make qualifying suppressors eligible for Commerce’s Baggage license exception, which is the provision that matters to Americans traveling abroad to hunt. The framework changed, not the requirement: exports still need Commerce authorization, and country and end-user screening still applies. The next concrete beat is NSSF’s Annual Import/Export Conference, Aug 4–6 in Washington, which is now the first venue where manufacturers and exporters work through the transition in detail. (Rule specifics remain AmmoLand/ASA-sourced; federalregister.gov was not reachable this run and the rule text has not been independently verified.)

RTB angle: A positive, concrete, non-litigation story in a category that has been nothing but countdowns — and it has a nearly four-month runway before the effective date, which makes it ideal evergreen content. The consumer hook is the Baggage exception: taking a suppressed hunting rifle overseas has been a paperwork nightmare, and this changes the framework. Be careful not to imply it removes any requirement. Retrieve the Federal Register citations before publishing anything with a date or a rule number in it.

The real fight in Viramontes/Grant is over methodology — and the “common use” test is now being questioned from both directions

The Supreme Court granted certiorari on June 30, 2026 in Viramontes v. Cook County (No. 25-238, 7th Cir.) and consolidated it with Grant v. Higgins (No. 25-566, 2d Cir.) for one hour of argument, taking up whether ordinances and statutes banning AR-15s and similar semiautomatic rifles survive the Second Amendment. Two commentary pieces this week sharpened what the briefing will actually contest. Attorney Mark Smith’s roadmap flags five things to watch: mootness risk (low — Viramontes pleaded nominal damages, closing the escape hatch that ended NYSRPA v. City of New York in 2020); whether Solicitor General D. John Sauer files in support of the challengers; which amici the Justices actually read closely; and the tradeoff between a 9-0 result that might soften Heller–Bruen methodology and a 6-3 that keeps it intact. Separately, longtime plaintiff Charles Nichols argues the common-use test rests on weak originalist ground — traced to a passing line in United States v. Miller (1939), a case argued with no brief or appearance from the gun owner’s side — and that making constitutional protection depend on how many Americans already own an arm is circular: a ban keeps an arm uncommon, and the government then cites its uncommonness to defend the ban. Smith’s argument projection of the first week of December, with a decision no earlier than late June 2027, is his own docket-sequence estimate; the Court has published no argument date.

RTB angle: Most coverage this fall will ask “will the Court strike the bans down.” The more useful question for members, and the more durable content, is “what standard will the Court use, and what does that mean for the next ten cases” — because that is what determines whether magazine bans, suppressor rules, and under-21 restrictions rise or fall afterward. A short, honest explainer on what “in common use” means, where it came from, and why serious people on the pro-2A side disagree about it would be unusually differentiated. Label every date as a projection. Do not predict outcomes.

De-banking flares again — and this time a governor, an attorney general, a state statute, and a federal rule are all already in place

The federal backdrop has also shifted since the last round of de-banking fights: a 2025 executive order on fair banking, and an OCC/FDIC final rule eliminating “reputational risk” from supervisory programs, which bars the agencies from requiring, instructing, or encouraging institutions to close accounts based on lawful but politically disfavored business activity. Nearly $1.3 billion to state wildlife agencies — more than $804 million of it paid by firearm and ammunition makers

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s latest apportionment sends nearly $1.3 billion to state conservation and wildlife-access programs, derived from Pittman-Robertson excise taxes paid by firearm and ammunition manufacturers and Dingell-Johnson excise taxes paid by the fishing-tackle and boating industries. Three defensive-use cases now resolved or unresolved in different ways — and the differences are the lesson

Three cases carried across recent runs have reached different postures, which is exactly why they are useful together rather than separately. In Springfield, Massachusetts, a legally armed woman fired a single shot after an armed man allegedly pursued her husband at their home; prosecutors ruled the shooting lawful, with video, witness testimony, 911 recordings, and physical evidence supporting her account. In Gastonia, North Carolina, a 74-year-old homeowner fired once at an alleged repeat burglar forcing a window; police say he will not face charges. In Phoenix, Arizona, a resident reportedly intervened during an ongoing knife attack in a neighboring apartment, shooting a man police say was stabbing two women — and that legal outcome remains unresolved. The pattern is not that armed defense works; it is that the evidentiary record and the relationship between the defender and the threat drive what happens afterward. Massachusetts had corroboration from four independent sources. The Phoenix case involves intervention on behalf of third parties in another dwelling, which is the hardest category in almost every state’s law.

RTB angle: This is the core of what RTB actually does, and the honest version is more persuasive than a highlight reel: lawful self-defense is frequently followed by a legal process, and how that process goes depends heavily on evidence and on the specific legal posture of the encounter. Third-party defense is genuinely more legally complex than defending yourself or your household, and members deserve to know that before they need to know it. Factual sequence only, no celebration, no speculation about the Phoenix outcome, and no naming of private individuals beyond what local reporting has published.
