Virginia quietly ended its own AR-15 ban fight: the Attorney General’s office told plaintiffs in writing it will not appeal the statewide injunction.

The lead. Two things happened in Virginia and the second one matters more than the first. On Wednesday, Washington County Circuit Judge Jeffrey L. Campbell issued a stay in Santolla v. Katz holding that his statewide preliminary injunction stays in force — the “Firearms Ban and Magazine Ban are not enforceable” — until no later than 60 days after the Supreme Court decides Viramontes and Grant. US District Judge Leonie Brinkema entered a parallel stay in the federal McDonald v. Katz case. That much was known Friday. What is new is why the state agreed. A July 20 filing in the sister case Crump v. Katz reproduces an email from Gretchen Nygaard, Virginia’s Deputy Attorney General for civil litigation, telling opposing counsel: “we have made this same representation to all Plaintiffs in these pending cases—including that we will not be appealing the Washington County injunction during the pendency of any stay.” State senator Bill Stanley, who represents the Santolla plaintiffs, confirmed publicly that the plaintiffs agreed to the stay only once the AG accepted statewide scope. Attorney General Jay Jones has not answered repeated press questions about litigation plans, and his office is still formally defending the law’s constitutionality — but on the ground, the Commonwealth has stopped trying to switch enforcement back on.

Why that is a bigger deal than a stay. A stay pauses a case; a written commitment not to appeal removes the mechanism by which enforcement could return before the Supreme Court speaks. The Reload’s read is that if you believe SCOTUS is likely to strike AR-15 bans — and nearly every analyst tracking Viramontes does — then Virginia’s ban is practically finished. Two caveats deserve to travel with that: plaintiffs could still lose on the merits in Virginia’s appellate courts, and the Crump plaintiffs are separately asking Judge Martin for an order protecting anyone who buys a covered firearm during the injunction period, because “it is far from clear whether a temporary injunction protects against future enforcement for past conduct.” Government counsel, per that filing, refused to disavow future prosecutions. Present-tense legality and retroactive safety are not the same thing.

New ▲▲ Courts · Under-21 · Circuit split Heat74▲▲Reload analysis 07/31 · opinion linked

The under-21 fight now has a complete map — six appellate courts, four rationales, and no way for the Supreme Court to keep ignoring it forever

The Reload’s July 31 analysis of Succow v. Blanche is the most useful single document on young-adult gun rights currently available, because it lays the incompatible reasoning side by side. Judge Sarala Nagala’s 76-page merits opinion upheld the federal statutes barring licensed dealers from selling handguns to adults under 21, and Connecticut’s broader laws banning all pistol sales to that age group including private transfers, and Connecticut’s ban on 18-to-20-year-olds even possessing or carrying a lawfully obtained handgun. Nagala herself noted no circuit has addressed a state law prohibiting both sale and possession/carry. The split she stepped into: the Fifth Circuit struck the federal purchase ban down in Reese v. ATF (DOJ declined to appeal); a divided Fourth Circuit upheld it in McCoy v. ATF on founding-era “infancy doctrine” grounds; the en banc Eleventh upheld Florida’s sales ban; a Tenth Circuit panel upheld Colorado’s by holding commercial purchase is not covered by the Second Amendment at all; and the Third and Eighth Circuits struck down carry bans in Pennsylvania and Minnesota, with Judge Benton noting in Worth v. Jacobson that “a mandate to acquire a firearm is hardly ‘evidence’ that one was previously prohibited from owning one.” Nagala’s ruling is not precedential but is an obvious Second Circuit appeal candidate.

RTB angle: This is explainer territory where RTB can be genuinely more useful than anyone else, because the practical question a 19-year-old member asks is simple — “what can I legally do where I live?” — and the honest answer is that it depends on the circuit and is actively changing. A clean state-and-circuit reference table with as-of dates is the asset. Keep the tone factual and avoid framing 18-to-20-year-olds adversarially in either direction; the strongest version explains the competing historical arguments fairly and lets members see why courts disagree.

New ▲ National · Legal theory Reload podcast 08/02–08/03

A pro-gun critique of Bruen from the right: Ilya Somin argues the test is “too flabby” and could cut against gun owners

The Reload’s podcast this week features Cato Institute scholar and George Mason law professor Ilya Somin making an argument that is easy to miss because it does not fit either team’s script. Somin is pro-gun and largely agrees with the outcomes of recent Supreme Court Second Amendment cases — but he contends the history-and-tradition test is “too flabby,” creates large grey areas, and leaves too much room for judges to import their own preferences. Where left-leaning critics worry Bruen will be used to strike down too many regulations, Somin worries the same open-endedness will eventually be used to restrict Second Amendment rights — particularly if the Court’s balance shifts. He argues instead for a test grounded in the original meaning of the text, with history informing that meaning rather than historical laws overriding it. Public release is Monday; members got it Sunday.

RTB angle: This is unusually good material precisely because it is not a talking point. A short piece on “why some gun-rights lawyers are uneasy about the test that keeps winning” signals that RTB reads the debate rather than just cheering the scoreboard, and it builds the intellectual credibility that makes members trust the practical guidance. Present it as one serious view among several, name the counterarguments, and do not use it to imply recent wins are fragile — that is not what he said.

New ▲▲ Politics · Polling Heat68▲▲Reuters/Ipsos + Economist/YouGov · toplines linked

Two new polls put guns in their actual electoral place: Republicans hold a small edge, and almost nobody is voting on it

A Reuters/Ipsos poll released July 27 found 35 percent of Americans say the GOP has a better plan for handling “gun violence and gun control” against 32 percent for Democrats — widening to 39/34 among registered voters. The internals explain the gap: 79 percent of Republicans back their own party’s approach versus 74 percent of Democrats, and independents split evenly while being the most likely to pick neither. The same day, an Economist/YouGov poll found just 2 percent of American adults name guns their most important issue — identical to Quinnipiac and Economist/YouGov readings in May. Reuters/Ipsos listed fourteen issues concerning Americans; guns was not among them. The top concerns were the economy and unemployment (17 percent), threats to democratic values (14), and corruption (11); Economist/YouGov had inflation first at 27 percent. Eugene Volokh, quoted by The Reload, noted crime itself polls only a point above guns: “If Americans aren’t that worried about crime, it’s hard to see why they’d be that worried about guns in particular.” Reuters/Ipsos surveyed 1,248 adults July 24–26 (±2.9 points, ±3.2 among registered voters); Economist/YouGov surveyed 2,086 adults July 25–27 (±3.3).

RTB angle: This is the frame to keep in mind for every piece of political content between now and November: the gun issue is unlikely to decide the national result, but it can move races settled by thin margins. For RTB the more useful implication is about audience. Low salience means most people are not looking for an argument about guns — they are looking for practical answers when something in their own life changes, which is exactly the Colorado and New Jersey situation. Content that helps rather than persuades meets people where the polling says they actually are. Present the numbers evenhandedly, with margins of error and field dates, and avoid overreading a three-point gap.
