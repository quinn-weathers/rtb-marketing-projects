ATF has stopped requiring federal approval and registration for suppressors, short-barreled rifles and shotguns, and “any other weapons,” for everyone, nationwide. The member question has changed from “am I covered?” to “what does my state say, and what if this changes?”

What happened. On Friday 9 October, ATF Director Robert Cekada issued an open letter and ATF published an FAQ. Per The Reload (read in full), which quotes the letter: effective 9 October, ATF will no longer require approval before making or transferring short-barreled rifles, short-barreled shotguns, silencers and “any other weapons,” and will no longer require them to be registered in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record. The court injunction covered only plaintiffs and certain others, but the Department chose to use its enforcement discretion so the policy applies “uniformly to anyone otherwise subject to these requirements.” The Second Amendment Foundation (read) confirms the scope: only items on which Congress eliminated the $200 tax.

What has not changed. Machine guns and destructive devices remain fully under the NFA. Dealers still pay the special occupational tax. Short-barreled rifles and shotguns still need ATF approval (Form 5320.20) for interstate transport under the Gun Control Act. State law is untouched: many states ban these items or allow them only if federally registered, and ATF warns that the federal travel safe harbor may not protect unregistered items. ATF will still accept voluntary registration for people who need it to satisfy their state. And it is guidance, not law: ATF’s FAQ says enforcement could resume if Congress reimposes a tax, the injunction is overturned, or the Department rescinds the policy.

A correction to Friday’s framing. Friday’s lead said the ruling covered only some buyers and DOJ was arguing against widening it. Both were true in court: DOJ’s Missouri brief argued against wider court-ordered relief. Within hours, the agency extended non-enforcement to everyone as a matter of policy. That’s a different tool, and easier to reverse.

Why it matters for RTB. This is the biggest practical change for gun owners this year, and it lands on a buyer wave (suppressor sales passed 1.1 million in 2026 before this). The empowering message is know the rules in your state before you buy, build or travel. A plain-English “What changed on 10/9, what didn’t, and three things to check in your state” explainer is timely. None of the four peer sites we checked has published anything on it yet. Avoid “registration is dead” headlines: the policy can be reversed, and state law still applies. Not legal advice; point members to state-specific guidance.

New ▲ House · elections · open carry reported 10/10 · 1 outlet · bill text not read

A House bill would presume voter intimidation when someone carries a visible gun around voters or election workers

Per TTAG (10/10, read), a reintroduced House bill would amend the civil enforcement section of the Voting Rights Act. In a civil intimidation suit, anyone who displayed or carried a visible firearm, imitation firearm or antique firearm while interacting with or observing voters or election workers would be presumed to have violated the law unless they proved otherwise by a preponderance of the evidence. Courts could also impose temporary carry limits on defendants. It creates no new crime and has no distance-from-polls trigger. TTAG reports one co-sponsor and little chance of movement this Congress. The bill text and its number were not checked against Congress.gov.

RTB angleA timely, nonpartisan explainer topic three weeks before Election Day. Carrying on Election Day: what your state allows at polling places, and why polling places in schools and churches may be off-limits regardless. Keep it about state rules, not the bill’s politics. We don’t name sponsors.

Gun Owners of America sues Virginia over its new ban on handgun and “assault firearm” purchases by adults under 21

Per TTAG (10/9, read), Gun Owners Foundation and Gun Owners of America, with an 18-year-old and a 19-year-old plaintiff, challenge two Virginia laws enacted last session: one bars 18-to-20-year-olds from acquiring handguns and “assault firearms” from any source; the other requires background checks on private sales, which TTAG says under-21 buyers can’t pass for handguns because federal law blocks the check. 03ATF, courts & regulatory

Agency actions, litigation, the Supreme Court
Moved ▶ ATF · National Firearms Act · enforcement guidance Heat94▲▲▲ open letter + FAQ 10/9 · ~40 US outlets · effective 10/9

ATF stops requiring NFA approval and registration for suppressors, short-barreled rifles and shotguns, and AOWs, for everyone

See Top of mind. The detail that matters for compliance, per ATF’s letter as quoted by The Reload (read): no prior approval to make or transfer; no registration; no special markings; licensed dealers needn’t register the items either. Unregistered silencers may cross state lines under federal law, but SBRs and SBSs still need ATF approval for interstate transport under the Gun Control Act. Dealers can’t transfer these items where state or local law forbids it. Machine guns and destructive devices are unchanged. The ruling behind it is Judge James Wesley Hendrix’s August injunction in the Northern District of Texas (The Reload calls the case Silencer Shop v. ATF; SAF and the NRA cite Jensen v. ATF; we have not reconciled the case names). SAF says its Brown and Roberts challenges continue because guidance “cannot… provide a permanent fix.” The 22 October status conference in Brown v. ATF (E.D. Mo.) still stands as far as reported. Everytown criticized the ATF director (Breitbart headline); CBS News and AP-syndicated outlets covered it straight.

RTB angleLead with “what to check,” not “what’s legal now.” Federal paperwork is paused for four item types. Your state’s law, your travel route, and the chance the policy reverses are what to check before you buy. Suggested companion: “Keep your receipts and records” (ATF’s own FAQ warns enforcement could resume).

Moved ▶ National parks · Zimmerman v. Blanche Heat61▲ DOJ motion 10/2 · 2 outlets · response due ~10/23

DOJ tells a court that self-defense carry in national park buildings is lawful, but the Park Service’s own guidance still says no

Per TTAG (10/9, read): in Zimmerman v. Blanche (N.D. Tex.), DOJ’s 2 October motion to dismiss argues the federal-facility gun ban (18 U.S.C. § 930(a)) doesn’t reach licensed self-defense carry into park visitor centers and ranger stations, relying on the August OLC opinion about post offices. On that basis DOJ says the plaintiffs face no realistic prosecution and lack standing. TTAG notes NPS public guidance still bans firearms in those buildings, and an OLC opinion can be withdrawn. Plaintiffs’ response is due around 23 October. We did not re-check the NPS page this run; the “signs unchanged” point is TTAG’s.

RTB angleNot safe to tell members it’s allowed. A prosecutor’s position isn’t a court order, and the posted rule still says no. Good example for a “guidance vs. law” piece alongside the NFA lead.
