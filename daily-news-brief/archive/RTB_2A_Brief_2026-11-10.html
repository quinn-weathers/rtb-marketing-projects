Massachusetts keeps its 2024 gun law (Question 9 passes), Arkansas approves its right-to-bear-arms amendment, Missouri’s sheriff measure narrowly passes

Carried from 11/4; these sources were not re-read on 11/5, and results remain unofficial. Massachusetts Question 9: voters approved it, rejecting the repeal, per the Associated Press result carried by WBUR (read). Yahoo News (read) reports 68.6% yes (unofficial; one outlet, WBUR carried no figure). The 2024 law, as Yahoo lists it, requires nearly all guns to be registered, serializes and registers homemade “ghost guns,” bans a broader range of assault-style weapons and rapid-fire trigger activators, bars guns from polling places and some government buildings, raises the minimum age for most licenses from 18 to 21 with added training, lets certain health-care providers and school officials ask a judge for a red-flag order, and adds dealer inspections. Arkansas Issue 2: approved in early unofficial results (KAIT, read; no percentage); it would establish the right to keep and bear arms as “a natural, fundamental, and individual right that shall not be infringed.” Effective date not found. Missouri Amendment 8: just under 51% yes, unofficial (KOMU, read). It writes elected county sheriffs into the constitution and lets only the attorney general start a removal; it is a sheriff-governance measure, not a gun law, and the effective date is not stated. Moved ▶ Oregon · Portland · city buildings City release read 11/2 · 11 outlets

Portland’s own release: licensed carry ends in City Hall, the Portland Building and the 1900 Building once signs are posted

The City’s 29 October release (read) says the Council voted 12–0 on 28 October to end the exception that let concealed-handgun license holders carry into buildings the City uses for official meetings, naming City Hall, the Portland Building and the 1900 Building. It cites Senate Bill 243, effective 26 September 2025, which lets local governments end the license-holder exception in their meeting buildings (Multnomah County did so in September 2025). Not yet in force: as SB 243 requires, no designation takes effect until signs are posted at every normal entrance and the City publishes a list of covered buildings. The City Administrator will set a “progressive enforcement” procedure (notice, a chance to cure, exclusion from the building) before any police referral; the release says the ordinance creates no new offenses or penalties. Weekend commentary: Seattle Red (11/2), PJ Media (10/30), the Oregon Firearms Federation.

RTB angleThe “know before you go” piece now has its facts. Look for posted signs at government buildings; a license is not a pass into every building; Oregon law now lets more local bodies opt in. The “notice and a chance to cure” detail is useful and calming. No position on the ordinance. Not legal advice.

ATF’s chief counsel resigns; The Reload expects most of the 34-rule package to survive, but not the contested no-visit sales rule

It judges a sharp policy shift unlikely, expects the rule package and lawsuits to probably continue, but says they could fall short or fade without their champions. Single source; not checked at DOJ (not fetched). ATF still names no chief-counsel successor in anything read. ATF announced on 30 October that Chief Counsel Robert Leider has resigned and is expected to return to the Antonin Scalia Law School at George Mason University. The release says he joined in early 2025 and supported “more than 40 proposed and final rules” (read). The Reload’s analysis (11/1, read) describes him as the architect of the 34-rule package announced in May, still moving through rulemaking. It reports public statements from both sides describing an amicable split, but says it could not confirm the full story. Its assessment: most rules will likely be finalized because they are modest and durable; the proposal most at risk is “Revising Non-Over-the-Counter Firearms Transaction Requirements,” which would let dealers complete some sales without an in-store visit using approved online identity checks, and which drew heavy opposition from small dealers and NSSF. A rumored broader “sporting purposes” import rule, it says, probably will not come. The Truth About Guns, AmmoLand and Guns.com also reported the departure. Added 11/3: CBS News (10/30, read) reports, citing a source, that he remains an ATF employee through January but is on administrative leave from Monday 11/2; that a source says the decision was not related to DOJ’s choice not to appeal a Texas court ruling on the National Firearms Act; that earlier reports said he did not always agree with DOJ leadership on legal positions in pro-gun groups’ suits; and that the article does not say what happens to the proposals. No successor named. Update 11/4: ATF’s own news page (read) lists “ATF’s chief counsel will return to George Mason University” (30 October), consistent with the departure; no successor named and no new rule on the page through 11/3. New ▲ Sixth Circuit · §922(g)(4) · mental-health prohibition decided 10/29 · reported 11/5 · The Reload read · one outlet

Sixth Circuit rejects a Second Amendment challenge to the federal ban on gun possession by people found mentally ill and dangerous

In United States v. Laykovich (appeal from the Eastern District of Kentucky), a three-judge panel affirmed on 29 October that 18 U.S.C. §922(g)(4), which bars firearm possession by people adjudicated mentally defective or committed to a mental-health institution, does not violate the Second Amendment, at least as applied to the defendant, per The Reload (read, 11/5). The panel reasoned that the law resembles historical laws allowing the government to detain people whose mental illness made them dangerous, serves the same public-safety purpose, and lasts only until the person no longer poses a threat. The defendant had also challenged the federal drug-user prohibition, §922(g)(3); the article summary does not state how that challenge came out. According to the article the defendant was held involuntarily in 2023 and later found with multiple firearms; the article is internally inconsistent about his home state, so no state is given here. The district court had sentenced him to four years in 2025. Single outlet (The Reload); the opinion itself was not read.

RTB angleA court explaining a limit, not a change in law. The evergreen explainer on who is prohibited from possessing firearms under federal law, and how rights can be restored where a state allows it, fits here. No commentary on the ruling and no forecast of further review. Not legal advice.

October gun sales ran 7.9% above last year, the strongest post-pandemic October behind only 2020 and 2021

An NSSF analysis of FBI NICS data, released Tuesday 3 November and reported by The Reload the same evening (read), puts October 2026 adjusted sales-related checks 7.9% above October 2025 and the strongest post-pandemic October, behind only 2020 and 2021.
