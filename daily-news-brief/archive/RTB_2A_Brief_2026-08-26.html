Top of mind08 · 26

AmmoLand is readable at article level for the first time in weeks, and the first thing it does is correct this board: the “ghost gun” ruling is relief as applied to Defense Distributed, SAF and covered members — it is not a nationwide vacatur. Two new federal-data stories land alongside it and both are unusually useful to a self-defence organisation: NIST found no agreement that microstamping works, and CDC has quietly restored its defensive-gun-use estimates.

The correction first, because it is the one that protects the team. Monday’s edition described Judge Reed O’Connor as having vacated ATF’s frame-and-receiver rule in Defense Distributed v. Blanche. AmmoLand’s report — now readable in full — is more precise, and narrower: the court found the rule unconstitutionally vague and incompatible with the Second Amendment “as applied to Defense Distributed, SAF, and covered members,” and states directly that “the ruling is significant, but it is not nationwide.” That is party-scoped relief, the same architecture as the Silencer Shop injunction — not universal vacatur. The holdings themselves are unchanged and still the clearest post-Bruen teaching case of the year: the rule fails the Fifth Amendment because it “does not provide fair notice to the reader about when a component becomes subject to enforcement,” and fails the Second because it “does not merely lack supporting analogues. It contradicts the actual historical tradition of personal gunsmithing.” A second correction rides along: the Ninth Circuit’s rejection of the California and Giffords bid to expand the same rule was decided on standing — the challengers “lacked standing” — not on the merits, so it says nothing about whether ATF could have gone further. Still unresolved: the ruling date. The Reload says “Monday” (08/17); the SAF-hosted opinion PDF is filenamed 8.19.26; AmmoLand posted 08/23 without a date. Do not put a date in published copy.

Second: a federal standards body has taken the air out of microstamping. AmmoLand reported on 08/25 that the National Institute of Standards and Technology reviewed the microstamping research literature and found significant disagreement over whether the technology can reliably produce repeatable, legible markings — and that the finding arrives as California moves toward a conditional 2028 mandate for semiautomatic pistols. This is a technical-feasibility question with a direct policy consequence, and it is exactly the kind of story a legal-coverage organisation can report without taking a political side: mandates of this kind are premised on the mark being identifiable, and the federal body whose job is measurement standards has now said the literature does not agree that it is. The NIST document is not in this board’s hands and nist.gov is unreachable, so the finding is carried at the level AmmoLand stated it — “significant disagreement” — and should not be characterised more strongly than that.

Third, and it is the one with the most direct line to RTB’s subject matter: CDC has restored its defensive-gun-use estimates. Also 08/25, also AmmoLand: the agency has put the figures back after emails revealed that gun-control advocates pushed for their removal in 2022. The restored range, per AmmoLand’s own presentation, runs from roughly 60,000 to 2.5 million defensive uses a year. That range is enormous, and the temptation will be to quote whichever end suits the argument. The credible move is the opposite one: say plainly that defensive-gun-use estimation is genuinely contested, that the methodologies differ by orders of magnitude, and that a federal agency removing the number under advocacy pressure and then restoring it is a story about process rather than about who is right. Note for planning: USCCA already owns the “why do these numbers vary so widely” explainer, published 08/04. RTB’s open lane is the procedural history, not the statistic.

A NIST literature review finds no agreement that microstamping works reliably — and California is heading toward a conditional 2028 mandate anyway

AmmoLand reported on 08/25 that the National Institute of Standards and Technology reviewed the body of research on firearm microstamping and found significant disagreement over whether the technology can reliably produce repeatable, legible markings. The finding lands, per the same report, as California moves toward a conditional 2028 mandate for semiautomatic pistols. Microstamping is the requirement that a firing pin or breech face imprint a traceable code onto the cartridge case at the moment of firing; the entire policy case for mandating it rests on the mark being legible and repeatable enough to identify a specific firearm. NIST is the federal body whose remit is measurement standards, so a finding of substantial disagreement in the literature is not a partisan claim about the policy — it is a statement about the state of the evidence. This board has not read the NIST document: nist.gov is unreachable, the review’s scope, date and formal conclusions are unknown, and the item is single-outlet at headline-and-dek level. Treat “significant disagreement” as the ceiling of what can be said.

Sensitive-topic notes. The ghost-gun correction is itself the compliance point. Party-scoped constitutional relief is narrower than a vacatur, this judge was overruled on this rule once already, and nothing in the ruling touches state law — several states independently regulate unfinished frames and receivers. Any RTB piece must be explanatory rather than operational. On the CDC defensive-gun-use range: do not lead with 2.5 million. The estimates span roughly a factor of forty because the methodologies differ fundamentally, and quoting the top of a contested range is the fastest way to lose a reader who checks — it is also the fear-and-inflation register RTB exists to be an alternative to. On the NIST microstamping review: “significant disagreement” is the ceiling of what can be said until the document is read. The restoration rule remains the highest-liability item this brief carries — its audience is by definition people with prior convictions or adjudications making consequential decisions about firearm possession, and a sentence read as “you are clear to buy a gun” could put a reader in federal custody. Procedural, never evaluative; explicit “not a law firm, not legal advice, consult counsel in your state” on every piece. On the Virginia State University shooting — an active investigation with shooter and motive undetermined — publish nothing.
