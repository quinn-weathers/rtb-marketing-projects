The ATF breach stopped being a claim and became a document. Qilin’s countdown expired, 6.3GB went up and came back down, the breached system finally has a name — and for the first time this board can answer the question members are actually asking, with a primary source behind it.

Start with what is now established rather than claimed. Qilin’s 72-hour timer on the ATF listing hit zero on Monday, August 31, and the group briefly published approximately 6.3GB of files before pulling them down again. AmmoLand’s John Crump reported the dump on September 1 and identified the compromised environment: the ATF’s Communications Assistance for Law Enforcement Act (CALEA) system — the bureau’s own internal store for material from federally authorised intercepts, described by officials as a legacy standalone platform. ATF published its own update the same day, acknowledging claims that CALEA material had been published, saying it cannot yet confirm the authenticity, nature or scope of that material, and repeating that other operational systems were unaffected. Senior Justice Department officials had already designated the event a “major incident” — a formal label that triggers congressional notification.

This is the paragraph that matters for our copy, and it is good news honestly stated. The named system is not the one members worry about. Per the ATF spokesperson, quoted directly: the breached platform “was not connected to any other ATF systems, including any case management systems, laboratory systems, or eForms systems, and it was quickly shut down when the breach was discovered.” Crump’s reporting adds that out-of-business records were not accessed and that the CALEA environment was not emptied. So the answer to “were my records exposed?” is, on every source available this morning, no — the breached system held wiretap and intercept material about investigative targets, not the forms gun owners file. That is a real, checkable, calming answer, and RTB is in a position to be the outlet that gives it plainly instead of the one that trades on the ambiguity. Six days of this board saying “no registry-exposure headline” has now been rewarded with something better than silence: a specific, sourced reassurance.

Say what is in the leak, too, because vagueness is its own kind of alarm. Gun Owners of America posted publicly on August 31 that the published set included “criminal investigation target names, phone numbers, IP addresses, iCloud data, Cellebrite phone dumps, and more.” Crump’s account describes subjects under investigation for firearms trafficking, arson, explosives, organised crime and illicit tobacco and alcohol, with material spanning roughly ten to fifteen years, and several field offices appearing in the set. The operational harm is real — sources, methods and open cases — and it is separate from the consumer question. Both things are true at once and the copy should hold both: this was a serious breach of a law-enforcement intercept system, and it was not a breach of gun-owner records.

What is still not established, and must not be smoothed over. ATF has not authenticated the files; AmmoLand’s confidence rests on unnamed sources inside the bureau. ATF will not attribute the intrusion to Qilin publicly, will not describe the initial access path, and will not say whether data left before the system was pulled. Whether a ransom was paid is unknown — Crump notes only that Qilin pulling a dump often means payment, and that there is no public evidence the ATF paid. And AmmoLand’s finding that the bureau “maintains an unusually large number of administrators relative to other agencies” is single-sourced and flagged by the outlet itself as the subject of later reporting. Treat that last one as a lead, not a fact.

Second: a second under-21 ruling in twelve days, on the same reasoning, and now it is a pattern rather than an outlier. On September 1 AmmoLand reported that U.S. District Judge Jill A. Otake (D. Haw.) issued a 43-page order in Roache v. Lopez — filed originally as Pinales v. Lopez — denying summary judgment to the plaintiffs, granting Attorney General Anne Lopez’s counter-motion, and directing the clerk to enter judgment for Hawaii and close the case. Plaintiffs were Juda Roache, the Second Amendment Foundation, and dealers Aloha Strategics LLC and JGB Arms LLC. Hawaii’s law is broader than the Massachusetts statute upheld in Escher on August 21: it bars adults aged 18 to 20 from acquiring any firearm by purchase, gift, inheritance or bequest, and restricts ammunition too. And the mechanism is the same one Escher used — the founding-era infancy doctrine from contract law.

The concessions in that order are what make it usable, and they should be quoted rather than characterised. The court rejected Hawaii’s argument that under-21 adults fall outside “the people”: “The Court is satisfied that 18- to 20-year-olds are part of ‘the people,’” Otake wrote, and acknowledged that an acquisition ban implicates the right to keep arms. It conceded the founding-era record on parental gifts was “ambiguous.” It conceded Hawaii’s law sweeps more broadly than the nineteenth-century pistol and concealable-weapon laws it leaned on. It discounted the Militia Act of 1792 — which enrolled able-bodied citizens from age 18 and required them to appear armed — as an obligation rather than evidence of an individual right. And it expressly found the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning in Reese v. ATF less persuasive than Fourth and Eleventh Circuit decisions. The under-21 explainer this board has been recommending for eleven days now has two fresh district-court pegs, opposite circuit results on an identical federal statute, and a court admitting on the record that the historical evidence was ambiguous and ruling for the state anyway. Write it this week.

Third, and this is the best content peg the week produced. The petitioners’ merits brief in Viramontes v. Cook County and Grant v. Higgins has been filed — on behalf of individual owners, the Firearms Policy Coalition, the Second Amendment Foundation and the Connecticut Citizens Defense League — and it is built on William English’s 2026 National Firearms Survey: 51,398 adults surveyed, 16,688 validated gun owners. The findings now in front of the Supreme Court: 30% of American gun owners — roughly 26.4 million adults — currently own at least one rifle targeted by “assault weapon” bans, about 22.1 million own an AR-15-type specifically, and Americans hold approximately 40 million AR-15-type rifles plus 20 million similarly styled semi-automatics — about 60 million in total. Reasons for ownership, and this is the number to build on: home defence 77.7%, recreational target shooting 65.1%, hunting 50.2%, defence outside the home 41.1%, protection against animals 33.8%, competition 25.9%. Combine the two defensive categories and 83% of owners cite a defensive purpose. The brief also reports that 16.4% of the defensive gun uses English documented involved rifles. Cases were granted June 30, consolidated, allotted one hour of argument, and no argument date is yet docketed.

Fourth: a court win that protects exactly two people, which is the fifth worked example the explainer needed. On August 27, U.S. District Judge James O. Browning granted summary judgment to Samuel Ortega and Rebecca Scott in Ortega v. Lujan Grisham, holding New Mexico’s seven-day waiting period unconstitutional — and enjoined enforcement only against those two plaintiffs. Everyone else in New Mexico still waits. Browning was candid about why: “Although the Court continues to believe the Waiting Period Act is constitutional, the Court is bound by the Tenth Circuit’s decision and the law of the case.” The Tenth Circuit’s 2025 holding is the quotable part: “One cannot keep or bear arms if one cannot acquire them. That is an infringement, even if it is not a direct prohibition or ban.” Two dates follow. Browning ordered state officials to notify the New Mexico Department of Public Safety by September 10, after which DPS must post the notice and the order. And on August 25 a cert petition was filed in Beckwith v. Frey, challenging Maine’s 72-hour period, after the First Circuit held acquisition falls outside the Second Amendment’s plain text — a square conflict with the Tenth Circuit. Colorado’s three-day period is being litigated inside the Tenth Circuit, where Ortega binds; Florida’s officials have conceded their three-day law is unconstitutional in an NRA-backed suit. Waiting periods are now a live circuit split with a petition pending.

The competitive layer moved too, and one finding revises something this board said yesterday. Yesterday we wrote that almost every peer price rung below $20 is eligibility-gated by “a referral code, a badge, a permit, or a state.” Add a fourth gate, read at source this morning: a training certificate. FLP’s own join funnel asks “Have you taken a firearms training, safety, or state-approved firearms licensing course within the last 12 months?” and answers “Great! That unlocks a discount,” with a completion-date field. That is a discount for having trained, built into checkout — not a referral deal. It is also the most interesting thing any peer did this week, because it prices a behaviour we actually want members to have. Also newly read at source: FLP publishes a $24/year annual-payment saving and bail-bond premium up to $250,000 on Individual Premium; and its Individual Basic covers only the state of residence while Premium is marketed as “Protection in all 50 states” — which sits against the six states where FLP says memberships are not offered. The reconciliation is almost certainly coverage while travelling versus availability to purchase, and that distinction is exactly the sort of thing a shopper needs explained rather than adjudicated.

Two more peer facts, both new to the board. (1) US LawShield’s state footprint, read off its own selector: 46 states plus the District of Columbia, excluding Alaska, Maine, New York and Rhode Island. Read that against yesterday’s FLP finding and the picture is genuinely useful: US LawShield serves New Jersey, Massachusetts, Hawaii and Oregon — four of the six states FLP does not, while FLP serves Maine and Rhode Island, which US LawShield does not. Availability is not a ranking, it is a map, and no single provider covers the whole country. Its homepage also publishes “more than 2 million people served,” “less than 50 cents a day,” and “zero attorneys’ fees for covered events—no limits, caps or deductibles” — an uncapped structure that contrasts directly with CCW Safe’s $5,000–$10,000 red-flag cap captured yesterday. (2) CCW Safe runs a four-step Plan Advisor — assessment, plan selection, cart, checkout — and its four questions are who is this for, do you have a CCW permit, are you military, a veteran, law enforcement or a citizen, and what state. It asks nothing about training or education. That confirms yesterday’s structural read from the other direction: CCW Safe segments on eligibility, USCCA on education volume, FLP on price. Three different front doors to the same category.

Comeaux, decided June 18, 2026, and it is confirmed independently today by the Second Amendment Foundation’s own Director of Legal Research, Kostas Moros, writing on USCCA’s blog: the Fifth Circuit held that suppressors are “arms,” rejecting the government’s necessity argument on the ground that an item “need not be necessary for a firearm’s functioning but instead must only ‘facilitate armed self-defense,’” creating a split with other circuits. The publication hold lifts. The framing changes from “breaking” to “a June decision that is about to matter,” because California and New York maintain total suppressor bans and Viramontes’s treatment of what counts as an “arm” feeds straight into it.

Not a talking point, not an advocacy estimate: a sourced, dated, citable figure with a docket behind it. It answers the single most common claim made against these rifles, it is empowerment-led by construction, and it has a URL. Use it.

The single new federal fact today is procedural and easy to miss: senior Justice Department officials designated the ATF intrusion a “major incident”, a label that triggers congressional notification. That converts the breach from a news story members read about into something with an existing paper trail on the Hill before either chamber sits again. The rest of the recess inventory is unchanged and got bigger overnight: federal district courts have enjoined core National Firearms Act registration provisions, held ATF’s frame-and-receiver rule unconstitutional as applied to the plaintiffs, vacated ATF’s 2024 “Engaged in the Business” rule, and now held New Mexico’s seven-day waiting period unconstitutional on the merits; DOJ finalised a firearm-rights restoration process Congress did not design; NIST told a House committee chairman in writing that the microstamping evidence is contested; the CDC restored defensive-gun-use estimates; the FBI reported a murder rate tying the 1955–56 historic low; the Third Circuit struck New Jersey’s rifle and magazine bans 10–5 and stayed its own mandate; the Supreme Court has a granted assault-weapons case whose petitioners’ merits brief is now filed; and two federal judges in twelve days upheld under-21 bans on a doctrine borrowed from contract law. New ▲ English 2026 National Firearms Survey 51,398 respondents · in a SCOTUS brief

Eighty-three per cent of AR-15 and similar-rifle owners cite a defensive purpose — from a 51,398-person survey that is now cited in a Supreme Court merits brief

The strongest single piece of publishable data this board has logged this month. William English’s 2026 National Firearms Survey sampled 51,398 American adults and identified 16,688 validated gun owners, and unlike the 2021 edition it asked specifically about firearms respondents personally and currently own — not a spouse’s guns, not guns owned years earlier — and separated AR-15-type rifles from other similarly styled semi-automatics. Ownership: 25% of gun owners own an AR-15-type rifle, 12.9% own a similarly styled semi-auto with a pistol grip, 7.9% own both, for 30% overall — approximately 26.4 million adults owning at least one rifle commonly targeted by “assault weapon” bans, and about 22.1 million owning an AR-15-type specifically. Totals: roughly 40 million AR-15-type rifles plus 20 million similarly styled semi-automatics, about 60 million rifles. Reasons given for ownership: home defence 77.7%, recreational target shooting 65.1%, hunting 50.2%, defence outside the home 41.1%, protection against animal attacks 33.8%, competitive shooting 25.9%. Combining the two defensive categories yields 83% of owners citing a defensive purpose, and the survey found 16.4% of documented defensive gun uses involved rifles. These figures are not sitting in an academic queue: they are cited repeatedly in the petitioners’ merits brief in Viramontes v. Cook County and Grant v. Higgins, filed for individual owners, FPC, SAF and the Connecticut Citizens Defense League, in service of Heller’s holding that arms “in common use” for lawful purposes cannot be banned. Separately and also new, AmmoLand reports a national survey estimating Americans use firearms defensively against animal threats roughly 180,000 times a year — headline-level, not read at source, and almost certainly drawn from the same English work.
