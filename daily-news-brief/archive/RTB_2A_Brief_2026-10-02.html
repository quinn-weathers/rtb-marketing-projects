Top of mind10 · 02

A federal judge has put Connecticut’s “convertible pistol” law on hold the day before it was due to take effect — the first court win against the new “Glock ban” laws. The state says it will keep defending the law.

What happened. On Wednesday 30 September, US District Judge Kari A. Dooley granted a preliminary injunction against Connecticut’s Public Act 26-41 (House Bill 5043), The Reload reports. The law creates a new category of “convertible pistols”: semi-automatic pistols with a cruciform trigger bar that can be illegally converted to automatic fire. It was to take effect on 1 October. The judge found the plaintiffs likely to win on their Second Amendment claims and found that the state’s comparison to founding-era gun “proving” laws did not fit. There are two lawsuits: one brought by Glock, and one by Shadow Systems, the NSSF and Blue Trail Range. The judge did not decide whether Glock’s newer models fall under the law’s definition. Connecticut’s attorney general says the state will keep defending the statute. At least seven outlets covered the ruling.

Two corrections to earlier editions. (1) Johnson v. Jacobson (26-255) is a carry-licence reciprocity case, not an under-21 case. The petition asks whether Americans need a licence from each state to carry across the country, and whether reciprocity laws that don’t recognise every state’s licence are valid under Bruen (Duke tracker, read). The under-21 carry petition at the same conference is Salas-Martinez v. Texas (26-5146). (2) The Justice Department did sue Minnesota on 24 September (DOJ release, read). The 25 September–1 October editions said nothing had been filed. Both errors came from gaps in our sources. Neither is a new event today.

Why it matters for RTB. Connecticut members who own Glock-pattern pistols are asking a simple question, and today it has a factual answer: the law is not being enforced while the injunction stands. That can change on appeal, so present it as the current status, not a final result. The reciprocity correction matters more to members than the error suggested. Carrying across state lines is one of the most common questions members ask. Monday’s order list is the next point where Johnson could move, and the Court has 13 gun petitions listed for its 9 October conference.

Moved ▶ DOJ Civil Rights · US v. Minnesota Heat65▲▲ CORRECTION · complaint filed 9/24

Correction: the Justice Department sued Minnesota, Minneapolis and St. Paul on 24 September over purchase permits and waiting periods

DOJ’s release, read today, says the suit claims Minnesota’s permit-to-purchase and waiting-period scheme violates the Second Amendment for people buying a firearm for self-defense at home. Minnesotans who do not intend to carry face a 30-day wait. The suit also challenges waiting periods in the two cities, which DOJ says run close to 60 days. CBS and the Pioneer Press reported it on 9/25–26. This board’s editions from 25 September to 1 October said nothing had been filed. That was wrong. Our feeds missed the filing; it is not a new event. Separately, US v. San Jose was reportedly expanded on 9/30 to also challenge the city’s gun-owner insurance requirement (AmmoLand, Breitbart; headline only).

RTB angleEvergreen piece: “What a permit-to-purchase law is, and what the wait means for you.” The law is in force while the case proceeds. Nothing is enjoined. Minnesota sits in the Eighth Circuit, so Yukutake doesn’t govern it.
