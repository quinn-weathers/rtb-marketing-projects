A federal judge just struck down most of the National Firearms Act — and gave the Justice Department one week to appeal.

Sourcing status — partial-access run, and this edition covers 48 hours. There was no August 5 edition, so everything below spans Aug 4–6. The Reload was reachable and has published three new pieces since the last run, including the full text of a 66-page opinion analysis. All four competitor sites were reachable and checked directly. The Google News RSS recall layer returned empty on all six queries for the eighth consecutive run — the multi-outlet pickup proxy is unavailable, and local, regional, and defensive-use stories are under-sampled by construction. AmmoLand, NSSF, SCOTUSblog, congress.gov, regulations.gov, atf.gov, justice.gov, the Federal Register, and Ballotpedia were not reachable; anything sourced to them is carried forward and flagged.

The lead is the biggest federal firearms ruling of the year. On Wednesday, August 5, US District Judge James Wesley Hendrix (N.D. Tex., a Trump appointee) issued a permanent injunction against the National Firearms Act's regulatory provisions for untaxed firearms in Silencer Shop v. ATF. The reasoning is narrow and structural rather than Second Amendment: Congress zeroed out the NFA's transfer and making taxes on silencers, short-barrel rifles, short-barrel shotguns, and “any other weapons” in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Judge Hendrix held that because the NFA was enacted solely under the taxing power, and because a tax that collects no revenue is not a tax, the registration and regulatory requirements attached to those now-untaxed items exceed Congress's enumerated powers. He rejected the DOJ's Necessary-and-Proper and Commerce Clause fallbacks, writing that if the government's theory held, “anyone wishing to possess broccoli” could be fingerprinted to police a $1 grocer tax. The plaintiffs abandoned their Second Amendment claims, so the court never reached them.

Read the scope carefully — this is where the marketing risk lives. The injunction runs only to the plaintiffs and, where applicable, “their agencies, political subdivisions, members, and customers—both current and future.” Those plaintiffs include Gun Owners of America, the Firearms Policy Coalition Action Foundation, the Citizens Committee for the Right to Keep and Bear Arms, and the Firearms Regulatory Accountability Coalition. And Judge Hendrix stayed his own ruling for one week to let DOJ appeal — so as of this morning nothing has changed for anyone, and the practical answer to “can I skip the Form 4 now?” is no. DOJ has not said whether it will appeal. The stay lands around Wednesday, August 12. Every asset RTB publishes on this needs the stay, the party-only scope, and the appeal posture in the first two sentences, not the fine print.

Second story: DOJ opened a new front on carry restrictions. On Monday, August 3, the DOJ Civil Rights Division sued Montgomery County, Maryland, over Bill 23-26 — the county's rewritten “gun-free” zone ordinance, which still carries a 100-yard buffer around thousands of locations. Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon argued it is “much more restrictive than the offending law in Wolford,” partly because it allows no property-owner permission carve-out at all. County Executive Marc Elrich responded Wednesday: “We're just not going to surrender.” The suit again rests on Section 12601 of the 1994 Crime Act — the same novel theory a California federal judge has already flagged as untested, which is the honest caveat to include.

What went quiet. The ATF “engaged in the business” comment record closed Tuesday, so that item is now a retrospective and falls out of the top ten. Virginia is unchanged — the Reload's August 2 analysis stands, the Deputy AG's no-appeal commitment is undisturbed, and the two Crump motions on retroactive protection remain pending. New Jersey is on Day 6 with no answer on the rehearing petition and still no compliance guidance.

Moved ▲▲▲ Federal · NFA · OBBBA Heat95▲▲▲#1 · legislative root of the ruling

Congress's own tax cut is now the legal reason the NFA's registry fell — the OBBBA $0 rate is the operative fact in Silencer Shop

The legislative story underneath this week's ruling is worth telling on its own terms. On January 1, 2026, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act's firearms provisions took effect, cutting the NFA transfer and making taxes on silencers, short-barrel rifles, short-barrel shotguns, and “any other weapons” from $200 to $0 — while leaving the registration requirement in place, because the registry repeal could not survive the reconciliation process. Gun-rights groups argued at the time that the tax cut alone was fatal to the rest: “The constitutional foundation on which the NFA rested has dissolved.” Judge Hendrix agreed. He found the NFA was enacted exclusively under the taxing power, that “the essential feature of any tax” is that it produce “at least some revenue,” and that the surviving Special Occupation Tax on manufacturers and dealers has its own separate regulatory scheme and cannot carry the user-side rules. So a provision Congress could not repeal directly was undone by a rate change Congress did pass. (OBBBA effective date and rate figures are from The Reload's reporting and the opinion; congress.gov was not reachable this run.)

RTB angle: This is the single best civics explainer available to RTB right now, and it needs no adversarial framing at all: here is how a tax rate, a reconciliation rule, and an enumerated-powers doctrine combined to produce an outcome nobody voted for directly. It also sets up the honest caveat that protects members — a ruling built on this reasoning is exactly the kind an appellate court can narrow or reverse without touching the Second Amendment, which is why nothing about anyone's paperwork changes today. Lead with the mechanism, close with the stay.

New ▲▲▲ N.D. Tex. · NFA · Enumerated powers Heat95▲▲▲#1 · stayed 7 days · T-6 to Aug 12

Silencer Shop v. ATF: Judge Hendrix permanently enjoins the NFA's regulatory provisions for untaxed firearms — then stays his own ruling for a week

On Wednesday, August 5, US District Judge James Wesley Hendrix (N.D. Tex.) issued a 66-page opinion holding that the National Firearms Act's registration and regulatory requirements for now-untaxed items exceed Congress's enumerated powers. The chain of reasoning: the NFA was enacted solely under the taxing power; “the essential feature of any tax” is that it produce “at least some revenue”; the One Big Beautiful Bill Act zeroed the transfer and making taxes on everything except machineguns and destructive devices; therefore “because today's NFA does not generate any revenue from untaxed firearms, its regulatory provisions cannot be upheld under the taxing power.” He rejected DOJ's three fallbacks in turn — the surviving Special Occupation Tax has “its own regulatory scheme” and the challenged provisions bear “no reasonable relation” to it; the Necessary and Proper Clause “would open the door to limitless federal regulation” (hence the broccoli hypothetical); and the Commerce Clause cannot be invoked retroactively because “Congress relied clearly and exclusively on its taxing power,” unlike the Gun Control Act and its Federal Firearms Act predecessor, which were expressly tied to interstate commerce. Scope, precisely: relief runs only to “the plaintiffs and, where applicable, their agencies, political subdivisions, members, and customers—both current and future” — plaintiffs including Gun Owners of America, the FPC Action Foundation, the CCRKBA, and the Firearms Regulatory Accountability Coalition. Some plaintiffs lost standing on the AOW category. The Second Amendment claims were abandoned and not decided. The ruling is stayed one week for DOJ to appeal; DOJ has not commented. (Single reachable outlet — The Reload — plus the opinion PDF; AmmoLand, SCOTUSblog, and justice.gov were unreachable this run. The opinion itself is linked and quoted.)

New ▲▲ D. Md. · DOJ · Carry Heat77▲▲#4 · filed 08/03, reported 08/05

US v. Montgomery County: DOJ's first § 12601 suit aimed at carry restrictions rather than sales bans — and the theory is still untested

The DOJ Civil Rights Division filed suit Monday, August 3, alleging Montgomery County, Maryland is engaged in a pattern or practice of Second Amendment violations through Bill 23-26. Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon's complaint calls the ordinance “breathtaking in scope,” describes an “Exclusion Zone” that “encompasses literally thousands of locations” because of the 100-yard buffer, and argues it is “much more restrictive than the offending law in Wolford” — specifically because, unlike Hawaii's rule, it contains no property-owner-permission exception, so “the owners of churches, recreational facilities, etc. are not allowed to grant their visitors permission to carry.” County Executive Marc Elrich responded in a Wednesday briefing: “We're just not going to surrender. If we get to a legal point and we lose in court, we lose in court.” The caveat that belongs in every version of this story: the suit rests on Section 12601 of the 1994 Crime Act, and US District Judge Monica Ramirez Almadani noted last month in US v. California that “no court has determined whether the United States is authorized by the statute to facially challenge a state firearms law.” The same open question hangs over DOJ's whole 2A litigation program. (Single reachable outlet plus the complaint; the DOJ press release URL was not reachable this run.)

RTB angle: Two distinct assets. First, the practical one for DC-metro members: what the exclusion zones cover today and why a lawsuit does not change them while it is pending. Second, the analytical one: DOJ has now brought sales-ban suits, manufacturing suits, police-practice investigations, and a carry-restriction suit on the same statutory hook — and a judge has already questioned the hook. Reporting that honestly, including the part that cuts against the outcome most members would prefer, is what makes the brief credible. Quote Elrich accurately and in full context; he is a named public official stating a position, not a foil.

New ▲▲▲ Coverage market · Structural Highest-value item in this section today

Membership just became the unit of legal relief — the NFA injunction protects people because of who they belong to, and that is a coverage-market story

Strip out the firearms and this is a story about associations. The Silencer Shop injunction does not apply to the public; it applies to “the plaintiffs and, where applicable, their agencies, political subdivisions, members, and customers—both current and future.” In practice that means whether a given American is covered by the most significant NFA ruling in ninety years may turn on whether they hold a membership in Gun Owners of America, the Firearms Policy Coalition Action Foundation, the Citizens Committee for the Right to Keep and Bear Arms, or the Firearms Regulatory Accountability Coalition — or whether they are a customer of a plaintiff business. Judge Hendrix explicitly framed this as a consequence of the Supreme Court no longer tolerating universal injunctions: he took “a fairly broad view of who should be covered,” but the ceiling is the parties. The same dynamic is visible in Virginia, where the Crump and Santolla injunctions differ in whom they bind, and where plaintiffs are now moving to enlarge the covered set. (Scope language quoted directly from the opinion; the practical effect of member-scoped injunctions on individual compliance is analysis, not a legal conclusion — RTB is not a law firm and should not tell anyone they are covered.)

RTB angle: Handle this one with unusual care, because it is both the most interesting insight of the week and the easiest to get wrong. What RTB can say: litigation increasingly delivers relief through organizations, which is a real and underappreciated reason people join advocacy groups — and it is separate from what a legal-defense membership does, which is fund and coordinate your defense if you are charged. What RTB must not say or imply: that any membership, including RTB's, makes a member exempt from the NFA. Draw the distinction explicitly; the honest version is more persuasive than the loose one, and the loose one is a compliance problem. New ▲▲ Constitutional law · Commentary Expect non-firearms coverage

The broccoli hypothetical is going to travel — this ruling is an enumerated-powers case that happens to be about guns

Judge Hendrix's central worry was not about firearms. It was that allowing the Necessary and Proper Clause to justify user-side regulation in service of an unrelated occupational tax “would open the door to limitless federal regulation.” His illustration: “If Congress imposed an annual $1 occupational tax on grocers, ‘anyone wishing to possess broccoli’ could be forced to jump through endless federal regulatory hoops—applications, fingerprints, and so on—just to ensure that they are not unlawfully evading the grocer tax. If that is outlandish for broccoli, it is no less so for firearms.” That is a general-purpose federalism argument with an obvious lineage to the broccoli hypothetical from the Affordable Care Act litigation, and it means this opinion will get read and argued about by people who do not follow firearms policy at all. Expect law-professor commentary, and expect some of it to be critical of the reasoning from directions that have nothing to do with the Second Amendment.

RTB angle: A rare chance to write about a firearms case in language that a non-gun-owning reader finds reasonable, because the argument is about the limits of federal power generally. Present the counter-position honestly — DOJ's view that a statute's constitutionality does not depend on which clause Congress recited, and that Commerce Clause authority plainly exists over an interstate market in these items, is a serious argument that appellate judges may well accept.
