Top of mind08 · 24

A federal judge has struck down the ATF “ghost gun” kit rule that the Supreme Court upheld 7–2 fifteen months ago — same rule, same judge, same case, different constitutional claims. It is the clearest teaching example of the post-Bruen landscape anyone will get this year, and AmmoLand is reachable again for the first time in five runs, which surfaced a backlog of six further legal developments this board could not see.

The lead. US District Judge Reed O’Connor vacated the Biden-era ATF rule that classified unfinished frames and receivers sold with jigs and finishing tools as functioning firearms. The case is Defense Distributed v. Blanche — a continuation of Bondi v. VanDerStok, in which a 7–2 Supreme Court majority upheld the very same rule in 2025. What changed is not the rule but the claims. VanDerStok was an Administrative Procedure Act case: Justice Gorsuch wrote that “at least some kits will satisfy both” statutory requirements, using the now-famous IKEA-table and unfinished-manuscript analogies, while expressly cautioning that the holding was “limited” and that “future cases may present other and more difficult questions about ATF’s regulations.” O’Connor took up two of those questions. On the Fifth Amendment, he held the rule void for vagueness because it “does not provide fair notice to the reader about when a component becomes subject to enforcement” — there is no stated point between “an unformed block of metal” and a finished receiver at which the rule attaches. On the Second Amendment, applying Bruen’s history-and-tradition test, he found the government’s colonial barrel-proofing and gunpowder-storage analogues inapposite and went further: the rule “does not merely lack supporting analogues. It contradicts the actual historical tradition of personal gunsmithing.” He rejected most of the plaintiffs’ other claims. Date discrepancy, flagged rather than resolved: The Reload’s 08/23 pieces say the ruling came down “on Monday” (08/17); the opinion PDF hosted by SAF is filenamed 8.19.26. This board could not retrieve the PDF and does not know which is right.

Second: AmmoLand came back, and it had been holding a queue. After four consecutive runs unreachable, ammoland.com resolved this morning and corroborated the ghost-gun ruling independently — the first time in sixteen runs that the multi-outlet pickup proxy has fired on anything. It also surfaced six developments no reachable source had given this board: a Texas federal judge has now fully vacated ATF’s 2024 “Engaged in the Business” rule, rejecting DOJ’s attempt to preserve pieces of it and warning that ATF’s proposed rewrite repeats the same legal error; FFLs have passed ATF compliance inspections after transferring suppressors on a Form 4473 under the NFA injunction, which is the first operational evidence the injunction survives contact with the agency; the Ninth Circuit rejected a California and Giffords bid to expand the ATF ghost-gun rule; a new federal Second Amendment suit challenges New York’s “convertible pistol” law, which reaches common Glock and Glock-pattern handguns; a proposed DOJ settlement would put Los Angeles County’s carry-permit backlog under four years of court-enforced reporting; and gun-control organisations have opened a campaign against ATF’s proposed reforms. Each of these is single-outlet and headline-level only — AmmoLand’s article pages did not render and its RSS feed returned a cached 08/05 copy — so all six are marked accordingly.

    New entry, and the state-law story with the widest practical reach. Congress is still out and nothing legislative can move until September — but the courts have now vacated two ATF rules in a fortnight, which changes what returning members will be asked about

Congress remains in recess with no chamber activity of any kind until September, so the legislative picture is unchanged for a fourth consecutive run. What has changed is the environment it returns to. In the space of roughly two weeks, federal district courts have enjoined core National Firearms Act registration provisions, vacated ATF’s frame-and-receiver “ghost gun” rule, and fully vacated ATF’s 2024 “Engaged in the Business” rule — while DOJ has separately finalised a rights-restoration process Congress did not design and set a $30 fee Congress did not authorise by statute. The reversal mechanism on the NFA side remains the one to keep quotable: the making and transfer taxes on silencers, SBRs, SBSs and AOWs fell from $200 to $0 on January 1, 2026 under the reconciliation package enacted July 4, 2025, registration survived the cut, and that mismatch is the whole constitutional argument. The Reload’s analysis is that raising the tax to one dollar “would seem to undo Judge Hendrix’s order because the tax would once more achieve the goal of raising at least some revenue.” Everything in this section remains carried without primary verification — congress.gov has now been unreachable from this board for five consecutive runs.

RTB angleThe September piece writes itself and it is not about any bill: it is “three ATF rules, three courts, one fortnight — what actually changed for you.” Mechanism, not prediction. Explain that a reconciliation statute, a court injunction and a vacated agency rule are three different kinds of thing with three different reversal paths, and that a member is standing on a different one in each case. The “convertible pistol” theory has spread to at least four states — and the September 4 California hearing is the first real test of it

The most consequential state-law development for ordinary gun owners this month, and it has been building without a single card on this board. A cluster of states has enacted or is enacting bans on so-called “semiautomatic machinegun-convertible” pistols — laws that target the cruciform trigger bar, a component integral to Glock’s design since the early 1980s, on the theory that criminals can fit “Glock switches,” devices already unlawful machine guns under both state and federal law. New York: a new federal Second Amendment lawsuit challenges the state’s version, which AmmoLand reports exposes dealers to felony charges for selling common Glock and Glock-pattern handguns. California: Penal Code § 27595(a), enacted by AB 1127 and effective July 1, 2026, is under a preliminary-injunction motion filed August 4 in Renna v. Bonta by the Firearms Policy Coalition, Second Amendment Foundation, CCRKBA, the NRA, San Diego County Gun Owners PAC, individual owners and dealers. The hearing is set for September 4 before Judge Dana Sabraw — the same judge who in April 2023 preliminarily enjoined California’s Handgun Roster chamber-indicator, magazine-disconnect and microstamping requirements, a ruling still under Ninth Circuit review. The plaintiffs’ filing states the definition “effectively covers every factory-stock Glock semiautomatic pistol and many Glock-pattern handguns, including the Palmetto State Armory Dagger, Ruger RXM, and several Shadow Systems models.” DOJ separately sued California on July 1 and was denied a TRO. Maryland has enacted a similar restriction with an injunction motion filed before its effective date; Connecticut, Massachusetts and New York are cited in the California filing as having taken comparable legislative or regulatory action.

Explain the distinction and do not editorialise about legislative motive.

The ATF “ghost gun” kit rule is struck down — by the same judge, in the same case, that the Supreme Court reversed fifteen months ago

The most important legal development of the month and the clearest available illustration of how post-Bruen litigation actually works. US District Judge Reed O’Connor vacated the Biden-era ATF rule classifying unfinished frames and receivers sold alongside jigs and finishing tools as functioning firearms. Defense Distributed v. Blanche is a continuation of Bondi v. VanDerStok — plaintiffs dropped off and government defendants were swapped, but it is the same litigation in which a 7–2 Supreme Court majority upheld the rule about fifteen months ago. What changed is the claims, not the rule. VanDerStok was an Administrative Procedure Act challenge. Justice Gorsuch held that “at least some kits will satisfy both” statutory requirements, reasoning through an unfinished manuscript, an unassembled IKEA table and a disassembled rifle, and concluding that “an ordinary speaker might well describe the ‘Buy Build Shoot’ kit as a ‘weapon.’” He also wrote, explicitly, that the holding had “its limits” and that “future cases may present other and more difficult questions about ATF’s regulations.” O’Connor took up two of them. Fifth Amendment, void for vagueness: the parties agreed the Supreme Court “did not address any constitutional issues” in Bondi, and O’Connor found the rule’s language — a frame or receiver “can sometimes include a forging, casting, printing, extrusion, unmachined body, or similar article,” depending on whether it has “reached a stage of manufacture where it is clearly identifiable” — fails to give fair notice: “But who’s to say when that stage is.” Second Amendment, Bruen history-and-tradition: he rejected the government’s colonial Maine and Massachusetts barrel-proofing statutes and gunpowder-storage laws as inapposite analogues, then went further — “The Final Rule does not merely lack supporting analogues. It contradicts the actual historical tradition of personal gunsmithing. Self-manufacture of firearms in America was common and indeed foundational to establishing our Nation.” He rejected most of the plaintiffs’ remaining claims. Date discrepancy, unresolved: The Reload says the ruling issued “on Monday” in a piece published Sunday 08/23, which points to 08/17; the opinion PDF hosted by the Second Amendment Foundation is filenamed 8.19.26. saf.org was not retrievable this run and this board cannot say which is correct.

RTB angleWrite the explainer, and let the structure carry the point: “How the same rule can be upheld by the Supreme Court and struck down by a district judge — and both be correct.” The through-line is that courts answer the question in front of them, that VanDerStok was about statutory authority while this is about notice and history, and that Gorsuch said so in the opinion itself. Three guardrails, all mandatory. (1) Do not write “SCOTUS reversed itself” — it did not, and it has not spoken here. (2) Do not tell any reader what they may lawfully build, buy or sell today; a district-court vacatur is appealable, O’Connor was overruled on this rule once already, and state law is untouched by any of it. (3) Get the ruling date right before publishing — the two available sources disagree by two days.

New ▲▲ ATF · NFA injunction · Form 4473 Heat74▲▲#3 · new entry · single-outlet

FFLs have passed ATF compliance inspections after transferring suppressors on a Form 4473 — the first operational proof the injunction holds

New and the most immediately useful item on the board for anyone who sells or buys. Under Judge Hendrix’s injunction, covered dealers have been transferring suppressors on an ordinary Form 4473 — no Form 4, no NFA registration, no fingerprints, no advance ATF approval — and AmmoLand reports those dealers have since been through ATF compliance inspections and passed. That is a meaningful escalation from the position on Friday, when the board’s standing complaint was that eleven days had produced no agency guidance of any kind. It still has not; what it has produced instead is de facto evidence of how ATF is treating these transactions in the field. The known limits are unchanged and load-bearing. The injunction covers plaintiff organisations’ current and future members and the commercial plaintiffs’ customers in transactions with those plaintiffs — Hendrix wrote that benefit to non-party customers is “merely incidental” and expressly declined to extend it to resident family members. It runs to state agencies of the fifteen plaintiff states, not their residents. And in the fifteen-or-so states with NFA-mirroring statutes, state law requires registration regardless. Single-outlet, headline-and-dek only; the identity of the dealers and inspecting field offices is not in this board’s hands.

RTB angleThis is the FFL-facing piece that has been sitting unwritten for two weeks, and it now has a concrete hook. Write it for dealers: what a covered transfer looks like on paper, what the inspection reportedly found, what an FFL is exposed to if the ruling is later reversed, and why “my supplier is a plaintiff” is the operative question rather than “is this legal now.” It reaches exactly the retail partners RTB wants and no coverage peer will produce it. Verify the inspection claim independently before publishing — it is currently one outlet at headline level.

Day eleven of the live NFA injunction — still no DOJ notice of appeal, still no agency guidance, roughly five weeks of window left

Cooling for a third consecutive run because nothing has happened, which is itself the story. No notice of appeal, no ATF or DOJ guidance, no new filings reachable from this board. The Reload’s 08/13 report that DOJ “has another seven weeks to decide” puts the outside edge near October 1, leaving roughly five weeks today. AmmoLand’s independent account, now retrievable, restates the holding with useful precision: Judge James Wesley Hendrix entered a permanent injunction because the NFA registration scheme “is unconstitutional because it no longer functions as a revenue measure under the Taxing Clause,” following the One Big Beautiful Bill Act’s reduction of making and transfer taxes on SBSs, SBRs, silencers and AOWs to $0 effective January 1, 2026 (machine guns and destructive devices keep the $200 tax). He rejected the Necessary and Proper Clause argument as neither “plainly adapted” nor “proper,” and rejected the Commerce Clause fallback because Congress never invoked it — noting the NFA’s placement in Title 26 and contrasting the Gun Control Act’s jurisdictional hooks, citing Lopez and Morrison. Enjoined provisions: 26 U.S.C. §§ 5812(a)–(b), 5822, 5841(a)–(c) and (e), 5842(b), 5861(b)–(f) and (i) and related regulations. Plaintiffs abandoned their Second Amendment claims after winning on enumerated powers, so the court never reached them — which is why this ruling and the ghost-gun ruling rest on entirely different foundations. Per Trump v. Casa, relief is not nationwide. The three-statement discipline still holds: Rep. Andrew Clyde’s 08/12 social post claiming confirmation DOJ will not appeal is a member of Congress’s post; The Reload records DOJ “did not respond to multiple requests for comment”; DOJ told The New York Times only that it will not seek a stay if and when it appeals.

RTB angleHold the drafted piece and keep the countdown internal. When the window closes with no notice, that is a genuine news event and the explainer should already exist. In the meantime, the useful public asset is the enumerated-powers point most coverage is missing: this ruling is not a Second Amendment ruling — the plaintiffs dropped that claim — and that is precisely why a one-dollar tax could undo it. Explaining that well distinguishes RTB from everyone writing “court strikes NFA.”

New ▲▲ ATF · Rulemaking · Engaged in the Business Heat71▲▲#5 · new entry · single-outlet

ATF’s 2024 “Engaged in the Business” rule is fully vacated — and the court warns the proposed rewrite repeats the same error

New, and it matters to more ordinary members than its heat score suggests. A Texas federal judge has rejected DOJ’s attempt to preserve portions of ATF’s 2024 rule redefining who is “engaged in the business” of dealing firearms and therefore required to hold an FFL and run background checks, leaving the entire regulation vacated. Per AmmoLand’s account, the court also warned that ATF’s proposed rewrite repeats the same legal error — which, if accurate, forecasts the outcome of the next round before it starts. The practical significance is the line between a lawful private sale and unlicensed dealing, which is among the most frequently misunderstood questions in everyday firearms law and one where a member’s mistake is a federal felony rather than a fine. Note the contrast with the ghost-gun ruling on the same page of the same outlet: this is an administrative-law vacatur, that one is a constitutional holding, and they are reversible by entirely different routes. Single-outlet, headline-and-dek only — the judge, case name, district and reasoning are not in this board’s hands, and AmmoLand’s article page did not render.

RTB angleDo not publish anything that could read as “you can now sell freely.” The statutory definition of dealing did not change; an agency rule interpreting it was vacated, and the statute still governs. The safe and genuinely useful piece is the standing one: what federal law actually says about occasional private sales, why the answer is fact-specific, and why a member should talk to counsel in their state before selling a collection. Verify the case name and judge first.

Moved ▼ National · FBI 2025 crime data · AH Datalytics Heat59▼#9 · podcast context 08/23

The analyst who forecast the record murder drop says it has not stopped — and that its breadth defeats the simple partisan explanations

Carried from Friday and enriched rather than changed. The Reload published a podcast on 08/23 with Jeff Asher of AH Datalytics, who had been predicting the FBI’s 2025 numbers would land almost exactly where they did. Three additions worth having. First, the decline is continuing: Asher’s real-time data, which tracks closely with the FBI’s, indicates the murder rate is still falling in 2026. Second, the drop has been “consistent across the nation and across demographics,” which he argues “defies the simplest partisan explanations for the decline.” Third, he offered a preferred theory for why it is happening while stating plainly that it is impossible to say for certain — a posture worth borrowing wholesale. The underlying figures are unchanged: violent crime −9.7 percent, the largest annual drop since the FBI began reporting in 1936; murder and non-negligent manslaughter −18.1 percent, the largest single-year decrease on record; the 2025 murder rate 4.1 per 100,000, tied with 1955 and 1956 for the lowest ever measured; preliminary January–June 2026 figures down a further 10.6 percent on violent crime and 23 percent on murder. And the honest full picture any credible piece must carry: of 15,522 murders in 2025, 10,229 — two-thirds — involved a firearm; assaults on law-enforcement officers reached a ten-year high; and the gun-suicide rate remains at 7.6 per 100,000, essentially level with its 1977 all-time high, even as the gun-homicide rate has fallen well below its 1974 peak.

RTB anglePublish this week if it is not already out, and let Asher’s epistemic honesty set the tone. The strongest version credits nobody and claims nothing: here is the largest recorded drop, here is the analyst who called it, here is why he says the cause is genuinely unclear, and here is the firearm share and the suicide figure that any complete account has to include. That completeness is the differentiator — a piece that cites only the good number reads as advocacy and will be picked apart.

Five Washington Supreme Court seats are on the November ballot — a reminder that state high courts decide most of what a member actually experiences

A useful corrective to a board that spends most of its attention on Congress and the federal judiciary. New ▲ Politics · TX-23 · under-21 purchase age new · doctrinal split is the story

The purchase-age question surfaces in a congressional race — and the underlying legal split is genuinely unresolved

A campaign story with a real doctrinal question inside it. AmmoLand reports that the Democratic opponent of Rep. Brandon Herrera, Katy Padilla Stout, has argued that legal adults under 21 should be barred from purchasing commonly owned semiautomatic rifles, invoking brain-development research. Setting the politics aside entirely, the legal position is unsettled in a way most coverage on both sides elides: the Supreme Court declined earlier this year to take up petitions concerning firearm restrictions on 18-to-20-year-olds, leaving a three-way circuit split in place. That means the answer to “may an 18-year-old buy a rifle” currently depends on which circuit a member lives in, and that is a real, practical, apolitical thing worth explaining.

RTB angleDo not cover the race. Sensitive-topic notes. The ghost-gun ruling is a compliance-risk item, not a permission slip. A district-court vacatur is appealable, this specific judge was overruled on this specific rule once already, and nothing in the ruling touches state law — several states independently regulate unfinished frames and receivers. Any RTB piece must be explanatory rather than operational: no reader should finish it believing they have been told what they may lawfully build, buy or sell. The restoration rule remains the highest-liability item this brief carries, for the same reason as before: its audience is by definition people with prior convictions or adjudications making consequential decisions about firearm possession, and a sentence read as “you are clear to buy a gun” could put a reader in federal custody. Procedural, never evaluative; explicit “not a law firm, not legal advice, consult counsel in your state” on every piece.
