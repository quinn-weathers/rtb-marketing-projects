What happened, precisely. On August 5, US District Judge James Wesley Hendrix (N.D. Tex.) permanently enjoined the National Firearms Act's regulatory provisions for the items Congress zeroed out, then stayed his own order for seven calendar days. The Justice Department did not appeal within that window. The stay expired at midnight Central. At 12:01 a.m. on August 13, Gun Owners of America, Silencer Shop and two buyers — congressional candidate Brandon Herrera and Texas Rep. Wes Virdell — completed suppressor transfers with no ATF Form 4, no advance approval, and no entry in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record. GOA sent DOJ and ATF a letter in advance stating exactly what it intended to do and why: both buyers are GOA members and Silencer Shop customers, so they are covered twice over by an injunction that runs to plaintiffs and “their agencies, political subdivisions, members, and customers—both current and future.”

The correction almost every outlet will get wrong today: missing the stay deadline is not the same as declining to appeal. Per The Reload, DOJ still has roughly seven more weeks to decide whether to appeal — it simply lost the chance to keep the injunction on hold while it thinks. DOJ did not respond to multiple requests for comment on its plans. Separately, Rep. Andrew Clyde (R., Ga.), who has been lobbying the administration not to appeal, posted Wednesday that he has “received confirmation from the Trump Administration that the DOJ will NOT appeal.” That is a member of Congress characterizing a private communication, not a filing or a DOJ statement. Treat it as a claim, attributed, and not as the government's position. A parallel NFA challenge brought by the NRA and others is still pending in the Eighth Circuit, and DOJ has said nothing about that one either.

And the caveat list is longer than the headline. Silencer Shop published its own limits, and they are the thing members will actually ask about. The company says suppressors and AOWs obtained through it can now transfer with no Form 4 and no federal registration — but only in the 27 states without state-level laws mirroring the NFA, and it identified 15 states where ATF registration is still required as a matter of state law. Purchases must run through Silencer Shop or its dealers. And critically: short-barrel rifles and shotguns are not included, because the 1968 Gun Control Act independently requires ATF approval for those. As the company put it, “the tax is gone, but the paperwork isn't.” The injunction also does not touch machine guns or destructive devices, whose taxes were never zeroed.

The second story of the day is a real carry win, and it is being buried. Per AmmoLand, the Ninth Circuit has reinstated an injunction against Hawaii's private-property “vampire rule” — the default rule that treated every private property as off-limits to licensed carry unless the owner affirmatively said otherwise — after the Supreme Court held that such a default ban violates the Second and Fourteenth Amendments. This is the kind of ruling that changes what a permit holder can actually do on an ordinary Tuesday, and it will get a fraction of the NFA story's attention. (Single reachable outlet; verify the panel order before member-facing publication.)

Congress is not idle, even in recess. Per AmmoLand, 47 Republican lawmakers urged DOJ to stop defending the NFA's zero-tax registration scheme and to direct ATF to apply Judge Hendrix's reasoning nationwide — which, notably, is something a court cannot order and an agency can choose. And Rep. Lauren Boebert's Freedom From Taxes Act would extend the same $0-tax logic to machine guns and destructive devices, the two categories Judge Hendrix's ruling deliberately leaves alone. NSSF also praised Senate introduction of the Federal Firearms Licensee Protection Act.

Two data points worth writing down. A new study reported by AmmoLand found firearm background checks in Oregon surged 157% after voters approved Measure 114 — the same demand-response pattern Colorado just produced at +264%. And a survey of general-election voters found nearly one in three say they carry a concealed handgun at least occasionally, continuing a sharp rise since December 2024. That second number is the single most useful figure on this board for a membership organization.

Eight days after the biggest firearms ruling in ninety years, and on the morning it takes practical effect, not one coverage peer has published anything about it. USCCA's most recent posts are a Staccato pistol review and an August 6 gun-storage liability piece. US LawShield's front-page legal-update module still features 2024 items. New ▲▲ Federal · House · NFA Heat78▲▲#3 · 47 signatories · per AmmoLand

Forty-seven Republican lawmakers are asking DOJ to do something no court can order: stop enforcing the enjoined NFA provisions against everyone

This is the most structurally interesting item in the whole NFA story and it has nothing to do with a courtroom. Judge Hendrix was explicit that he could only bind the government as to the plaintiffs and, where applicable, their members and customers — the Supreme Court no longer tolerates universal injunctions. But an injunction is a floor, not a ceiling. Nothing prevents the executive branch from deciding, as a matter of enforcement policy, that it will not pursue the enjoined provisions against anyone. Per AmmoLand, 47 Republican lawmakers have now asked DOJ to do exactly that: stop defending the zero-tax registration scheme and direct ATF to apply the ruling nationwide. With the seven-day stay having lapsed without an appeal, that request is no longer a long shot. It is also, importantly, a discretionary outcome — which means it could be extended by one administration and withdrawn by the next, and nobody's rights would have changed in the interim. (Per AmmoLand's index dek of its Aug 11 report; the letter itself and the signatory list were not retrieved. Treat the count as reported, not verified.)

RTB angle: This is the single best piece of civics content available to RTB right now, and it flatters nobody's team. Three kinds of relief look identical from a distance and behave completely differently: a court injunction that binds the government as to specific people, an agency enforcement policy that can be reversed by memo, and a statute that requires an act of Congress to undo. Explain the difference plainly and let readers draw their own conclusions about which one they'd rather rely on. It also answers the question a member will actually ask — “am I covered?” — with the honest answer: today, that depends on which of the three you're standing on.

New ▲▲▲ Federal · House · Boebert Heat69▲▲▲#7 · bill number unverified

The Freedom From Taxes Act would point the same argument at the two categories Judge Hendrix deliberately left alone

Read the ruling closely and it contains its own roadmap. Judge Hendrix held that the NFA's registration machinery was built to collect the transfer and making taxes, that a $0 tax produces no revenue and therefore is not a tax, and that the machinery consequently loses its constitutional footing — but the One Big Beautiful Bill Act zeroed the tax on “any firearm” except machine guns and destructive devices. Those two categories still carry a $200 tax and are therefore untouched by this ruling. Per AmmoLand, Rep. Lauren Boebert's Freedom From Taxes Act would zero those rates as well, which on Judge Hendrix's reasoning would expose their registration requirements to the identical challenge. Whether that is desirable policy is a genuinely contested question — the Hughes Amendment's 1986 machine-gun ban is a separate statutory bar that a tax change would not remove, and serious people disagree sharply about the underlying merits. But as a description of where this litigation strategy goes next, it is not really ambiguous. (Per AmmoLand's Aug 10 analysis piece; no bill number, cosponsor count or committee referral was retrievable. congress.gov not reached.)

RTB angle: Cover the mechanism, not the merits. The interesting and non-obvious point for a general audience is that a tax rate set to zero can dismantle a regulatory scheme built on top of it — a piece of constitutional plumbing almost nobody knew was there until three weeks ago. Pair it with the Hughes Amendment history, note explicitly that zeroing a tax would not itself legalize new machine guns because a separate 1986 statutory bar exists, and present the policy debate as a debate. Do not publish anything that reads as advocacy for expanded access to machine guns or destructive devices.

New ▲▲ Executive branch · Non-defense Claim attributed · not a DOJ statement

An administration declining to defend a ninety-year-old statute is a significant institutional story, and it is being told entirely through a congressman's social media post

Strip out the subject matter and this is a familiar constitutional question: what happens when the executive branch chooses not to defend a federal statute it has been sued over. It is not unprecedented — administrations of both parties have declined to defend statutes they considered unconstitutional, and it draws sharp criticism each time from whoever is disadvantaged. What is unusual here is the record. DOJ has said nothing. It did not respond to multiple requests for comment on whether it would appeal. It let the seven-day stay lapse, which has the practical effect of allowing the injunction to operate, but it retains roughly seven weeks to file. The only affirmative statement that DOJ will not appeal comes from Rep. Andrew Clyde (R., Ga.) — who lobbied for that outcome — describing confirmation received from the administration, and thanking the President and Attorney General publicly. AmmoLand reported on August 9 that career attorneys and administration officials were split. So the state of the record is: an outcome that looks like non-defense, no official acknowledgment of it, and an interested third party characterizing a private communication. (Per The Reload and AmmoLand; justice.gov was not retrieved and no DOJ statement has been located.)

RTB angle: A short institutional explainer, written to be readable by someone who disagrees with the outcome. When the executive declines to defend a statute, the practical effect is that a single district judge's reasoning governs unless someone else has standing to appeal — and that is a structural point about how law changes, independent of whether you like this particular change. Present the criticism fairly; the strongest version of it is that non-defense lets one district court set national policy without appellate review, and that argument has been made by both parties depending on who was in office. RTB's readers can handle the nuance and will trust the brand more for it.

Watch → Federalism · Preemption Structural · sharper this week

As of this morning, whether federal firearms registration applies to you can depend on which organizations you belong to — which is a genuinely strange state of affairs

The synthesis this board has been building for weeks got a great deal sharper overnight, and it is worth stating without editorial heat because the plain description is remarkable enough. Today, whether the NFA's registration requirements apply to a given American depends on: whether they are a member of one of four specific organizations or a customer of a specific retailer; which state they live in and whether that state independently mirrors the federal scheme; which category of item is involved, because the 1968 Gun Control Act still governs short-barrel firearms; and whether the executive branch chooses to extend non-enforcement beyond what a court ordered. Set that alongside the rest of the board — a federal suit against a county over a carry ordinance, two courts reaching opposite answers on identical foster-care storage questions, fourteen prosecutors declining to enforce a state statute, a Ninth Circuit ruling on whether a state can make private property carry-free by default, and proposed federal rules to stop states prosecuting people who drive through. These are not separate stories. They are one unresolved question about which sovereign's rules govern a given person at a given moment, and the answers currently vary by geography, by licence status, by item category, and now by organizational membership.

RTB angle: The strongest structural argument for what RTB does, made descriptively rather than through fear. “Your rights change when you cross a line, and here is the map” is the single most useful thing a national self-defense membership can explain, and today it has an unusually vivid example: a federal law that applies differently depending on who you belong to. Publish it monthly or quarterly as a synthesis, keep it descriptive, and let the conclusion — that the patchwork is real and navigable with good information — do the work. No implication that anyone is out to get anyone.
