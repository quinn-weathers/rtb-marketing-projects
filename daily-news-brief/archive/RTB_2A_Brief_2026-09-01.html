Start with the one that changes a number this board has been quoting for a week. The reason it matters is not the discount — it is what it says about how FLP goes to market. This board has logged FLP as content-dormant for four runs, and that reading is still correct: its newest blog post is dated September 24, 2025, now eleven months old, and the site’s copyright line still reads 2025. But dormant content is not the same as dormant growth. The same site carries a Partner Login, a Producer Login, a Become a Partner form, a Retail Activation path and a dealer webinar sign-up — and now a named creator funnel with its own discount code. Read FLP as a distribution-led competitor, not a media-led one. Judging it by its blog cadence understates it, and any RTB competitive assessment built only on published-content volume will be wrong about where its members are coming from.

Second: CCW Safe’s full plan matrix, captured completely for the first time. The comparison page (modified April 9, 2026) publishes nine plans with annual and monthly pricing: Protector $179/$16 (active or retired law enforcement and military, honourably discharged veterans, credentialed first responders), Defender $209/$19 (any CCW permit holder), House of Worship Volunteer Security $275 annual-only with a three-member minimum, Constitutional Carry $299/$27, Home Defense $299/$27, Liberty $399/$37, HR-218 $399/$37 (LE/LEOSA only), Ultimate $519/$47 and Freedom $609/$55. What is worth studying there is the architecture, not the prices. Three details stand out because they are the kind of thing a member actually asks about. (1) Every one of the nine plans answers Y to “Civil Trial Costs Covered, Even if Found Guilty in a Criminal Trial.” (2) Red-flag / extreme-risk-protection-order defence is capped — $5,000 to $10,000 by tier — which is a different structure from an uncapped attorney-fee promise, and members comparing the two need the distinction explained, not adjudicated. (3) The segmentation is by eligibility: permit holder, non-permit holder, law enforcement, constitutional-carry resident, home-only, church security team. That is a different axis from USCCA, whose three tiers differ mainly on education volume rather than on who qualifies. Also published: lost wages $250–$1,000 per day, crime-scene cleanup $3,000–$4,000, 10 to 40 counselling sessions ($1,500–$6,000), firearm replacement to MSRP, bond included at $1M ($1.5M on Ultimate and Freedom, $250,000 in-home for spouse and children), a $15,000 funeral benefit if a member dies during a self-defence incident, up to $10,000 for an unintentional TSA screening violation, and state-specific riders for the Texas School Guardian Program and Louisiana Act 680 veteran permitless carry.

The four-ways-a-court-win-can-fail explainer is now fully assembled and unpublished, and it is the single strongest evergreen this board is sitting on

No movement today, which is the right moment to note that the piece is complete. The four failure modes, each with a worked example from this year: (1) relief that runs only to the named parties — Silencer Shop v. ATF, where the NFA registration injunction protects the plaintiffs and their customers rather than every owner in the country. (2) Relief that is as-applied — Defense Distributed v. Blanche, where ATF’s frame-and-receiver rule was held unconstitutional as applied to those plaintiffs. (3) A mandate the winning court stays itself — Cheeseman, where the Third Circuit struck New Jersey’s rifle and magazine bans 10–5 on August 4 and then stayed its own mandate while the state prepares a Supreme Court petition, so nothing changed for New Jersey owners. (4) Standing — from The Reload’s 08/30 analysis: in California v. Texas a challenge to Obamacare’s $0 individual mandate won at the district court and before a Fifth Circuit panel, then lost 7–2 at the Supreme Court without the merits ever being reached. Justice Breyer: “There is no one, and nothing, to enjoin.” S. 218, which would remove suppressors from the NFA by statute, has none of the four — but its number and status are unverified for a tenth run.

RTB angleWrite it this week. The reason it works is that the fourth mode is the one nobody explains to members: a case can be thrown out before any court rules on whether the law is constitutional at all, because the court decides the plaintiffs were not injured in a way it can fix. That single idea turns a civics post into something that reads like insider knowledge, and it is the difference between a piece members skim and a piece they send to someone. Use California v. Texas as the worked example, keep the distinguishing sentence verbatim — there the $0 was the penalty; here the $0 is the tax, and the penalty for not registering is still fines and prison — note that a statute avoids all four modes, and take no position on any pending bill. The empowerment payoff is the closing line: this is why the sentence “a court struck it down” is the beginning of the answer and not the end of it.

The microstamping finding has been quotable for five days, no coverage peer has touched it, and nist.gov has now been unreachable for five consecutive runs

Unchanged in substance and still the most underused document on the board. The review was produced at the request of House Science, Space, and Technology Committee Chairman Brian Babin (R-Texas), and the National Institute of Standards and Technology responded in writing that “there is significant disagreement regarding the viability of this technology.” NIST flagged firearm design, ammunition type, primer coatings, residue, firing-pin movement, wear, repeated firing and maintenance as factors affecting mark transfer; said the available studies are too limited to establish viability across firearms and ammunition in actual use; and noted there have been no adequate large-scale independent blind studies. Two admissions constrain how far the finding can be pushed and must appear in any copy built on it: NIST conducted no laboratory testing of its own and maintains no microstamping research programme; and it acknowledged that firing pins can be replaced, engraving damaged and parts swapped, with no realistic mechanical means of preventing those modifications. This still rests on the NSSF write-up as relayed by AmmoLand plus a linked but unfetched PDF.

RTB angleFive days of exclusivity in a four-competitor content lane that is publishing gear reviews is not a coincidence, it is an opening — and the SAAMI Ballistics Conference on September 15–17 is the deadline, because that is where the question gets a technical audience and the finding stops being anyone’s exclusive. The publishable formulation is unchanged and precise: a federal standards agency told a House committee chairman that the research literature does not establish microstamping reliability — and said it has not tested the technology itself. Pin it to California SB 452 and the conditional January 1, 2028 dealer trigger so members understand this is a future obligation rather than a current one; a meaningful number of them believe it already binds. Never write “NIST found microstamping does not work.” That is not what the letter says, and the accurate version is more useful anyway.
