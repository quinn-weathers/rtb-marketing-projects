Top of mind09 · 03

Registration got argued from both ends in the same twenty-four hours. A federal appeals court held that gun registration it considers “minimal” does not implicate the Second Amendment at all — while a manufacturer, not the ATF, published the first plain-language instructions for buying a suppressor without registering it. Members are standing between those two facts today.

The Third Circuit’s September 1 opinion in United States v. Berger conceded that machine guns are “Arms” under the Second Amendment’s plain text, upheld the federal ban anyway, and then went further: a two-judge majority held that the NFA’s registration process imposed only an incidental burden, and that “minimal, objective buying-and-selling regulations are not infringements” and therefore fall outside the Amendment’s scope entirely. Judge Chung declined to join that reasoning and flagged tension with the Supreme Court’s rejection of interest-balancing. On the same board, SilencerCo published buyer guidance for the Silencer Shop injunction covering 27 states — no Form 4, no fingerprints, no CLEO notification, Form 4473 and a background check — with an unusually candid warning that 26 U.S.C. § 5861(j) makes interstate transport an open question, so keep the suppressor in the state where you bought it. Twenty-one days into the injunction, ATF has still published no guidance. That is the whole brief in one line: one court says the paperwork was never a burden worth constitutional attention, and the people living without the paperwork cannot get a straight answer about crossing a state line.

03ATF, courts & regulatory

Rulemaking, litigation, enforcement
New 3rd Circuit · decided 09/01 United States v. Berger · Bibas, Restrepo, Chung (concurring in judgment)Heat80#1

A federal appeals court conceded machine guns are “Arms,” upheld the ban anyway, and then wrote a sentence that could excuse gun registration from Bruen review entirely

The Third Circuit affirmed Joseph Paul Berger’s convictions under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) and the NFA. Two holdings matter well beyond the facts. First, at step one the court agreed machine guns are “‘Arms’ protected by the text of the Second Amendment,” then upheld the ban under Heller’s “dangerous and unusual” language, relying on twentieth-century material — congressional reports and laws enacted by at least 29 states between 1925 and 1934 — which the panel conceded are not founding-era analogues. Judge Bibas opened with “Gun bans are strong medicine. But occasionally, they are just what the doctor ordered.” Second, and further-reaching: a two-judge majority held that the NFA registration process — personal information, fingerprints, photograph, suppressor identifiers, local law-enforcement notification, serialisation and the then-applicable $200 tax — was an ordinary shall-issue licensing system imposing only an incidental burden, and wrote that “because minimal, objective buying-and-selling regulations are not infringements, they are outside the scope of the Second Amendment.” That stops the analysis before the government must produce any historical analogue. Judge Chung joined the judgment but expressly refused that reasoning, noting the government had never argued it and perceiving “some tension between this approach and the Supreme Court’s rejection of means-end scrutiny and interest balancing”; she would have held a suppressor an unprotected accessory instead. The panel noted Berger presented no evidence of excessive waiting periods and did not argue the tax was exorbitant, and his challenge was decided as applied. The slip opinion is posted in full as a PDF.

RTB angleThis is the most important thing to explain to members this week, and it explains cleanly without any advocacy. The question is not “are machine guns legal” — almost no member is asking that. The question is what a court means when it says a rule is too small to count. Registration, fingerprints, notification and a fee were held to be paperwork rather than infringement; a second judge on the same panel said that reasoning may conflict with the Supreme Court. Write it as a doctrine explainer with both readings quoted and neither endorsed, and pair it with the practical piece below — a member reading only Berger would conclude the paperwork is settled, and a member reading only SilencerCo would conclude it is gone. Neither is true, and the gap between them is exactly the space RTB exists to occupy. No prediction about en banc or certiorari.
