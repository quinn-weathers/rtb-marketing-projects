Top of mind10 · 15

Washington is now policing permit delays: DOJ has opened a civil-rights investigation into how one New Jersey township handles handgun purchase permits. On the same day, 174 House members asked DOJ to reverse a different decision, on under-21 handgun sales.

What happened. Per the Daily Caller News Foundation’s report (republished by BizPac Review, 10/14, read), the Justice Department announced Wednesday that its Civil Rights Division, through its Second Amendment Section, is investigating Pennsauken Township, N.J. (Camden County) over alleged “unconstitutional delays” in answering applications for permits to purchase handguns. DOJ officials said individuals and groups had complained of extensive delays, that the department has “not reached any conclusions,” and that it will examine whether there is a “pattern or practice” of rights violations. The township’s mayor had not responded to a request for comment. Breitbart and STL.News carry the same news (headlines). DOJ’s own release was not located on justice.gov this run.

It fits a pattern. The same report lists DOJ’s June probe of Philadelphia’s permit revocations, its September suit against San Jose over carry-permit fees, and its August settlement with the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Department over carry-permit delays. The legal hook is a footnote in Bruen (2022) leaving room for challenges where “lengthy wait times” or “exorbitant fees” deny ordinary citizens their rights. New Jersey requires a permit to purchase each handgun.

And the enforcement posture is itself contested. Per TTAG (10/14, read), 174 House members sent DOJ a letter asking it to rescind its opinion that the federal ban on licensed dealers selling handguns to adults 18–20 is unconstitutional, and to resume enforcing it. DOJ has told the Fifth Circuit (in FPC v. ATF) that it will not enforce that law “against anyone, anywhere.” A letter from Congress does not change the law or DOJ’s position; it signals that the next administration, or a court, could.

Why it matters for RTB. This is an empowerment story with a practical action attached: if your permit is slow, keep a dated record (when you applied, what was asked, every follow-up and reply). That record is what complaints, and investigations like this one, are built from. Two cautions for any content: an investigation is not a finding against Pennsauken, and federal enforcement priorities can change, as the House letter shows. Not legal advice.

01Federal legislation

Bills, hearings, appropriations
Moved ▶ Congress · adults 18–20 · DOJ policy Heat66▲▲ 10/14 · TTAG (read) · letter not read directly

174 House members ask DOJ to rescind its under-21 opinion and resume enforcing the handgun-sales ban

Per TTAG (10/14, read), a letter from 174 House members, announced in a news release by the House Gun Violence Prevention Task Force, urges DOJ to “immediately rescind” its Office of Legal Counsel opinion that the 1968 ban on licensed dealers selling handguns to 18-to-20-year-olds is unconstitutional. The letter argues the law has been enforced by attorneys general of both parties for more than half a century and that the decision belongs to Congress; Giffords backs the letter. TTAG, which supports DOJ’s position, notes several courts have held that 18-to-20-year-olds are among “the people” the Second Amendment protects. Where things stand: DOJ told the Fifth Circuit in FPC v. ATF it will not enforce the provisions “against anyone, anywhere” and asked the court to treat the case as moot. A letter is not a bill; no legislation was reported. Many states set their own minimum ages, which are unaffected.

RTB angleSame advice as the NFA, different item: federal non-enforcement is a policy, and policies can be reversed. Young adult members should check their state’s age rules before buying. We don’t name or grade the signers.

Moved ▶ ATF · National Firearms Act · the courts Heat88▼ Duke analysis 10/14 (read) · Federal Register checked · ruling dated 1 Sept.

While ATF pauses NFA enforcement, a federal appeals court has held that suppressor registration does not “infringe” the Second Amendment

Per the Duke Center for Firearms Law (10/14, read): in United States v. Berger (decided 1 September; not a new ruling, newly analyzed), a Third Circuit panel upheld a Pennsylvania man’s convictions for possessing machine guns and unregistered silencers. It held machine guns are “dangerous and unusual” (no federal appeals court of nine to rule has struck down § 922(o)). On silencers it skipped whether they are “Arms” and held that the NFA’s registration rules are “objective, minimally burdensome” incidental burdens, not infringements; one judge concurred on narrower grounds. Duke notes a circuit split on whether silencers are “Arms” (Fifth Circuit yes, Ninth Circuit no). Why it matters now: ATF’s nationwide non-enforcement is a policy following the Texas injunction; Berger shows other federal courts still treat registration as constitutional. Federal Register: checked via API; no ATF documents published 1–15 October. A headline about new ATF explosives regulations (Israel Defense, 10/15) does not match any October Federal Register document and is not carried. Practical rules unchanged: Form 4473 and NICS still apply; Form 20 still needed for interstate SBR/SBS transport.

RTB angleThis strengthens the “policy, not law” message. The paperwork is paused by ATF; courts outside Texas have upheld it. Keep records; no predictions.
