Over the weekend the fight moved up a level: the Fifth Circuit voted to reconsider whether Congress had the constitutional power to enact the federal gun ban at all — and the executive branch quietly pulled civilian suppressors out of the military export system.

Sourcing status — live access, second consecutive run. The Reload, AmmoLand, NSSF, and SCOTUSblog were all reachable this session and items below are freshly dated (07/24–07/27). WebSearch was unavailable, so heat signals are directional editorial estimates rather than platform pulls. Primary-source-anchored today: the Squire en banc petition and Judge Higginson’s dissent (both quoted at length and linked to CourtListener/Justia by The Reload), and Justice Thomas’s Hemani concurrence (supremecourt.gov PDF).

The lead. On Monday, July 20, the Fifth Circuit voted to rehear United States v. Squire en banc — and the reason it matters is the theory it agreed to hear. Squire’s lawyers deliberately set the Second Amendment aside and challenged § 922(g)(1), the lifetime felon-in-possession ban, under the Commerce Clause: that the only federal hook was that a gun in a Louisiana home “happened to have been manufactured outside the state.” A Second Amendment win would free one defendant; a Commerce Clause win could strike the felon ban on its face and put the whole 1968 Gun Control Act architecture in question. The court is picking up an invitation Justice Thomas issued last month in his solo Hemani concurrence, where he wrote that § 922(g) “appear[s] to exceed Congress’s enumerated power” and that “the Court, and lower courts, should revisit” it. Judge Higginson, the lone recorded dissenter, warned the move would “imperil much of the United States Code.” No argument date is set.

The second front — and this one is already final. Two coordinated interim final rules will move export jurisdiction over suppressors for non-automatic and semiautomatic rifles, pistols, and shotguns off the State Department’s U.S. Munitions List and onto the Commerce Control List, ending their treatment under ITAR as defense articles. Most provisions take effect November 20, 2026. The American Suppressor Association, which pushed the change for over a decade, says it opens established foreign commercial markets to U.S. makers; the rules also make qualifying suppressors eligible for Commerce’s Baggage license exception, which matters to Americans traveling abroad to hunt. Exports still need Commerce authorization and country/end-user screening still applies — the change is the framework, not the approval.

The clocks are now short. New Jersey’s July 31 effective date is three days out (T-3), Colorado’s permit-to-purchase regime starts Aug 1 (T-4) alongside National Shooting Sports Month, and the ATF “engaged in the business” comment deadline is Aug 4 (T-7), the same day NSSF’s Import/Export Conference opens — which the suppressor rule just made a much more interesting agenda. The empowerment lane holds: exact dates, plain-language “what changes and when,” and comply-until-the-date discipline. Structural litigation news like Squire gets explained, not predicted.

Rep. Burchett’s Protection Against Mass Surveillance Act (H.R. 9800) opens a Fourth-Amendment front that lands squarely on gun owners

Introduced July 21 and referred to House Oversight and Government Reform, H.R. 9800 would bar federal agencies from buying, deploying, operating, accessing, or contracting for automated license-plate-reader networks — Flock Safety is named explicitly — along with facial recognition and biometric identification systems used for persistent or mass surveillance. It would also bar state, local, and tribal governments from spending federal funds on the same systems, require deletion of non-compliant data within 30 days, and make that data inadmissible in court. The gun-owner relevance is direct: plate readers log every vehicle passing a gun store, gun show, or range, and AmmoLand documents four separate cases of officers running ex-partners’ plates dozens to 179 times before anyone noticed — in every publicly reported instance, the victim discovered it, not an internal audit. The NRA has flagged the pairing of ALPR networks with device-signal collection as a specific gun-owner privacy threat. Legislative reality check: the bill currently has zero cosponsors, and Flock and Motorola Solutions together reported millions in recent federal lobbying. This is a framing and education story, not a bill with a path.

RTB angle: This is an untapped lane and a natural fit — “your lawful gun ownership shouldn’t create a permanent record of where you drive.” The strongest asset is a plain-language explainer on what plate readers actually capture and what recourse exists, with the documented misuse cases named and sourced. Keep it Fourth-Amendment-framed and non-partisan: privacy skepticism about ALPR networks is genuinely cross-ideological. Do not overstate the bill’s prospects.

New ▲▲ Courts · 5th Circuit Heat88▲▲The Reload analysis · petition + dissent quoted, CourtListener-linked

U.S. v. Squire goes en banc — and the question is not the Second Amendment, it is whether Congress ever had the power

On Monday, July 20, the Fifth Circuit voted to rehear United States v. Squire en banc. Curtis Squire, a Louisiana man convicted under § 922(g)(1) for possessing a handgun after prior felonies including heroin dealing, lost before a three-judge panel on June 2. His rehearing petition then made a deliberate strategic move: it set aside Second Amendment claims entirely and attacked § 922(g)(1) under the Commerce Clause, arguing that United States v. Lopez forbids federal regulation of noneconomic conduct and that “the only connection to commerce was that the firearm and ammunition that Mr. Squire possessed in his own home happened to have been manufactured outside the state… That’s it.” Why this is different in kind: a successful Second Amendment challenge frees one defendant, while a successful Commerce Clause challenge could invalidate the felon-in-possession ban on its face. Every appeals court to consider such a challenge has so far upheld § 922 — until Justice Thomas’s solo Hemani concurrence last month said the prohibited-person categories “appear[] to exceed Congress’s enumerated power” and that lower courts “should revisit” them. Judges Ho, Smith, Engelhardt, Willett, and Duncan have all previously written along these lines. Judge Higginson, the only recorded dissenter from the en banc grant, wrote that the majority risks “imperil[ing] much of the United States Code,” asking what would become of federal statutes on explosives, biological weapons, financial transactions, and intellectual property. No argument date has been scheduled. (The vote breakdown was not released; the en banc grant does not indicate an outcome.)

RTB angle: This is a “teach, don’t predict” story and it is a genuinely good one — the Commerce Clause is the least understood and most load-bearing piece of federal gun law. A short explainer on why “this gun crossed a state line once” became the hook for federal jurisdiction, and what an enumerated-powers challenge is, differentiates RTB from outlets that will run this as “court may free felons.” Present Higginson’s objections fairly — they are the strongest argument against, and members deserve to see both. State plainly that an en banc grant decides nothing.

New ▲▲▲ Regulatory · State/Commerce Heat87▲▲▲AmmoLand + ASA · Federal Register text not yet reviewed

Civilian suppressors leave the U.S. Munitions List: export jurisdiction moves from State to Commerce, effective Nov 20

Two coordinated interim final rules transfer export jurisdiction over suppressors for non-automatic and semiautomatic rifles, pistols, and shotguns from the State Department’s U.S. Munitions List and ITAR to the Commerce Control List and the Export Administration Regulations. Most provisions take effect November 20, 2026. This completes an inconsistency left open in 2020, when many non-automatic and semiautomatic firearms moved to Commerce jurisdiction but suppressors stayed behind under the defense-article framework. Commerce says the transfer reduces regulatory burden while keeping oversight tied to destination, end user, and national-security and foreign-policy screening, and estimates roughly 200 additional license applications annually; prohibited-country and questionable-end-user restrictions remain, and most exports still require authorization on case-by-case review. Two consumer-relevant details: qualifying suppressors become eligible for Commerce’s Baggage license exception, which allows citizens and permanent residents to temporarily take certain firearms and accessories abroad for lawful hunting or sporting use (destination-country law still governs); and ASA Executive Director Knox Williams frames the reform as access to “thriving, well-established international commercial markets.” (Reported via AmmoLand/ASA; the Federal Register documents themselves were not retrievable this run — confirm rule numbers and effective dates against federalregister.gov before publishing specifics.)

RTB angle: The best genuinely good news in weeks, and it should be written as such — a policy category finally being described accurately. Two strong pieces: a plain explainer of what changes Nov 20 and what does not (this is not “suppressors are deregulated”), and the honest hearing-protection framing — roughly 20–35 dB of reduction, comparable to good muffs, not Hollywood silence. Pair it with the domestic contrast: treated as a commercial product for export, still an NFA item at home. Verify the Federal Register citations before any date claim goes out.

New ▲ Courts · W.D. Louisiana Heat57▲AmmoLand (Crump) · complaint not independently reviewed

FPC’s Pool v. Hodges: Louisiana lets 18-year-olds carry without a permit, then withholds the permit that makes carrying workable

FPC filed Pool v. Hodges on July 22 in the Western District of Louisiana, challenging La. R.S. § 40:1379.3(C)(4), which bars concealed-carry permits for residents aged 18–20. Plaintiffs are Ella Pool (19), Christian McDuffie (18), and FPC; the defendant is State Police Superintendent Col. Robert P. Hodges. The paradox is the point: Louisiana is a permitless-carry state, so 18-to-20-year-olds may carry — but both the federal Gun-Free School Zones Act (18 U.S.C. § 922(q)) and Louisiana’s parallel 1,000-foot rule exempt only state permit holders. With more than 1,300 schools statewide, the unmarked 1,000-foot buffers overlap across roads and daily travel routes, especially in cities. Louisiana also extends civil immunity for justified defensive force only to licensed carriers, so a young adult who lawfully defends themselves under permitless carry can still be sued. The complaint leans on the Fifth Circuit’s binding Reese v. ATF holding that peaceable 18-to-20-year-olds are part of “the People,” plus Worth v. Jacobson (8th Cir.); FPC moved for summary judgment with the filing. (Single-source; docket and complaint not independently reviewed.)

RTB angle: The civil-immunity gap is the RTB-relevant fact and almost no one will cover it: in Louisiana, whether a lawful defensive shooting leaves you exposed to a civil suit currently turns on a permit that one group of adults cannot obtain. That is a legal-exposure story, not a politics story. Broaden it into the evergreen version — “permitless carry does not mean permit-free consequences” — because the same asymmetry exists in other states. Verify the Louisiana statutes directly.

Watch ▲ Courts · SCOTUS Heat73▲AmmoLand (Mark W. Smith) · projections are the author’s

Viramontes/Grant: the roadmap is public, and the argument date projection has slipped to December

Mark Smith’s July 27 piece is the most concrete public framework yet for the consolidated AR-15 cases (Viramontes v. Cook County, No. 25-238, from the Seventh Circuit; Grant v. Higgins, No. 25-566, from the Second Circuit; one hour of argument). Five things he flags: (1) Mootness is unlikely — unlike NYSRPA v. City of New York (2020), no legislature will legalize AR-15s to escape review, and Viramontes pleaded nominal damages, which survives repeal. (2) Watch for a Solicitor General amicus supporting the challengers, with no federal statute analogous to either ban. (3) Amicus quality matters — methodology, social-science, and Founding-era-arms history briefs. (4) Timing: Smith counts the cases roughly 22nd and 23rd in the OT2026 grant sequence and projects argument in the first week of December, with a decision no earlier than late June 2027 — later than the “October” expectation carried in earlier editions of this brief. (5) The real fight is methodology: whether arms-ban cases are governed by Heller’s common-use standard or a freestanding historical inquiry. Maine: Troy Jackson replaces Platner as the likely Democratic nominee — and the race’s gun-policy contrast widens sharply

Former Maine Senate President Troy Jackson has secured enough delegate support to become the Democratic nominee against Sen. Susan Collins, after Graham Platner withdrew roughly two weeks ago over misconduct allegations. The gun-policy shift is substantial and documented. Jackson’s earlier record: he voted against a waiting period, against expanded background checks on many private sales, and against a rate-of-fire device ban, was NRA-endorsed at one point, and held an A+ rating from the Sportsman’s Alliance of Maine as recently as 2022. After the 2023 Lewiston shooting his positions changed — he wrote “I’ve evolved because Lewiston showed us the cost of doing nothing” in a 2025 op-ed backing the state’s Red Flag ballot initiative, led the push that passed Maine’s 72-hour waiting period and expanded private-sale checks (signed April 2024), and last month backed new restrictions on bump stocks, “assault weapons,” and “high-capacity” magazines at a Maine Gun Safety Coalition rally. Platner, by contrast, had rejected AR-15 bans “in the form that they have been put forward” and called the waiting period “a bit excessive.” Collins has opposed “assault weapons” bans throughout her career but now supports banning “high-capacity” magazines and bump stocks; all three back some form of Red Flag law, and Collins co-sponsored a federal bill encouraging state adoption. One UNH poll has Jackson leading Collins, and Maine is among Democrats’ best Senate flip opportunities. Polling also continues to rank guns low among voter priorities.
