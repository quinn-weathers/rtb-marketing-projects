Top of mind10 · 13

The NFA story moves to the states: 15 states still make suppressors and short-barreled guns legal only if they’re federally registered, and a gun-rights group has asked each one to stop enforcing that link. Federal paperwork is paused; state paperwork may still be a felony.

What happened. Per TTAG (10/13, read in full), Firearms Policy Coalition lawyers sent letters on 9 October to the attorneys general of Alaska, Colorado, Connecticut, Georgia, Michigan, Mississippi, Montana, Nevada, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Washington and Wisconsin. Each of those states ties the legality of suppressors, SBRs, SBSs or similar items to compliance with the federal NFA registration process. FPC asks each AG to stop enforcing those provisions now and to work with legislators to repeal them; it says it hopes not to need to sue. States that ban these items outright, such as New Jersey, are not on the list; neither ATF’s policy nor the court injunction touches them.

Why it is a trap for members. ATF’s 9 October guidance says it does not override state law, and, per TTAG, ATF won’t say whether an unregistered item counts as “lawfully held under federal law” for state purposes. That’s why ATF still accepts voluntary registrations. A member in one of these 15 states could buy or build a suppressor or SBR with no federal paperwork and still face state charges. Local coverage is already catching up state by state: Washington (Seattle Red, The Center Square, 10/12–10/13), Louisiana (wildlife agents’ suppressor guidance for hunting season, 10/12–10/13), Idaho (Idaho Statesman, KBOI), Alaska (Must Read Alaska). All headline-level except TTAG.

Also settled today. The case name Monday flagged as unreconciled: Judge James Wesley Hendrix’s 5 August ruling came in Silencer Shop Foundation v. ATF, consolidated with the FPC-backed Jensen v. ATF (TTAG, read). Both names were right.

Why it matters for RTB. This turns Monday’s “check your state” advice into a concrete list. The empowering message: if you live in one of these 15 states, keep doing the federal paperwork until your state changes its law or its attorney general says otherwise in writing. A state-by-state lookup (with the 15 named) is a practical, shareable asset, and no coverage peer has published one (peer watch, section 04). Not legal advice; avoid predicting how any AG will respond.

What could bring NFA paperwork back: Congress is one of three switches

A Daily Caller explainer (10/12, syndicated by AOL and BizPac Review; headline-level) asks what could revive the suppressor rules. ATF’s own FAQ (as quoted by The Reload and TTAG, both read) names three: Congress reimposes a tax on these items, the injunction is overturned, or the Department rescinds its policy. TTAG adds that ATF has made no promise to grandfather unregistered items if enforcement resumes. No bill to reimpose a tax has been reported, and Congress is out until after the election. The stopgap funding law (Public Law 119-103) runs to 11 December, T−59.

RTB angle“Keep your records” is the evergreen advice here. The rules changed by policy, and policy can change again. Keep receipts, serial numbers and dates. Neutral framing; we don’t name members or forecast votes.
