Top of mind10 · 30

Two cities moved in opposite directions on concealed carry this week. Portland ended the license-holder exemption in city meeting buildings; the Justice Department gave Glendale, California, until 4 November to drop carry-permit costs reported at about $2,000.

What happened. Portland: the City Council voted (reported 10/28–29) to bar concealed handguns, including those carried by concealed-handgun licensees, from City Hall and certain city buildings where public meetings are held. Eight outlets carried it (KGW, KATU, KPTV, KOIN, OregonLive, FOX 10, Yahoo, Grants Pass Tribune); OregonLive framed the debate as a “good guy with a gun” argument after Bend. Glendale: USA Carry, Townhall and Breitbart (10/29) report that DOJ’s Civil Rights Division told the city it will be sued unless it stops charging what the outlets describe as about $2,000 to obtain a concealed-carry license, with a 4 November deadline. It follows DOJ’s 23 October investigations of permit delays in Waterbury and Middletown, Connecticut.

What is not confirmed. We could not open any of these articles, the Portland ordinance or the DOJ letter. Which buildings are covered, the effective date and signage rules in Portland, and how Glendale’s reported $2,000 breaks down (fees, training, psychological exam, or other costs) are all unverified. Headlines only.

Also this morning. A DOJ court filing says ATF employees will be among its Election Day poll monitors, unarmed and without ATF insignia (The Reload, read; Section 03). Massachusetts votes on Question 9 in four days; Friday’s ballot sweep is in Section 02. Everytown’s Victory Fund has passed $34.7 million for the cycle (Section 06). Bend, day fifteen: coverage turns to recovery and fundraising (Section 05).

Why it matters for RTB. “Where can I carry?” and “what does a permit cost?” are the two questions members ask most, and both changed locally this week. A calm “check the building before you go” explainer (Portland as the example: posted city buildings, a license is not an exemption) and a short note on permit costs varying by city serve members without taking sides. No forecast on Glendale. Not legal advice.

New ▲ ATF · Election Day · DOJ filing Heat75▲▲ 10/28 filing · The Reload read 10/30 · ~8 outlets

DOJ says ATF employees will serve among its Election Day poll monitors, unarmed and without ATF insignia

In a 28 October response to an NAACP records lawsuit, DOJ’s Voting Section said employees of the Bureau of Prisons, ATF and DEA will serve as election monitors (The Reload, read). Per the filing as quoted: monitors may not carry firearms, wear agency insignia or make arrests; they observe and take notes, must introduce themselves to local election officials, and may not talk with voters inside the polling place. DOJ declined to list locations. Separately, DOJ said FBI and ICE agents will not serve as monitors (The Hill, Newsweek, KFI). A former DOJ official quoted by The Reload called ATF’s role unusual but said monitors who disrupt can be removed. Newsweek (10/30) reports monitors being barred from some polling places (headline; details unread). We read The Reload’s account, not the filing itself.

RTB angleNot a firearms-law change; keep it out of member messaging beyond plain facts. If members ask: monitors are observers, unarmed by rule, and state polling-place laws still govern. This is also a reminder that many polling places are gun-free by state law; a nonpartisan “know your state’s polling-place rules” note fits. Not legal advice.
