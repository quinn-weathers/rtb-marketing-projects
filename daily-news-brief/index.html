Top of mind10 · 23

New York now requires gun owners to lock up firearms that are not under their immediate control, and has expanded checks on ammunition buyers. Governor Hochul signed the package Wednesday evening, the same week a federal court struck down the state’s body-armor ban.

What happened. Per the New York State Senate’s release (10/22, read), the governor signed Francesco’s Law, which requires that all firearms be secured with a locked container, a trigger lock or another safety device when they are not under the owner’s immediate control. Penalties escalate when the owner knows a minor or a person prohibited from having guns is likely to gain access, and a Class A misdemeanor applies when such a person is likely to gain access and actually does. The law is named for a 17-year-old who died in 2021 after gaining access to an unsecured firearm. Regional outlets (WHEC, News12, RochesterFirst, Post Journal; headlines) report that the signing also expanded ammunition background checks to stop prohibited buyers; we did not read the text of that bill.

What is not confirmed. The release did not give an effective date, the bill numbers, or the exact definition of “immediate control.” We read the sponsors’ release, not the statute, and no outlet we could open detailed the ammunition provisions. Nine-plus outlets carried the signing: high salience on the pickup proxy, plus a one-time institution credit for the signed law.

Also this morning. Delaware sued Glock under the state’s firearm-industry accountability law, alleging that its pistols are too easily converted into illegal machine guns; the state seeks an order barring civilian sales of “easily convertible” pistols in Delaware. Sales to law enforcement are not affected (Delaware DOJ release, read; Section 04). Massachusetts: Question 9 enters its final ten days with new guides and ads; the voter-registration deadline is Saturday (Section 02, with the Friday ballot-measure sweep).

Why it matters for RTB. Responsible storage is now a legal duty for every New York gun owner, not only a best practice. That is the empowerment message in its plainest form: a short, practical “what New York’s storage law asks of you” explainer (what counts as secured, when the misdemeanor applies, and what is still unknown, such as the start date) helps members comply with confidence. It pairs naturally with the general secure-storage piece recommended Thursday. Keep the tone practical and respectful of the law’s namesake; do not reference the family’s loss in marketing. Not legal advice.

Manufacturers, retail, legal-coverage market
New ▲ Manufacturers · litigation · Delaware Heat76▲▲ Delaware DOJ release read 10/22 · 4 outlets

Delaware sues Glock under its firearm-industry accountability law, alleging the company’s pistols are too easily converted into illegal machine guns

Per the Delaware Department of Justice (read), Attorney General Kathleen Jennings filed a civil suit against Glock, Inc. and its Austrian parent under the Keshall “KeKe” Anderson Safe Firearm Sales Act. The state alleges that Glock knew its design allows cheap conversion devices (“switches”) to make pistols fire automatically, declined to redesign, and marketed content that glorified automatic fire. It asserts five causes of action, including the industry-accountability statute and public nuisance, and seeks an injunction barring distribution of “easily convertible” pistols to civilians in Delaware, plus abatement, restitution and damages. The release says sales to law enforcement are unaffected. These are allegations; no court has ruled, and we have seen no response from Glock or NSSF. Coverage: WBOC, The News Journal, CoastTV, WGMD (headlines).

RTB angleExplain the mechanism, not the fight. Members will ask whether they can still buy or keep a Glock in Delaware. The honest answer today: nothing changes unless a court grants the injunction, and the suit does not target existing owners. Conversion devices are already illegal under federal and Delaware law, which is worth restating plainly. No commentary on the merits. Not legal advice.
