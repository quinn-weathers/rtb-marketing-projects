Top of mind10 · 16

Three children and a teacher were killed at an elementary school in Bend, Oregon, on Thursday, before a community member who followed the 16-year-old attacker inside shot and stopped him. Police say the attacker was dead within minutes of the first 911 call.

What happened. Per the Associated Press (updated 10/16, read), a 16-year-old Bend resident shot his way through locked doors at Silver Rail Elementary shortly before 1 p.m. and killed three children, aged 5, 8 and 11, and a teacher. A 7-year-old and a 54-year-old teacher are in critical condition. Bend’s police chief said a person at the scene saw the suspect enter, pursued him into the school and shot him; officers arrived within minutes and found the suspect down. A law-enforcement official told AP the attacker used a pump-action shotgun bought by his mother. Oregon’s governor thanked the civilian, saying the act carries “a heavy emotional burden.” All 32 Bend-La Pine schools are closed Friday. AP counts it as the 28th mass killing in the U.S. this year.

What is not yet confirmed. The intervener has not been publicly identified. Fox News, citing federal sources, reported he held a valid concealed handgun license, and the New York Post, citing dispatch audio, that he was a student’s father; neither is confirmed by police in what we read. AmmoLand (read) and Newsweek (headline) report that the district’s policy bars concealed-license holders from carrying on school property. Authorities have not said how that rule applies here, and no charging decision has been reported; the investigation is ongoing.

The policy debate has already started. Members of Oregon’s congressional delegation called for gun-law changes (KGW, Fox News headlines); The New York Times notes that Oregon’s recently approved gun laws have been held up in the courts (headline). 2A-native outlets (AmmoLand, TTAG, HotAir) frame the day around the school’s no-carry policy and the civilian’s response time. Both lines of argument will run through the final 18 days of the campaign.

Why it matters for RTB. This is a grief story first. Recommendation: pause scheduled promotional and membership-drive posts for the weekend, and do not use the event, the victims or the intervener in marketing. When the team does speak, the honest, empowerment-led point is the one the governor made: a lawful defensive act is followed by an investigation, legal questions and a personal toll, and preparation (training, knowing the rules where you carry, and support for afterwards) matters before the moment, not after. Not legal advice; no one named.

Moved ▶ NFA-linked state rules · Florida, Maryland, Pennsylvania Heat85▲ letter 10/11 · reported 10/13–15 · Florida item read

A Florida State Attorney tells local police that short-barreled firearms and suppressors are no longer presumed illegal without ATF registration

Per The Reload (10/15, read), State Attorney Brian S. Kramer wrote to police chiefs and sheriffs on 11 October that Florida’s short-barreled-firearm statute (§ 790.221) exempts items “lawfully owned and possessed under provisions of federal law,” and that Florida law does not itself require a Form 1, Form 4, tax stamp or registry entry. Citing Silencer Shop v. ATF, DOJ’s decision not to appeal and ATF’s guidance, he concluded that the absence of federal registration alone no longer shows unlawful possession. Scope caution: a State Attorney is an elected regional prosecutor, not the statewide Attorney General; The Reload headlines it as Florida’s position and the Alachua Chronicle (10/13, headline) covers it locally. Whether other circuits or the state follow was not reported. Elsewhere: Maryland outlets report that state’s requirements are unchanged (The BayNet, Eastern Shore Undercover; headlines); GOA calls on Pennsylvania’s attorney general to act (GOA, headline); Louisiana coverage continues (WNTZ).

03ATF, courts & regulatory

Rulings, rules, enforcement, dockets
New ▲ Third Circuit en banc · 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) Heat76▲▲ decided 10/13 · 8 outlets · opinions not read directly

Full Third Circuit: the federal felon ban can’t disarm a man for life over a 2005 DUI; a companion case upholds disarmament for violent threats

Per AmmoLand (10/15, read): in Williams v. Attorney General the en banc court ruled 8–6 that the government could not constitutionally keep disarming Edward Williams, whose 2005 Pennsylvania DUI was a misdemeanor but carried a possible five-year sentence, which is what triggers the federal ban. The majority found that historical laws on habitual drunkenness involved temporary confinement or guardianship, not lifetime bans, and noted his two decades without violence and an expert finding of no addiction. It rejected disarming people as “irresponsible” by the government’s own definition. In United States v. Bost (12–2) the court upheld a conviction tied to an earlier terroristic-threats conviction. The case builds on the circuit’s Range line. Late find, not a new event: decided 13 October; it was a standing assignment and is now read through a detailed secondary account. The government’s next step (rehearing is exhausted; certiorari is possible) was not reported.

RTB angleThe “prohibited person you didn’t know you were” story. Old convictions with long maximum sentences can trigger the federal ban even when the state calls them misdemeanors. That is a practical check every member can do. The ruling applies in the Third Circuit (PA, NJ, DE), to this person’s circumstances. Not legal advice.
