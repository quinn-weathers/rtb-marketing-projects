Top of mind10 · 08

Scam ads selling a “national concealed carry permit” that does not exist have spent more than $3.1 million on Facebook and Instagram, and they are aimed at seniors and veterans. The same people RTB serves are being sold a fake product with fear.

What happened. A report from the Institute for Strategic Dialogue (read), a research group that tracks online manipulation, reviewed Meta’s Ad Library from July to September 2026. It found 22 pages promoting concealed carry “permits” that can supposedly be bought online, with more than $3.1 million in ad spend and at least 24.8 million impressions. Many ads claim a “new 2026 law” created a 50-state permit. No such law exists. Several used AI-generated likenesses of law-enforcement officers, national officials and celebrities, and leaned on fear: terrorism, home invasions, “seniors alert.” ISD links at least 10 pages to one network (shared domains, hosting and shared-office addresses) and says Meta removed many ads but enforced unevenly; some pages were still running ads as of 2 September.

What it is not. This is about fake permit sellers, not about legal-coverage memberships or any named company in our category. The figures come from Meta’s own ad-library estimates, which ISD says are approximate. The report is dated 1 October and entered the news feeds on 10/7, so it is a late find, not a new event.

Why it matters for RTB. Three things. First, a simple, true message RTB can own: carry permits are issued by states, there is no national permit, and nobody can sell you one online in five minutes. This week’s Denver, Minnesota and national-park stories already show how much the rules vary by state. Second, a “how to spot a carry scam” piece for seniors and veterans is educational, protective and squarely on-brand. Third, check our own Meta ads against what ISD flagged: a clear advertiser identity, no AI likenesses of real officials, no “new law” claims, no fear hooks. If Meta tightens review of concealed-carry ads after this report, honest advertisers could face more approval friction too (our inference, not ISD’s finding).

New ▲ California · San Diego County · 3D printing complaint dated 10/5 · 1 outlet

San Diego County sues filament maker Polymaker, arguing its marketing to gun printers turns plastic into a regulated “precursor part”

Per AmmoLand (10/7, read), San Diego’s County Counsel, suing in the name of the People of California, filed in San Diego Superior Court against Polymaker LLC, a Texas-based 3D-printer filament company. The single claim is under California’s Unfair Competition Law. The theory: filament marketed to people printing gun frames becomes a “firearm precursor part” under state law. The complaint also invokes California’s 2026 law against aiding unlicensed manufacture with a 3D printer and its firearm-industry “reasonable controls” duty. It cites creator sponsorships, affiliate deals and gun-themed product marketing. It says it is not trying to ban filament. It seeks an injunction, disgorgement and penalties of up to $2,500 per violation. Giffords Law Center is co-counsel. AmmoLand’s account is openly critical of the suit; the complaint itself was not read here.

RTB angleA legal-education point, not a product story. In California, home-building a firearm without a state manufacturing licence carries real legal exposure, and the state is now reaching suppliers too. No comment on the company or the outcome.

New ▲ D. Md. · SB 334 · “convertible pistol” ban Heat70▲▲ opinion 10/5 · late find · 3 outlets

A federal judge declines to block Maryland’s ban on “machinegun convertible pistols”, a week after Connecticut’s version was put on hold

Per the Daily Caller (10/7, read), Senior U.S. District Judge Richard D. Bennett issued a written opinion on Monday 5 October denying a preliminary injunction against SB 334, the Maryland law signed in May that restricts sales of pistols the state labels “machinegun convertible.” The opinion formalises an earlier bench ruling that let the law take effect. As quoted, the court found the law does regulate conduct covered by the Second Amendment’s text, but that the plaintiffs had not shown a likelihood of success at Bruen’s historical-tradition step. Plaintiffs include FPC and the Second Amendment Foundation; SAF told the paper it expects to win on appeal, but no appeal filing has been reported. BizPac Review (headline) and FPC’s own post also reported the ruling. The opinion itself was not read here.

RTB angleThe status line now differs by state. Maryland: in effect. Connecticut: on hold pending the case. California: a judge declined a temporary restraining order in July (per The Reload). Both rulings are preliminary and appealable. Point members to their state’s own guidance on what is covered; no buy-or-sell advice.

Moved ▶ 5th Cir. · FPC v. ATF · under-21 handgun sales Heat65▲ DOJ brief reported 10/7 · no ATF guidance

DOJ tells the Fifth Circuit it will not enforce the federal ban on dealer handgun sales to 18-to-20-year-olds “against anyone, anywhere”

According to TTAG (10/7, read), a DOJ brief in FPC v. ATF says the Office of Legal Counsel’s 17 September opinion, which concluded the federal restriction on licensed dealers selling handguns to adults under 21 is unconstitutional and “may not be enforced,” makes the case moot, and asks the court to dismiss it rather than decide how far the existing injunction should reach. TTAG quotes the brief: the decision not to enforce applies “against anyone, anywhere.” The brief’s filing date was not given, and the brief itself was not read here. The Federal Register shows no ATF documents since 6 October, so there is still no published dealer guidance.

RTB angleAn enforcement position, not a repeal. The statute is still on the books, many states set their own age limits, and individual dealers may still decline. A future administration could change course. Don’t tell 18-to-20-year-olds they can now buy a handgun anywhere.

New ▲ Self-defense verdict · Portland, Ore. · transit verdict (Aug.) · sentencing reported 10/7 · 3 outlets

A Portland jury acquitted a 71-year-old train rider of attempted murder in a self-defense shooting, but convicted him of illegally possessing the gun

Per The Oregonian (read), the man shot a stranger once in the thigh on a late-night MAX train in January after, he testified, the man exposed himself, threatened to rob him and sexually assault his partner, and then advanced on them despite a shouted warning. Prosecutors argued he could have used the train’s call button or alerted security officers who had been aboard. The jury acquitted him of attempted murder and assault after an August trial, but convicted him of being a felon in possession of a firearm because of past convictions. The judge imposed about two years in prison, saying he would not punish the shooting itself. The New York Post and a third outlet also covered it. We are not naming him.

RTB angleSelf-defense is judged one charge at a time. The jury cleared the shooting, but the separate question of whether he could legally have the gun still carried prison time. It also shows what prosecutors ask about: alternatives, warnings, and what you did afterwards. Educational, not sensational.
