New ▲ Ohio · SB 214 · awaiting governor Heat81▲ Passed week of 11/10 · AP via Fox 9 and Local 12 read · first carried today

Ohio SB 214, taking suppressors and short-barreled rifles and shotguns out of “dangerous ordnance,” awaits the governor

New to the brief; the bill passed the week of 10 November but was not carried in Thursday’s or Monday’s editions. Per the AP-credited story (Fox 9, read), the bill removes the state-law requirement tied to federal registration for suppressors and short-barreled rifles and shotguns; it passed mostly along party lines with a handful of Democratic votes. Ohio’s Attorney General had urged lawmakers last month to address the remaining state requirements. On 13 November the Hamilton County sheriff urged a veto, saying it could make policing harder (Local 12, read). The governor’s position is not reported. If signed, it would generally take effect 90 days later (Local 12). TheGunMag (read) says it was sent to the governor on 10 November; Ohio’s constitution generally allows ten days, Sundays excepted, which would point to about 21 November. That deadline is our reading and unverified.

RTB angleNot law until signed, and not in effect for about 90 days after. Ohio members will see “suppressors legal” headlines; the accurate version is that federal NFA rules still apply and the state change is pending. A short “what SB 214 would and wouldn’t change” explainer, published after the governor acts, is the empowering move. Not legal advice.

North Carolina’s permitless-carry override (SB 50) is on the House calendar for the lame-duck session; no vote outcome found as of this run

WITN (11/16, read) lists SB 50 among four vetoed bills on the House calendar and puts the bar at three-fifths of members present and voting: 72 if all 120 vote, against 71 Republican seats. Carolina Journal (11/13, read) calls the outcome uncertain, noting some Republicans have opposed the bill and that attendance changes the math. Besides permitless carry, WITN notes the bill raises line-of-duty death benefits and creates scholarships for children of fallen officers.

Moved ▶ Arizona · new majorities · red-flag proposal Heat73▲ KJZZ read 11/17 · Arizona Capitol Times headline 11/16

Arizona’s new Democratic majorities are exploring a red-flag (“severe threat order of protection”) bill for the session that opens in January

KJZZ (Capitol Media Services, 11/17, read) reports that with one party now holding the governor’s office and both chambers, sponsors are exploring a version of the red-flag plan a former governor proposed nearly a decade ago. The 2025 model would let certain adults petition a court for an order requiring firearms be surrendered, with a hearing within 14 days and possible extension up to a year; an earlier version allowed brief pre-hearing detention, which the 2025 bill dropped. Details are still being negotiated with Giffords. The Arizona Citizens Defense League opposes ex parte orders on due-process and Fourth Amendment grounds; the state’s Attorney General says orders would not be permanent and the bar would be high. Arizona Capitol Times (11/16, headline) reports more gun bills could get hearings. Nothing has been filed. This replaces Monday’s six-state row; the other five states (Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin) have agenda talk but no new filings read, apart from Michigan below.

RTB angleExplain the process, not the politics. Members in Arizona will hear “red flag” and assume the worst. A neutral explainer on how protective orders work where they already exist (who can petition, notice, hearing, how to respond, why to call your attorney first) is useful in every state. No legislator names. Not legal advice.

Novotny v. Moore: Justice Thomas, respecting the denial, doubts Maryland’s sensitive-places law but says the Court should wait for a narrower case

The order list (read) shows cert denied in No. 25-1324 with a statement by Justice Thomas; no other justice is shown joining and no dissent is noted, which resolves the open question in Monday’s edition. He writes that at least some locations probably fail the historical test, describing a right that “admits of only historically grounded exceptions,” but agrees with denial because each statute implicates a different historical tradition. His statement cites Wolford v. Lopez (2026) and lower-court rulings including Antonyuk, and notes the Third Circuit vacated its Koons panel opinion for rehearing en banc. The Reload (read) frames it as Thomas calling for sensitive-places review. ABC (AP), NBC Washington and WSLS carried the denial (headlines); SCOTUSblog grouped it with the day’s other denials. AmmoLand’s “22 gun rights cases” count from Monday remains unverified; today’s read of the order list was limited to Novotny and gun-related items, and none of the 13 November relists (Balogh, Coleman) appeared in what was returned.

RTB angleRead it as a signal, not a ruling. For members, nothing changes in Maryland. For content, the useful takeaway is that sensitive-places rules vary by state and are still being litigated, so a “know before you go” checklist ages well. Avoid predicting what the Court will do. Not legal advice.

05National & cultural

Culture, events, public debate
New ▲ Arizona · self-defense determination · castle doctrine Announced Sat 11/14 · AZFamily read

Cochise County, Arizona declines charges against a homeowner after an August shooting, citing the state’s castle doctrine

The county sheriff’s office and county attorney announced on Saturday 14 November that no charges will be filed against a homeowner in Saint David over a 30 August fatal shooting (AZFamily, read). Officials said they reviewed Arizona’s castle doctrine, which allows a lawful occupant to use deadly force against someone attempting criminal trespass when a reasonable person would see it as immediately necessary, along with the state’s self-defense and defense-of-others rules, and found charges “not warranted” based on the findings and forensic evidence. The decision came about eleven weeks after the shooting. Single read outlet.

RTB angleThe review takes time even when the answer is no charges. Eleven weeks between incident and decision is a calm, factual way to show why access to an attorney matters from the first hour. Do not use the case’s details in marketing; speak to the process. Not legal advice.
