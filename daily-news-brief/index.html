Top of mind09 · 25

The full Ninth Circuit, 6–5, upheld Hawaii’s 30-day purchase-permit limit and its pre-possession police inspection — and did it by reading Bruen’s footnote nine as a carve-out for shall-issue licensing. Purchase-side rules, the theme of this month, now have a circuit-level test in the courts that cover Washington and California.

What happened. In Yukutake v. Lopez (No. 21-16756), an en banc panel of the Ninth Circuit held that Hawaii may require buyers of certain firearms to bring them to a police station for inspection before taking possession, and may limit purchase permits to 30 days. The opinion is dated Tuesday 22 September and was reported by The Reload on Thursday afternoon, after the 09/24 edition closed. It reverses a divided March 2025 panel that had struck both provisions down under Bruen’s history-and-tradition test. The majority instead treated objective, non-abusive “shall-issue” regimes as presumptively constitutional under Bruen’s footnote nine, and found these two requirements to be modest procedural burdens. Five judges dissented across two opinions, arguing acquisition is covered by the Second Amendment’s text and that Hawaii offered no matching historical analogue.

What it does and does not do. It is binding precedent for federal courts in the Ninth Circuit — Hawaii, California, Washington, Oregon, Nevada, Arizona, Alaska, Idaho and Montana. It does not reach Minnesota (Eighth Circuit) or any other state, and it changes no law anywhere: it lets two existing Hawaii rules stand. The majority also said what could cross the line — certain background checks, safety courses, lengthy waits or “exorbitant fees” — which is exactly the vocabulary of the live San Jose fee suit, the Minnesota delay letter and Washington’s $126-plus-training permit. No petition for Supreme Court review has been reported; The Reload notes the Court reversed the same circuit on a different Hawaii law (Wolford) in June, which is context, not a forecast. The court’s own PDF was located but could not be fetched here; the account rests on one outlet quoting it at length.

Why it matters for RTB. Members in the western states are about to hear “permit,” “shall-issue” and “footnote nine” a lot. The honest, useful piece is a plain-English explainer on how courts are treating the steps before you take a gun home — permits, waits, fees, inspections — as a different question from bans. It is fully sourceable today, it is non-partisan, and no coverage peer has touched it. Separately, it is the Friday ballot sweep: Ballotpedia answered for the first time since 09/09, but served a year-old snapshot (section 02).

New ▲ 9th Cir. en banc · Yukutake v. Lopez · 21-16756 Heat81▲▲▲ 6–5 · opinion dated 09/22 · reported 09/24

The full Ninth Circuit upholds Hawaii’s purchase-permit limits by reading Bruen’s footnote nine as a shall-issue carve-out

Per The Reload’s report, which quotes the opinion at length: the en banc majority held that Hawaii’s 30-day validity limit on purchase permits and its requirement to present certain newly acquired firearms at a police station for inspection impose only modest, procedural burdens and do not materially impede acquisition. Rather than run the full history-and-tradition analysis, the majority read Bruen’s footnote nine as making objective, non-abusive shall-issue licensing presumptively constitutional, while allowing that lengthy waits, exorbitant fees or certain other components could cross the line. It reverses a March 2025 three-judge panel. Dissents: the principal dissent, joined by four other judges, argues acquisition is covered by the Second Amendment’s plain text and that Hawaii showed no historical analogue; a separate solo dissent predicts Supreme Court reversal. No rehearing or certiorari filing has been reported. The ordinary deadline for a certiorari petition is 90 days from judgment — about 21 December — and is routinely extended.
