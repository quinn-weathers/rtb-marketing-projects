Top of mind10 · 14

The first state answers on the NFA: Mississippi’s attorney general says federal suppressor registration is now voluntary for Mississippians. She also flagged a second state law, on carrying a suppressor concealed, that is still on the books.

What happened. Per the Magnolia Tribune (10/13, read), Mississippi’s attorney general said on Monday that, after reviewing the court rulings and ATF’s 9 October guidance, it is her opinion that ATF Form 4 for suppressors is voluntary for Mississippians. Mississippi is one of the 15 states whose suppressor law refers back to federal law, and the first of FPC’s 15 to respond publicly (WTOK, WLBT and the Picayune Item carry the same news; headlines). She called ATF’s position guidance that “can easily be reversed” by a future administration, and said she will ask the 2027 legislature to remove the state law’s reference to federal law.

The detail members need. The same statement names Section 97-37-1, which makes concealed carry of “any muffler or silencer for any firearm” punishable by fines or jail; she will seek to amend it too. So in Mississippi today, buying a suppressor without federal paperwork may be fine in her view, while carrying one concealed is still a state offense. Elsewhere, signals are mixed and headline-level: Nevada’s rules “remain unclear” (KSNV, 10/14); a Florida state attorney says SBRs and suppressors are “no longer automatically illegal” there (Alachua Chronicle, 10/13; Florida is not on FPC’s list); Oklahoma outlets are working through AR-pistol questions (Z94). The American Suppressor Association says it will now work state by state on all 15 (AmmoLand 10/13, read).

And on the federal side, the floor is narrower than it looks. In a 12 October filing in the Texas case, DOJ told the court that ATF’s nationwide policy makes further clarification unnecessary, and argued that the injunction protects the plaintiff states, their agencies and subdivisions, and plaintiffs’ members and customers, not everyone who lives in those 15 states (AmmoLand 10/13, read). That is DOJ’s argument, not a ruling. For most buyers, protection today rests on ATF policy.

Why it matters for RTB. Yesterday’s advice holds and gets sharper: federal paperwork is paused by policy; your state’s law decides the rest, and “legal to buy” is not the same as “legal to carry.” The state-by-state NFA list proposed Tuesday now has its first entry with an official answer. Date-stamp it, attribute each entry to its source (an AG statement is an opinion, not a court ruling), and update as states respond. Not legal advice.

The durable fix for the NFA runs through Congress, and state officials are now saying so on the record

Mississippi’s attorney general called ATF’s position guidance that a future administration “can easily” reverse, and the Magnolia Tribune (read) recalls that she joined 20 other attorneys general in September urging Congress to repeal parts of the NFA. Tuesday’s three “switches” that could revive the paperwork (a new tax, a reversed injunction, a rescinded policy) still stand.

03ATF, courts & regulatory

Agency actions, litigation, the Supreme Court
Moved ▶ ATF · National Firearms Act Heat90▼ DOJ filing 10/12 · 46 items in 2 days · filing not read directly

DOJ argues the Texas injunction protects the plaintiff states and plaintiffs’ members, not every resident; ATF’s policy does the rest

Per AmmoLand (10/13, read), DOJ’s 12 October response in Silencer Shop Foundation v. ATF / Jensen v. ATF says ATF “will no longer enforce the challenged NFA provisions against anyone,” so clarifying the order’s reach “would have no real world impact.” It then disputes the plaintiff states’ reading: the injunction covers the states, their agencies and subdivisions, and the named plaintiffs’ members and customers, not every person or sale in the 15 states. DOJ also says adding a duty to issue guidance would change the judgment, and points to the states’ narrower description in Brown v. ATF (Missouri; status conference 22 October, T−8). These are arguments, not a ruling. Practical rules unchanged: Form 4473 and NICS still apply; interstate transport of SBRs and SBSs still needs Form 20 approval; machine guns and destructive devices are untouched. Federal Register: checked this run; no ATF documents published 6–14 October. ATF’s director also gave a Newsmax interview (10/14, headline).

RTB angleMembers should know which protection they have. For most people it’s an ATF policy, not a court order, and policies can change. Same records advice; no predictions about the court.

New ▲ Felon-in-possession · 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) Heat73▲▲ rulings 10/13 · 7+ outlets · opinions not read

Third Circuit: the federal felon gun ban can be applied for violent records, but not over a man’s old DUI convictions

Per Fox News (10/14, read) and Reuters (10/13, headline), the Third Circuit issued two rulings on Tuesday. It upheld the ban for a man with a 2002 carjacking-related record, holding that people convicted of crimes showing a risk of violent misuse of guns may presumptively be disarmed. In a second case it held the ban could not constitutionally be applied to a man whose triggering conviction was a drunk-driving offense from the early 2000s. Same day, per the Duke Center for Firearms Law (read), the Supreme Court denied 13 firearms-related petitions, eight of them felon-ban challenges, including a nonviolent-felon case (Johnson, 26-270) and one that Law360 and the Daily Caller describe as a cannabis-cultivation felony. Seven more felon-ban petitions arrived last week, and several are set for the 6 November conference.

RTB angleWho may lawfully own a gun now depends partly on which federal circuit you live in. The Third Circuit covers Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware and the Virgin Islands. Useful as a member-education point (“an old conviction? get advice before you buy”), never as a self-diagnosis tool. Not legal advice.
