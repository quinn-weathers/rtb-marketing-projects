Top of mind10 · 21

The Justice Department has added San Jose’s gun-owner liability insurance mandate to its lawsuit against the city. It is the first federal challenge we have seen to a rule that makes insurance a condition of owning a gun, and it lands squarely in RTB’s category.

What happened. Per The Truth About Guns (10/20, read), DOJ’s suit filed last month over San Jose’s concealed-carry permit costs (a $318 application fee plus $1,273 if the permit is issued, close to $2,000 all-in with state fees, fingerprinting, a psychological exam and training) has been amended to include the city’s 2022 insurance ordinance. In effect since 1 January 2023, it requires gun owners to hold a homeowner’s, renter’s or specialized gun liability policy covering losses from accidental, unintentional or negligent use of a firearm, plus a $25 annual fee that funds local violence-reduction programs. The amended complaint asks the court to declare both the permit costs and the insurance mandate unconstitutional and to permanently block them. DOJ argues that the Supreme Court’s recent Wolford v. Lopez decision undercuts an earlier court ruling that the insurance requirement does not touch the Second Amendment’s text.

What is not confirmed. TTAG is an advocacy-aligned outlet and the framing (“exorbitant,” “illegal”) is its own. We did not read the amended complaint; the filing date, court and case number were not in what we read, and no city response was reported. One outlet so far (baseline on the pickup proxy).

Also this morning: Bend students go back to class today. Silver Rail stays closed. Oregon Public Broadcasting (headline) reports investigators are examining the attacker’s possible connection to violent extremist groups; parents have started a school-safety petition (headline). No charging decision has been reported about anyone (Section 05).

Why it matters for RTB. Two different products, one confusing headline. The San Jose ordinance requires liability coverage for accidents and negligence; self-defense legal-coverage memberships exist for what happens after a defensive use of force. Expect members and prospects in California to ask whether one satisfies the other. A plain explainer (“what the San Jose rule requires, what is being challenged, and why liability insurance and self-defense legal coverage are not the same thing”) is an empowerment piece with a clear audience, and it is not tied to Bend. Until a court rules, the ordinance stays in force, so the guidance is “comply and follow the case.” Do not suggest any RTB plan meets the mandate unless legal and product confirm it. Bend guidance unchanged: keep Bend out of marketing; the general “two tracks” explainer can run from Thursday at the earliest. Not legal advice.

The ordinance. Passed in 2022 and in force since 1 January 2023, it requires gun owners in San Jose to carry a homeowner’s, renter’s or specialized gun liability policy covering death, injury or property damage from accidental, unintentional or negligent use of a firearm, and to pay a $25 annual fee that funds violence-reduction and safety nonprofits. DOJ argues that Wolford v. Lopez undercuts the earlier ruling that the insurance requirement does not implicate the Second Amendment’s plain text. Not in what we read: the amended complaint, the filing date, the court and the case number, and any response from the city.

Moved ▶ Waiting periods · New Mexico · Maine Heat80▲ SAF release read 10/20 · NSSF read · 8+ outlets

The New Mexico suit is NSSF v. Torrez, a nine-plaintiff challenge that asks the court to extend Ortega’s holding to everyone who passes a background check

Who is suing. Per SAF’s release (10/20, read), the plaintiffs are NSSF, the Second Amendment Foundation, the National Rifle Association, the New Mexico Firearms Industry Association, the New Mexico Shooting Sports Association, two retailers, an organization named Right to Bear Arms, and a private citizen. The suit targets HB 129 (2024), the seven-day wait after an approved background check. Why now. The Tenth Circuit held the law unconstitutional in Ortega v. Grisham (2025) and the district court entered a permanent injunction, but the state has said it will enforce the law against everyone except the named Ortega plaintiffs. The complaint, as SAF quotes it, seeks an order declaring HB 129 unconstitutional for buyers who pass the federal check and barring enforcement against the plaintiffs, their customers and members. Separately, Mountain States Legal Foundation, counsel in Ortega, says it is returning to court to end the wait statewide (headline only). The court and case number still were not in what we read. Maine: Tuesday follow-ups on the Supreme Court’s Beckwith denial (Seacoastonline, Yahoo, Legis1); the case continues in the lower courts.

RTB angleKnow your state’s wait before you need it. In New Mexico the seven-day wait is still being enforced for most buyers while this plays out; in Maine the 72-hour wait stands. Name-collision note: a plaintiff called “Right to Bear Arms” will appear in coverage. We believe it is unrelated to RTB; expect brand-monitoring alerts and social mentions to mix the two. Not legal advice.

New ▲ Massachusetts SJC · under-21 Heat74▲▲ CommonWealth Beacon read 10/21 · opinion not read

Massachusetts’ highest court, 5–2, upholds the law that bars people under 21 from a license to carry handguns or large-capacity firearms

Per CommonWealth Beacon (10/20, read), the Supreme Judicial Court ruled Tuesday in Commonwealth v. Thomson, a criminal case in which a 20-year-old without a license was convicted of carrying a handgun. The majority held he lacked standing to challenge the law as applied to him, because he never applied and was never denied, but could challenge it on its face; on the merits, restricting firearm possession by people “deemed too young to handle them safely outside of supervised settings” is consistent with the nation’s regulatory tradition, so the conviction stands. Two justices dissented, saying the case should have gone back to a lower court for fuller expert evidence and briefing. The same age restriction is part of the 2024 law that Question 9 asks voters to keep or repeal. Bloomberg Law and MassLive also covered it (headlines). We name no private party beyond the case caption.

RTB angleFederal and state answers on 18-to-20-year-olds keep diverging. Young adults in Massachusetts still cannot get a carry license, whatever happens federally. If we write for that audience, lead with what the law is today in their state. Not legal advice.
