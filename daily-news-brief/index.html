Top of mind10 · 26

The Justice Department has opened civil-rights investigations into how two Connecticut cities handle gun permits. It is the second such probe this month, after Pennsauken, New Jersey, and it treats slow or denied permits as a possible violation of residents’ rights.

What happened. Per CT Mirror (10/23, read), the DOJ Civil Rights Division announced it is investigating the firearms-permit “policies and practices” of Waterbury and Middletown. Certified letters to each city’s mayor and police chief say the department will examine whether local officials are “unnecessarily” delaying or denying permits, that it has “not reached any conclusions,” and that it will close the matter if it finds no pattern or practice of violating federal rights. In Connecticut, an applicant must obtain a local pistol permit from the town or city police before applying for the state permit, so local processing time decides how long the whole process takes. Waterbury says it will cooperate fully; Middletown had not responded; a Connecticut U.S. senator criticized the probe. More than ten outlets carried it over the weekend (Hartford Courant, WTNH, WFSB, FOX61, CT Insider, Patch, Hartford Business Journal and others).

What is not confirmed. We read the CT Mirror account, not DOJ’s release or letters. Neither the release nor the reporting says why these two cities were chosen, how long their applicants have waited, or whether more cities are next. Pennsauken (opened 10/14) has produced no public findings yet.

Also this morning. Ruger has suspended production of 19 product lines, including the LCR, SP101, GP100 and Bearcat revolvers effective immediately (Firearms News, read; Section 04). In New Mexico, the coalition challenging the seven-day wait has asked the court for a preliminary injunction (SAF release, read; Section 03). Massachusetts Question 9 enters its final week; mail-ballot requests are due tomorrow (Section 02).

Why it matters for RTB. A permit that takes too long is now something the federal government is willing to investigate. That is a plain empowerment message: know your state’s permit timeline, keep copies and dates of everything you submit, and follow up in writing if the clock runs past what the law allows. A short “how the permit process is supposed to work, and how to document yours” explainer fits RTB’s education role without commenting on the cities involved. Not legal advice.

DOJ opens pattern-or-practice investigations into gun-permit delays in Waterbury and Middletown, its second permit probe this month

Per CT Mirror (read), the Civil Rights Division sent certified letters to both cities saying it will investigate whether their permitting “policies and practices” unnecessarily delay or deny permits. Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon said in the department’s release that the right to bear arms is “not a luxury reserved for a privileged few.” The letters say no conclusions have been reached. Connecticut requires a local permit from town or city police before an applicant can seek the state permit. Waterbury’s mayor says the city will cooperate fully; Middletown did not respond to CT Mirror; the state attorney general’s office deferred to the cities. The Center Square framed the probe as about “waiting periods”; the letters, as reported, concern permit processing, not a statutory waiting period. This follows the 10/14 Pennsauken, New Jersey, investigation (no public findings yet) and AmmoLand’s commentary on New York City licensing delays (10/23), which is opinion, not an investigation.

RTB angleDocument your application. Members in permit states can protect themselves with simple habits: keep copies, note every date, and follow up in writing. A neutral explainer on how the permit process is supposed to work, and what to do when it stalls, is useful in every permit state. Do not characterize either city’s conduct; the investigation has made no findings. Not legal advice.

Moved ▶ Fifth Circuit · interstate handgun sales Duke analysis read 10/23 · 1 outlet

Interstate handgun ruling resurfaces with dates attached: the government has until 2 November to seek rehearing in Elite Precision v. ATF

A Duke Center for Firearms Law analysis (read) walks through the 18 September 2–1 panel decision holding that three provisions of 18 U.S.C. § 922 that together bar direct interstate handgun purchases violate the plaintiffs’ Second Amendment rights. It notes a split with the Second Circuit’s Vereen decision. The key dates: a rehearing petition is due by 2 November; if none is filed, the mandate issues about 9 November, and the ruling takes effect only then. Duke notes the administration may not seek further review. We last carried this item on 9 October with no coverage.

RTB angleNot yet in force. Today, buying a handgun from an out-of-state dealer still requires a transfer through a dealer in your home state. If the mandate issues, that could change for the plaintiffs and, depending on how ATF responds, possibly more broadly. Wait for the mandate before advising anyone. Not legal advice.
