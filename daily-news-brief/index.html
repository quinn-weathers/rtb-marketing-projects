Top of mind09 · 18

In a single day, the federal executive branch gave up two long-held positions against gun owners. The Justice Department’s own lawyers concluded that the federal ban on handgun sales to 18-to-20-year-old adults is unconstitutional — and the President ordered the Department not to appeal the ruling that gutted the National Firearms Act’s registration scheme. Neither is a statute and neither is a Supreme Court decision. Both are the government declining to defend its own law, which is a different and rarer thing.

First. On September 17 the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel issued a 49-page opinion, signed by Assistant Attorney General T. Elliot Gaiser, concluding that 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(b)(1) and (c)(1) “may not be enforced to impose criminal penalties on dealers” who sell handguns to otherwise law-abiding adults aged 18 to 20. Its reasoning tracks the Fifth Circuit’s Reese v. ATF: those adults are part of “the people,” handguns are protected arms, the right to keep arms includes acquiring them, and the government cannot evade the Amendment by criminalising the seller. It rejects the common-law “infancy doctrine” analogue, noting that 249 of more than 250 colonial and early state militia laws required service from men aged 18 to 20. What it is not: not a statute, not an injunction, not a Supreme Court ruling. The sections remain in the U.S. Code, the opinion does not say when or how ATF will change its instructions to dealers, it does not touch state under-21 laws, and federal courts remain split — a federal judge recently upheld Hawaii’s under-21 acquisition ban on exactly the infancy-doctrine theory this opinion rejects. Second. The President has directed the Justice Department not to appeal Silencer Shop Foundation v. ATF, in which Judge Wesley Hendrix (N.D. Tex.) held the NFA’s regulatory provisions unconstitutional as applied to suppressors, SBRs, SBSs and AOWs, on the ground that a $0 tax generates no revenue and so cannot rest on the taxing power. The non-appeal was reported by CBS News and confirmed by congressional sources, making this a two-outlet item. The relief is party-scoped, not nationwide — it reaches GOA and FRAC members and Silencer Shop, PSA, B&T and SilencerCo customers — ATF has issued no implementation guidance, and the White House states that everyone prohibited before the ruling is still prohibited now. Also today: a Maryland federal judge preliminarily enjoined Montgomery County’s place-of-worship gun ban as to one synagogue and one rabbi, and DOJ filed its third carry-permitting suit, against San Jose over roughly $1,600 in fees. Four new items. No peer move for an eighteenth day. And the Friday ballot sweep produced nothing new.

03ATF, courts & regulatory

Agencies, dockets, decisions — two executive-branch concessions today
New ▲ DOJ OLC · opinion issued 09/17 opinion, not law · state bans untouched Heat88▲

The Justice Department’s own lawyers concluded the federal under-21 handgun-sale ban is unconstitutional — and nothing a member may lawfully do changed today

Today’s lead, and the strongest document this board has carried in a fortnight. A 49-page Office of Legal Counsel opinion dated September 17, signed by Assistant Attorney General T. Elliot Gaiser, concludes that 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(b)(1) and (c)(1) “may not be enforced to impose criminal penalties on dealers” who sell handguns or handgun ammunition to otherwise law-abiding adults aged 18 to 20. The opinion PDF is hosted by the reporting outlet. Its reasoning: 18-to-20-year-olds are within “the people”; handguns are the arms most commonly chosen for self-defence; the right to keep arms entails the ability to acquire them; and Congress cannot evade the Amendment by criminalising the seller rather than the buyer. Private gifts are held an inadequate substitute, since not every young adult has a willing relative and several states restrict private transfers. The historical section is concrete: 249 of more than 250 colonial and early state militia laws required service of men aged 18 to 20, and the Militia Act of 1792 required enrollees to furnish their own arm. It rejects the common-law “infancy doctrine” analogue on the ground that a rule letting a minor void a contract is not a tradition of imprisoning a merchant — a dealer here faces licence loss and up to five years. The analysis tracks the Fifth Circuit’s Reese v. ATF. Now the limits, and they are the whole compliance story: this is an executive-branch legal opinion, not a statute, not an injunction, not a Supreme Court decision. The sections remain in the U.S. Code. The opinion does not state when or how ATF will change its instructions to dealers. It does not reach state under-21 purchase laws, of which it lists many. And the federal courts remain split — a federal judge recently upheld Hawaii’s under-21 acquisition ban on exactly the infancy-doctrine theory this opinion rejects. The outlet’s own advice is the right advice: dealers and young adults should wait for implementation guidance rather than become a test case. Single outlet plus the primary document.

RTB angleThe best content opportunity on the board and the easiest to get badly wrong, and the gap between those two is entirely about tense. The publishable piece is a process explainer: what an OLC opinion is, what it binds (the executive branch’s own enforcement posture) and what it does not (Congress, the states, the courts), why a government sometimes declines to defend its own statute, and what would have to happen next for the law on the books to change. Genuinely useful, genuinely uncommon, squarely in RTB’s lane. Say “the Justice Department concluded” and never “the ban has been struck down,” “is no longer in effect,” or “18-year-olds can now buy handguns.” Report the Hawaii ruling and the circuit split in the same piece — omitting them makes it advocacy rather than explanation. Tell no 18-to-20-year-old member that they may now buy a handgun from a dealer, and tell no dealer they may now sell one. State restrictions are unaffected and are where most members actually live. Do not name, praise or criticise the Assistant Attorney General, the President or any legislator. Do not merge this with the held, unverified Florida under-21 item.

New ▲ NFA · no appeal · two outlets party-scoped relief · no ATF guidance Heat87▲

The President ordered DOJ not to appeal the NFA ruling — and the injunction still protects members of specific organisations, not the public

The best-verified item on today’s board, and the end of a thread this board has carried since August. The President has personally directed the Justice Department not to appeal Silencer Shop Foundation v. ATF — reported by CBS News and confirmed to the originating outlet by congressional sources, with Gun Owners of America circulating the report. That resolves the internal DOJ division this board logged in August. The underlying ruling: Judge Wesley Hendrix (N.D. Tex.) held the NFA’s regulatory provisions unconstitutional as applied to suppressors, SBRs, SBSs and AOWs. The reasoning is narrow and worth quoting accurately — “Because today’s NFA does not generate any revenue from untaxed firearms, its regulatory provisions cannot be upheld under the taxing power… the regulatory provisions must be enjoined as unconstitutional because they exceed Congress’s enumerated powers” — following § 70436 of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act setting the making and transfer tax to $0 for those four categories. The court rejected the argument that licensees’ special occupational tax could substitute. Machine guns and destructive devices remain taxed at $200 and are wholly untouched. The qualifier that must lead every piece of copy: the relief is party-scoped, not nationwide. It reaches current and future GOA and FRAC members and customers of Silencer Shop, Palmetto State Armory, B&T and SilencerCo. ATF has issued no implementation guidance and is reported to be deliberately slow, to protect owners against a future reversal. The White House rebuttal to the “gangster weapons” framing is checkable and belongs in any balanced treatment: “Anyone who would have been prohibited from buying a firearm under the old structure is still prohibited… All federal criminal statutes imposing additional charges or penalties for the unlawful possession of a firearm remain in place.”

RTB angleThe honest and genuinely valuable piece is “who is actually covered by this injunction, and who is not” — because the answer is surprising, checkable, and almost certain to be mis-stated everywhere else. Most people reading a headline about the NFA will assume the rules changed for them, and for the overwhelming majority they did not. Write: a federal court enjoined enforcement as to specific plaintiffs, members and customers; the government has now chosen not to appeal; ATF has published no guidance; if you are not within a covered class nothing about your legal position has changed. Never tell a member they are a protected party under this or any injunction, and never suggest joining an organisation as a route to a legal result — that is compliance advice RTB is not positioned to give and the downside falls entirely on the member. Give no instructions on acquiring, making, transferring or modifying any NFA item. Do not write that the NFA has been repealed, gutted or ended. Do not predict ATF guidance or its timing. Quote the White House statement alongside the ruling. Do not name, praise or criticise the President or the judge. Do not amplify the reported dismissal of a charge against a defendant who joined covered organisations — it invites members to read an injunction as a shield against prosecution.

Promoted from the also-notable rail now that the opinion is readable and the docket PDF is linked. US District Judge Deborah Chasanow has granted a preliminary injunction in SSJC v. Montgomery County, barring the county from enforcing its firearm restrictions against the Silver Spring Jewish Center and Rabbi J. Menahse Shapiro — and expressly declining to block enforcement against anyone else. The two challenged provisions bar licensed owners from possessing a firearm within 100 yards of a place of worship, and within 100 yards of places of public assembly including schools, parks and libraries. The synagogue operates an armed volunteer security team, many former military, all state-licensed; it also hires one armed guard for part of the day who is statutorily exempt, and did not wish to rely on hired guards alone. The court found a likelihood of success on the as-applied Second Amendment claim and on the rabbi’s free-exercise claim, quoting a Ninth Circuit observation that “places of worship have been prevalent throughout our Nation’s history, but no colony, state, or territory banned firearms at places of worship until after the ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment,” and finding the county’s analogues neither closely matched, nor near the Founding Era, nor well-accepted. It also noted the county had not explained what distinguishes a synagogue’s licensed security team from the exempted licensed guards. Two further constitutional claims were left unresolved, nothing was held unconstitutional, and the judge cautioned that “further Supreme Court and circuit-court guidance may affect the ultimate resolution.” Counsel is the New Civil Liberties Alliance. The Maryland Attorney General’s office did not respond to the outlet. Related and separate: DOJ has its own pending suit against the same county’s wider gun-free-zone mandates.

RTB angleThere is a real and useful piece here, and it is about houses of worship as a category rather than about this synagogue. Security teams at churches, synagogues and mosques are a large, under-served and genuinely anxious audience, and the legal position is confusing almost everywhere. The publishable frame: what a preliminary injunction is, why this one protects exactly two named plaintiffs, and why a congregation twelve miles away is in precisely the same position it was in yesterday. Say “a court has preliminarily enjoined enforcement against one synagogue and its rabbi” and never “Maryland’s place-of-worship gun ban has been struck down.” Note the judge’s own caution — she flagged it, so RTB should. Tell no member in Maryland or anywhere else that they may now carry at a place of worship. Do not frame this in religious or communal-threat terms — using one congregation’s security posture to imply a general danger to worshippers would be fear-led, precisely the register RTB exists to avoid. Handle the free-exercise holding descriptively. If the team wants an evergreen, “questions to ask before your congregation forms a volunteer security team” is the one worth writing, pointing to local counsel rather than answering the legal question.

New ▲ DOJ Civil Rights · filed 09/16 third carry-fee suit · nothing enjoined Heat73▲

DOJ sued San Jose over $1,600 carry-permit fees — its third such suit, on a legal theory a judge has already questioned

On Wednesday the Civil Rights Division filed US v. San Jose, calling the city “a national outlier when it comes to burdening the exercise of fundamental rights” and arguing its fees “are abusive and violate the Second Amendment.” The complaint is published at justice.gov. The comparison table is the substance: San Jose charges roughly $1,591 in application and issuance fees, up about $300 in two years, against $144 in San Francisco, $255 plus $325 in Berkeley, $488 plus $488 in Santa Clara and $347 plus $346 in Alameda County — and among major cities, $75 in Washington DC, $340 for a three-year New York City licence, $150 in Chicago, $100 in Boston and $20 in Philadelphia. The complaint also challenges a requirement that applicants obtain and pay for psychological testing at whatever rate the “authorized psychologist” charges, with no way for an applicant to know whether the fee is limited to reasonable cost as state law requires. DOJ concedes at the outset that shall-issue permitting is lawful under Bruen, and rests instead on Bruen’s caution against “regimes where… exorbitant fees deny ordinary citizens their right to public carry.” The structural weakness is real and the reporting outlet leads with it: the suit hangs on § 12601(a) pattern-or-practice authority, the same novel theory Judge Monica Ramirez Almadani called “not obviously sound” in July when she denied DOJ a TRO in US v. California over that state’s handgun-roster law. If courts reject that authority, one of the department’s broadest Second Amendment initiatives loses its vehicle. Third carry-permitting suit — after Los Angeles County, which settled, and the US Virgin Islands — with Philadelphia and Minnesota openly threatened. Nothing is enjoined. San Jose’s fees are payable in full today.

RTB angleThe publishable piece is the cost-of-a-right explainer, and the fee table does the work without a single word of argument. “What a carry permit costs, city by city” is concrete, checkable, non-partisan, high-search and almost entirely unwritten — and it lands the point that the practical barrier to a lawful carry permit is often money and process rather than law. Use the complaint’s figures and attribute them to the complaint, not to the cities; they are DOJ’s numbers and have not been independently verified here. Say “the Justice Department has sued” and never “San Jose’s fees have been struck down” or “are being refunded.” Report the § 12601 weakness — a piece that omits it is cheerleading, and the outlet that broke it flagged it first. Tell no San Jose applicant to delay, withhold or dispute a fee; a member who defers an application on the strength of a filed complaint could lose a year. Do not predict settlement, and note the Los Angeles matter settled rather than being decided, so it set no precedent. Do not characterise the city, the police department or the psychologists.
